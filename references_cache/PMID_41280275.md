Abstract Bacterial diseases represent a major bottleneck in the sustainable development of aquaculture. The gut microbiota plays a vital role in host growth and health, including the enhancement of disease resistance. Although substantial progress has been made in elucidating the mechanisms of disease resistance in fish, the precise role of the gut microbiota in enhancing pathogen resistance in aquatic animals remains poorly understood. In this study, crucian carp (Carassius auratus) were used as a model to investigate the role of intestinal microbiota in modulating resistance to Aeromonas hydrophila. Individual crucian carp exhibited distinct clinical phenotypes following A. hydrophila infection. Specifically, significant differences were observed in the composition of the intestinal microbiota between fish displaying mild symptoms and those exhibiting severe phenotypic manifestations (α diversity: p < 0.01; β diversity: p = 0.001). Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments demonstrated that fish with mild symptoms conferred enhanced resistance to A. hydrophila when their intestinal contents were transplanted into other individuals (p = 0.006). Further microbial analysis identified Cetobacterium (p = 0.013), Paraclostridium (p < 0.01), and Pseudomonas (p < 0.01) as key differential taxa. A simplified microbial community comprising these three strains was subsequently constructed. Feeding experiments confirmed that administration of this community significantly improved host resistance to A. hydrophila (p < 0.05) by activating intestinal immune responses and reinforcing the gut barrier. Overall, our findings underscore the potential of the microbial community as a novel strategy for disease prevention and control in aquaculture, providing a theoretical foundation for the development of microbiome-based therapies in fish health management. Introduction Bacterial infectious diseases remain a major threat to human health and also pose a significant challenge to the sustainable development of the aquaculture industry. As aquaculture is expected to play a critical role in meeting the rising global demand for high-quality protein [ 1 , 2 ], disease outbreaks represent a serious obstacle to its growth. Infectious diseases result in an estimated annual economic loss of approximately 6 billion USD, with bacterial infections accounting for nearly half of all mortality in cultured aquatic animals [ 3 , 4 ]. Among these pathogens, motile aeromonads are opportunistic bacteria responsible for multiple fish diseases, including septicaemia, fin rot, haemorrhages, and dropsy [ 5 ]. Aeromonas hydrophila, in particular, is a highly virulent species isolated from diseased aquatic animals worldwide [ 6 ]. Upon infection, A. hydrophila initially colonizes the intestinal tract, proliferates rapidly, and subsequently spreads through the bloodstream to organs such as the kidney, liver, and spleen, leading to systemic symptoms. This pathogen affects a wide range of freshwater fish species, including crucian carp (Carassius auratus) and grass carp, and is also pathogenic to humans, where it can cause gastroenteritis characterized by abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea [ 7 , 8 ]. The crucian carp is one of the most important freshwater aquaculture species in China, contributing to national food security, optimization of agricultural structure, and the promotion of rural revitalization. However, A. hydrophila-induced disease represents the most economically damaging bacterial infection in crucian carp farming. Recent studies have demonstrated that dietary supplementation with plant-derived compounds and trace elements—such as polyphenols [ 9 ], Cornus mas (Cornelian cherry) fruit extract [ 10 ], and sodium [ 11 ]—can enhance host immunity and increase resistance to pathogenic infections. Furthermore, probiotic supplementation, particularly with Lactobacillus fermentum [ 12 ], has also been shown to effectively strengthen the host's defense against pathogens. Nevertheless, despite these advances, antibiotics remain the primary measure for disease prevention and control. With increasing attention to environmentally sustainable and health-conscious aquaculture practices, however, the use of antibiotics is facing growing regulatory restrictions and practical limitations. The intestine functions not only as the primary site for nutrient digestion and absorption but also as a vital immune organ in fish. Intestinal symbiotic microbes play a crucial role in maintaining intestinal function and integrity. These microbes can directly influence the structure and function of the intestinal barrier. On one hand, they inhibit pathogen colonization, thereby preventing mucosal damage; on the other hand, they promote barrier integrity by sustaining intestinal homeostasis [ 13 ]. Experimental studies in mice had demonstrated that a higher abundance of Erysipelotrichi in the gut is associated with reduced bacterial translocation, whereas increased levels of Proteobacteria and TM7 correlate with increased translocation, suggesting that specific microbial populations directly modulate intestinal barrier permeability [ 14 ]. Moreover, extensive evidence in mammals indicates that the gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in shaping innate immune responses. By regulating immune function and mucosal homeostasis, core microbial communities significantly influence susceptibility to pathogenic infections [ 15 , 16 ]. Notably, certain symbiotic microbes exhibit disproportionate functional importance within the microbial community, regulating colonization resistance against invading pathogens [ 17 ]. Therefore, targeted manipulation of these keystone species may offer a precise and effective strategy for preventing and controlling infectious diseases in aquaculture. Recent studies on defined microbial consortia have begun to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of microbiome-driven phenotypes, including immune regulation and polysaccharide metabolism [ [18] , [19] , [20] ]. However, despite these advances, studies focused on screening and assembling intestinal symbiotic microbes based on host phenotypes in aquatic animals remain limited. This knowledge gap underscores the need for further research to explore host-microbiota interactions in aquaculture systems and to guide the development of functionally tailored microbial therapeutics. Therefore, in this study, we investigated the differences in intestinal microbiota composition among crucian carp exhibiting distinct clinical symptoms following A. hydrophila infection. To further clarify the role of the gut microbiota in enhancing disease resistance, we conducted fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments, which confirmed the pivotal contribution of the intestinal microbiota to host defense. Through this approach, three core microbial taxa associated with enhanced resistance to pathogenic bacterial infection were identified. Based on these taxa, we constructed a simplified microbial community to evaluate their collective protective effects. Furthermore, integrated transcriptomic analyses were performed to elucidate the molecular pathways through which this community enhances crucian carp resistance to A. hydrophila. This work provides a theoretical foundation for developing synthetic microbial communities as targeted, microbiota-based therapeutic strategies in aquaculture. 2. Materials and methods 2.1. Fish Healthy crucian carp (Carassius auratus) with a body length of 8 ± 0.5 cm were obtained from Yongsheng Fishery Development Co., Ltd. (Guizhou, China). Prior to the experiment, the fish had not been exposed to antibiotics, probiotics, or prebiotics. They were acclimated to laboratory conditions for three weeks and fed commercial crucian carp feed (Tongwei, China) twice daily during this period. All procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Northwest A&F University (Approval No. 20240212). 2.2. Experimental design and sample collection Healthy crucian carp were randomly assigned to two groups: the control group (CON) and the infection group (TRE), with 150 fish per group and three biological replicates each. Fish in the infection group were orally administered Aeromonas hydrophila at a concentration of 1 × 105 CFU/mL, with each fish receiving 50 μL of the bacterial suspension. In contrast, fish in the control group received 50 μL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The infection dose (1 × 105 CFU/mL) was determined based on preliminary LD50 experiments in crucian carp, which ensured approximately 50–60 % mortality, thereby providing a clear differentiation between resistant and susceptible phenotypes. Subsequently, nine fish from each group were randomly sampled on days 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 to collect intestinal, kidney, and spleen tissues, which were immediately stored at −80 °C for further analysis. 2.3. Phenotype definition Infected crucian carp exhibited detectable Aeromonas loads in kidney and developed typical clinical symptoms, including hemorrhagic spots on the body surface, fin base congestion, and anal redness and swelling. To quantify disease severity, a scoring system was applied: a score of 1 indicated the presence of one clinical symptom, 2 indicated the presence of two symptoms, and 3 indicated the simultaneous presence of all three. In this phenotypic assessment, a mild clinical presentation was defined as a score of 1, whereas scores of 2 or 3 were considered indicative of severe symptoms [ 21 ]. 2.4. Fecal microbiota transplantation experiment The intestinal contents from crucian carp exhibiting mild clinical symptoms were selected as the donor microbiota. Following euthanasia in ice water, the body surface of each donor fish was disinfected with 70 % ethanol. The entire intestinal tract was then dissected, and its outer surface rinsed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Intestinal contents were gently extracted using sterile forceps and collected into a 10 mL centrifuge tube. Each sample was diluted with one-third its volume of sterile PBS, homogenized using a magnetic stirrer, and filtered three times to remove undigested particulates. The filtrate was then centrifuged at 5000×g for 5 min, and the pellet was resuspended in an equal volume of sterile PBS. Glycerol was added to the final suspension to achieve a final concentration of 10 % [ 22 ]. According to the established protocol, recipient fish were administered antibiotics to eliminate intestinal bacteria [ 23 , 24 ]. They were fed a diet containing vancomycin (2 g/kg feed), metronidazole (4 g/kg feed), and neomycin sulfate (4 g/kg feed) for seven consecutive days. Subsequently, donor bacteria were orally administered for two consecutive days. After anesthesia, each fish received 50 μL of donor microbiota suspension (1 × 108 CFU/mL) via oral gavage. Subsequently, an infection challenge was performed to evaluate disease resistance. Intestinal, kidney, and spleen samples were collected on the second day post-infection and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analyses. 2.5. Microbiome analysis Total genomic DNA was extracted from intestinal samples using the QIAamp PowerFecal DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer's instructions. The V3–V4 regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified using primers 341F and 806R. High-throughput sequencing was performed by LC-Bio Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) on the Illumina MiSeq platform. Sequencing and data analysis followed the procedures described in our previous study [ 25 ]. Alpha diversity metrics, including Shannon index and species richness, were calculated using R software (v4.4.3). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on weighted_unifrac and unweighted_unifrac distance was conducted using the vegan package in R. The linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) method was applied to identify differentially abundant microbial taxa at the genus level. Furthermore, functional profiling of the predicted intestinal microbiota was performed using PICRUSt2. 2.6. Quantification of pathogenic bacteria Total DNA from intestinal and kidney tissues was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the manufacturer's instructions. The amplified bacterial DNA fragments were cloned into the pMD19-T vector (Takara, Dalian, China), diluted tenfold, and used to generate a standard curve for quantifying bacterial load via quantitative PCR (qPCR). 2.7. Isolation and identification of differential microbes After euthanizing the crucian carp in ice water, the intestines were aseptically dissected and transferred into a sterile container. A volume of 200 μL sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added, and the tissue was homogenized. The resulting homogenate was serially diluted (10−3, 10−4, and 10−5) with 100 μL of sterile PBS and plated onto brain heart infusion (BHI) and Luria–Bertani (LB) agar media. Plates were incubated for 48 h under both anaerobic conditions at 28 °C and aerobic conditions. Subsequently, distinct colonies were isolated, streaked for purification, and then inoculated into 5 mL of BHI and LB broth for expansion. Bacterial identification was performed using universal primers targeting the 16S rRNA gene. 2.8. Construction of simplified microbial communities The isolated target strains were first activated on solid media. Individual colonies were then picked using sterile inoculating loops and transferred into liquid media, followed by overnight incubation in a shaker. When the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of the bacterial suspension reached 1.0, the cultures were centrifuged at 5000×g for 10 min at 4 °C to collect the cells. The bacterial pellets were resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the concentration was adjusted to 1 × 108 CFU/mL. Then, based on previous studies, equal volumes of the suspensions were thoroughly mixed to construct a synthetic microbial community [ 20 ]. 2.9. RNA extraction and qPCR analysis Total RNA was extracted from intestinal, kidney, and spleen tissues using TRIzol reagent (Accurate Biology, China), following the manufacturer's instructions. First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using PerfectStart Green qPCR SuperMix (2 × ; Vazyme) on the CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method [ 26 ]. The primer sequences used for qPCR are listed in Supplementary Table 1 . 2.10. Determination of biochemical indicators The intestinal tissue was homogenized and centrifuged to obtain supernatants for subsequent biochemical analyses. The activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), acid phosphatase (ACP), lysozyme (LSZ), and diamine oxidase (DAO) were measured using commercially available assay kits, following the manufacturer's instructions (Nanjing Jiancheng Institute of Bioengineering, China). 2.11. Transcriptome analysis Intestinal samples were sent to Lianchuan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) for RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis. RNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform. Raw reads were processed using fastp to remove low-quality and redundant sequences, thereby generating high-quality clean reads for downstream analysis. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with a significance threshold set at |log2 (fold change)| ≥ 1 and p < 0.05. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis was conducted, and multiple testing corrections were applied to differentially expressed genes (DEGs) to control the false discovery rate at p < 0.05. To validate the RNA-seq results, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed on eight selected genes using the same RNA samples. 2.12. Statistics and analysis Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 10). Differences between groups were evaluated using unpaired Student's t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. 3. Results 3.1. Aeromonas hydrophila infection induced differential clinical symptoms In this study, we investigated the immune response of crucian carp (Carassius auratus) using an Aeromonas oral infection model. Tissue samples were collected at 2, 4, 6, and 8 days post-infection (dpi) ( Fig. 1 A). Approximately 60 % of infected fish succumbed to the infection ( Fig. 1 B). Quantitative analysis revealed a marked increase in bacterial load in the kidney at 2 dpi (p < 0.05), followed by a gradual decline; a similar trend was observed in the intestine ( Fig. 1 C). Meanwhile, at 2 dpi, infected fish exhibited pronounced clinical symptoms, including skin ulceration, fin base hemorrhage, and anal inflammation characterized by redness and swelling ( Fig. 1 D). Based on the significant differences observed on the second day post-infection, diseased fish were classified into mild (TRE_M) and severe (TRE_S) groups according to the severity of their clinical symptoms. Expression analysis of inflammation-related genes (il-10, tgfβ, il-1β, tnfα) across immune-relevant tissues (spleen, kidney, and intestine) revealed significantly higher upregulation in the TRE_S group compared to the TRE_M group ( Supplementary Fig. S1 ). Histological examination using H&E staining showed epithelial cell detachment around the intestinal villi in the severe group, while the mild group displayed no notable structural abnormalities ( Fig. 1 E). Moreover, the expression of intestinal barrier-associated genes (zo-1, occludin, and claudin12) was markedly downregulated in the TRE_S group relative to both the TRE_M and CON groups ( Supplementary Fig. S2 ). Together, these results indicate that Aeromonas infection elicits a robust inflammatory response and compromises intestinal integrity in crucian carp. Crucian carp exhibiting distinct clinical symptoms harbor divergent intestinal microbial community structures To investigate differences in gut microbial composition associated with disease severity, we performed 16S rRNA gene sequencing on intestinal samples collected from control (CON), TRE_M, and TRE_S groups of crucian carp. Alpha diversity indices—including the Shannon index and observed OTUs—revealed significantly higher bacterial richness in the TRE_S group compared to the TRE_M group, while no significant difference was observed between the TRE_S and CON groups ( Fig. 2 A). β-diversity analysis revealed significant differences in gut microbiota composition among the three groups, with the centroids of each group clearly separated. ANOSIM based on unweighted UniFrac distances (R = 0.5614, p = 0.001) and weighted UniFrac distances (R = 0.7028, p = 0.001) confirmed these differences ( Fig. 2 B). At the genus level, microbial community composition differed significantly between the TRE_M and TRE_S groups ( Fig. 2 C). Compared to the TRE_S group, the TRE_M group showed a moderate enrichment of probiotic taxa, including significantly increased relative abundances of Cetobacterium, Pseudomonas, and Paraclostridium ( Fig. 2 D, Supplementary Fig. S3 ). Further insights were gained through molecular ecological network analysis of intestinal microbiota in the mild (TRE_M) and severe (TRE_S) groups. The TRE_M network exhibited a greater number of nodes, edges, and modular structures, indicating higher microbial interaction complexity. Notably, core taxa within the TRE_S group were dominated by potential pathogens, such as Aeromonas. In contrast, Cetobacterium, Pseudomonas, and Paraclostridium occupied central ecological positions in the TRE_M group ( Supplementary Fig. S4 ). Furthermore, Pearson correlation analysis between microbial taxa and host phenotypes in Aeromonas-infected crucian carp revealed that Cetobacterium, Pseudomonas, and Paraclostridium were significantly negatively correlated with infection severity, inflammation-related markers, and Aeromonas load in the intestine. Conversely, these taxa were positively associated with the expression of genes involved in maintaining the intestinal barrier ( Fig. 2 E). Fecal microbiota transplantation alleviates the clinical symptoms and inflammation caused by Aeromonas hydrophila infection To further assess whether intestinal microbiota can mitigate the clinical symptoms of Aeromonas infection in crucian carp, a fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiment was performed. Intestinal contents from donor fish in the TRE_M group were used for transplantation. Recipient fish were first treated with a broad-spectrum antibiotic cocktail for three consecutive days to deplete their native gut microbiota. Subsequently, on the first and third days following antibiotic treatment, the fish were orally administered intestinal contents from TRE_M donors. All recipients were then challenged with Aeromonas hydrophila ( Fig. 3 A). The results demonstrated that, on the second day after infection, crucian carp receiving FMT exhibited markedly milder clinical symptoms compared to untreated controls (TRE group), including fewer hemorrhagic spots and skin ulcers ( Fig. 3 B). Moreover, gene expression analyses revealed that transcript levels of key inflammation-related cytokines (il-10, tgfβ, il-1β, and tnfα) in the spleen, kidney, and intestine were significantly lower in the FMT group than in the TRE group ( Supplementary Fig. S5 ), suggesting that FMT mitigated the inflammatory response induced by A. hydrophila infection. Consistently, the bacterial load of Aeromonas in the kidney and intestine of the FMT group was significantly reduced relative to the TRE group ( Fig. 3 C). Histopathological examination via H&E staining revealed no apparent structural abnormalities in the intestine of FMT-treated fish ( Fig. 3 D). Furthermore, expression levels of intestinal barrier-related genes (zo-1, occludin, and claudin12) were significantly upregulated in the FMT group compared to the TRE group ( Supplementary Fig. S6 ). Finally, survival analysis showed that FMT significantly improved the survival rate of crucian carp following A. hydrophila infection (66.7 % vs. 36.7 %, Fig. 3 E). Collectively, these findings indicate that the intestinal microbiota plays a critical role in protecting crucian carp against A. hydrophila infection by enhancing epithelial barrier integrity and modulating the host immune response. Fecal microbiota transplantation accelerates restoration of intestinal microbiota Subsequently, we performed 16S rRNA gene sequencing to assess the microbial diversity of intestinal samples. The results revealed that the intestinal microbiota diversity in crucian carp following fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT group) was comparable to that of the control group (CON) and significantly higher than that of the TRE group ( Fig. 4 A). At the genus level, Aeromonas was dominant in the TRE group (84.7 %), while in the FMT group, Aeromonas (27.2 %) Cetobacterium (21.0 %), Deefgea (4.2 %), Flavobacterium (8.6 %), and Paraclostridium (3.4 %) were prominent constituents of the community ( Fig. 4 B). To further investigate taxonomic differences among groups, we conducted LEfSe (Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size) analysis. The results showed that Aeromonas was significantly enriched in the TRE group, whereas genera such as Cetobacterium, Flavobacterium, Deefgea, Paraclostridium, Pseudomonas, and Vibrio were enriched in the FMT group ( Fig. 4 C). Statistical validation using the Mann–Whitney U test confirmed that Cetobacterium, Flavobacterium, Deefgea, and Paraclostridium were significantly differentially abundant, while Pseudomonas also showed a significant difference between the FMT and TRE groups ( Fig. 4 D). To elucidate functional changes in the intestinal microbiota post-FMT, we employed PICRUSt2 for metagenomic prediction. Compared with the TRE group, microbial functions in the FMT group were enriched in pathways related to amino acid metabolism, replication and repair, and carbohydrate metabolism. Additionally, functions such as the metabolism of cofactors and vitamins were also enhanced ( Fig. 4 E), suggesting that FMT may improve microbe–microbe interactions and promote intestinal homeostasis by enhancing metabolic capacity. Consistent with these findings, phenotypic predictions indicated that the intestinal microbiota of FMT-treated fish harbored fewer Gram-negative and potentially pathogenic bacteria, and a greater proportion of anaerobes ( Fig. 4 F), favoring a more stable gut environment. Further analysis of the intestinal microbial ecological network revealed that fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) increased both the number of nodes and edges in the molecular ecological network of crucian carp, accompanied by a higher number of distinct modules. These findings suggest that FMT enhances microbial connectivity and community complexity ( Supplementary Fig. S7 ). In summary, fecal microbiota transplantation restructures and functionally reinforces the intestinal microbiota, promotes microbial interactions, supports intestinal homeostasis, and contributes to the maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity. Construction of simplified communities and determination of disease resistance To further identify intestinal microbiota that enhance resistance to Aeromonas infection in crucian carp, we conducted an intersection analysis of the differential taxa identified in two independent experiments. In both experiments, Aeromonas infection led to a reduction in the relative abundance of several genera, including Cetobacterium, Pseudomonas, Paraclostridium, Vibrio, Acidovorax, and Kinneretia, while significantly increasing the abundance of Aeromonas itself ( Supplementary Fig. S8 ). Among these, only Cetobacterium, Pseudomonas, and Paraclostridium showed statistically significant changes. We therefore hypothesize that these three bacterial taxa may play a key role in enhancing host resistance to Aeromonas infection. To validate this hypothesis, we isolated 212 bacterial strains from the intestines of crucian carp in the TRE_M and FMT groups using LB and BHI agar plates. Based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing, these isolates were assigned to seven genera. Notably, Cetobacterium somerae S3, Pseudomonas stutzeri J1, and Paraclostridium bifermentans M1 were identified as representative strains from the genera Cetobacterium, Pseudomonas, and Paraclostridium, respectively ( Supplementary Fig. S9 ). Then, we evaluated potential antagonism among the three strains using in vitro co-culture assays. Pairwise and triple-strain co-cultures were incubated for 78 h at inoculation ratios of 1:1 or 1:1:1, respectively, and no antagonistic or inhibitory effects were observed ( Supplementary Fig. S10 ). Subsequently, in preliminary feeding trials, crucian carp were administered diets containing either single strains, pairwise strain combinations, or all three strains. After 28 days of feeding, infection experiments were performed, revealing that fish receiving the three-strain combination exhibited the highest survival rate ( Supplementary Fig. S11 ). We then constructed a simplified microbial community by mixing equal volumes of suspensions of the three strains (∼108 CFU/L per strain), which was incorporated into the feed and administered to crucian carp for 28 consecutive days ( Fig. 5 A). By day 28, fish treated with the synthetic microbial community (syncom group) exhibited enhanced non-specific immunity. Specifically, the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) increased by 66.01 % and 24.82 %, respectively, compared to the control group (CON group). In addition, the enzymatic activities of acid phosphatase (ACP) and lysozyme (LSZ) in the intestine were significantly elevated (153.20 vs. 133.62 units/g prot; 24.41 vs. 17.74 μg/mg prot, respectively) ( Fig. 5 B). Moreover, the expression levels of genes related to the intestinal barrier—zo-1, occludin, and claudin12—were significantly upregulated in the synthetic microbiota-treated group (syncom group). Interestingly, diamine oxidase (DAO) activity, a marker of intestinal mucosal injury, was also significantly higher in syncom group than in the CON group ( Fig. 5 C), suggesting enhanced mucosal turnover or repair. Following challenge with Aeromonas, the synthetic microbiota-treated fish demonstrated improved resistance, as evidenced by a significantly higher survival rate (60 % vs. 32.5 %) ( Fig. 5 D). Furthermore, qPCR analysis revealed a markedly reduced Aeromonas load in the kidneys and intestines of the syncom group compared to CON group ( Fig. 5 E). Collectively, these results suggest that the simplified synthetic microbiota composed of C. somerae, P. stutzeri, and P. bifermentans confers enhanced intestinal immune function and barrier integrity, thereby increasing resistance to Aeromonas infection in crucian carp. Simplified microbial community reshaped the intestinal transcriptomic level To further investigate the mechanism by which the simplified microbial community enhances intestinal immunity in crucian carp, we performed transcriptome sequencing on intestinal tissues. The analysis revealed significant differences in gene expression profiles between the two groups ( Fig. 6 A). Compared with the control group (CON), treatment with the synthetic microbial community (SynCom) resulted in 637 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 353 upregulated and 284 downregulated transcripts ( Fig. 6 B). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these DEGs identified several significantly affected biological processes in the SynCom group, primarily related to immune functions. These included immune response, defense response to Gram-negative bacteria, and the positive regulation of cytokine production involved in immune responses ( Fig. 6 C). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were enriched in pathways associated with metabolism, immunity, and cell proliferation ( Fig. 6 D). Notably, immune-related pathways, such as cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, phagosome and the MAPK signaling pathway, were significantly enriched in the SynCom group. Moreover, pathways related to intestinal barrier function—including the intestinal immune network for IgA production and tight junctions—also exhibited greater gene enrichment in the SynCom group ( Fig. 6 E). To validate the transcriptomic findings, we performed quantitative real-time PCR to assess expression of key genes in the intestinal immune network for IgA production. SynCom treatment markedly elevated the intestinal expression of mhcII, ccr9, cxcr4, cxcl12, and itga3, which had the sample the same trend as the transcriptome data ( Fig. 6 F). These findings highlight the pivotal role of the synthetic microbiota in enhancing both intestinal immunity and barrier integrity in crucian carp. Discussion Given the detrimental impact of antibiotics on global ecosystems and food safety, there is an urgent need for effective alternatives in aquaculture [ 27 ]. Symbiotic bacteria that reside in the intestinal tract play essential roles in host immunity, digestion, and overall health. The microbial communities they form collectively contribute to maintaining host homeostasis. However, while synthetic microbial communities (SynComs) constructed from symbiotic bacteria have been extensively studied in plants, their assembly and functional roles in aquatic animals remain largely unexplored. In this study, we identified Cetobacterium, Paraclostridium, and Pseudomonas as core taxa contributing to the resistance of crucian carp against Aeromonas infection. Further experiments demonstrated that a simplified synthetic microbial community composed of these three strains significantly enhanced the host's resistance to Aeromonas hydrophila, primarily by promoting intestinal immune responses and strengthening gut barrier function. To the best of our knowledge, this study constructed a simplified disease-resistant synthetic microbial community using symbiotic bacteria derived from the fish gut. These findings highlight the potential of synthetic microbiota as a novel strategy for disease prevention and control in aquaculture, and underscore their importance in supporting intestinal health in farmed fish. Moreover, this work lays the foundation for the development of personalized microbial therapies targeting fish diseases. Phenotypic characterization is essential for the detection and evaluation of disease severity. In this study, crucian carp were classified into mild and severe infection groups based on the intensity of clinical symptoms following A. hydrophila infection, including skin ulceration, localized hemorrhaging, anal redness and swelling, and bleeding at the base of the fin rays. This classification facilitated a more accurate assessment of disease progression and host tolerance to pathogenic challenge. Innate immunity serves as a primary defense mechanism in fish against bacterial infections [ 28 ]. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed that, compared with mildly affected fish, severely infected individuals exhibited elevated expression of pro-inflammatory genes (il-1β, tnfα, tgfβ, il-10), alongside a significant downregulation of genes associated with intestinal barrier function. This was accompanied by marked damage to intestinal tissue architecture. Compromise of the intestinal barrier is known to promote bacterial translocation [ 25 ], which aligns with our findings that visceral tissues in severely affected fish carried a higher pathogen burden. Excessive expression of inflammatory mediators can lead to cellular injury, tissue damage, and ultimately, phenotypic deterioration [ 29 ]. Consistent with this, our data demonstrated a positive correlation between elevated inflammatory gene expression and the extent of intestinal tissue damage. These results were also in agreement with observations in mammalian models, where individuals with severe clinical manifestations typically exhibit higher bacterial loads and increased expression of inflammatory genes [ 30 , 31 ]. Furthermore, pathogenic infection can disrupt the equilibrium of the intestinal microbial community, leading to a dysbiosis characterized by an imbalance between beneficial and opportunistic microbes, thereby compromising host health [ 25 ]. In the present study, crucian carp with differing phenotypic severity exhibited distinct intestinal microbiota profiles. The relative abundances of Cetobacterium, Paraclostridium, and Pseudomonas were significantly higher in mildly affected individuals than in severely infected fish. Correlation analyses indicated that these taxa were negatively associated with inflammatory markers and phenotypic severity, but positively associated with intestinal barrier-related gene expression. These findings suggested that Cetobacterium, Paraclostridium, and Pseudomonas may constitute core microbiota involved in mitigating disease symptoms and maintaining intestinal homeostasis during Aeromonas infection in crucian carp. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has emerged as a promising approach for treating diseases associated with intestinal dysbiosis, including Clostridium difficile infection and obesity [ 32 , 33 ]. Although the precise mechanisms underlying FMT are not fully elucidated, current evidence suggests that multiple pathways may be involved. One proposed mechanism is the supplementation of lost microbial functions through the introduction of beneficial donor species or the modulation of the host's existing microbiota. Zhang et al. [ 34 ] demonstrated that FMT enhances growth performance, antioxidant enzyme activity, and intestinal health in juvenile large yellow croakers. Similarly, in the present study, crucian carp that received intestinal microbiota from mildly symptomatic donors exhibited milder symptoms and higher survival rates following Aeromonas infection, compared to those that did not undergo FMT. Notably, FMT also restored the diversity and composition of the intestinal microbiota, aligning with findings in carp [ 22 ]. Interestingly, we observed a significant reduction in Gram-negative and potentially pathogenic bacteria in the FMT-treated group, accompanied by an increased abundance of anaerobic bacteria. This shift suggested that the intestinal environment became more anaerobic, which is favorable for the growth and colonization of beneficial microbes. Consistent with these findings, FMT was associated with an increased relative abundance of potential probiotics, including Cetobacterium and Paraclostridium, and altered the functional capacity of the gut microbiota. Specifically, functions related to the metabolism of cofactors and vitamins, amino acid metabolism, and DNA replication and repair were enhanced—processes critical for energy replenishment and the maintenance of intestinal epithelial integrity [ 35 , 36 ]. Furthermore, prior studies had shown that FMT promotes the proliferation of beneficial bacteria such as lactic acid bacteria and bifidobacteria, thereby restoring microbial diversity and improving host immunity. These probiotic communities contribute to gut health by strengthening the intestinal barrier, suppressing pathogenic bacterial growth, and modulating immune responses [ 37 ]. Analysis of microbial composition across different treatment groups revealed that FMT significantly increased the relative abundance of Cetobacterium, Paraclostridium, Flavobacterium, Deefgea, and Pseudomonas in the intestines of crucian carp. Notably, intersection analysis of differential taxa identified Cetobacterium, Paraclostridium, and Pseudomonas as consistently enriched across experiments. Cetobacterium, a well-known probiotic in freshwater fish, was recognized for its role in enhancing pathogen resistance [ 38 ]. Likewise, Paraclostridium was a candidate probiotic in aquatic species, with studies showing that its dietary supplementation improves growth and immunity in Pacific white shrimp [ 39 ]. Pseudomonas has also been demonstrated to confer resistance to Aeromonas infection in grass carp [ 40 ]. Collectively, these findings suggested that these three bacterial genera may play pivotal roles in enhancing crucian carp resistance to Aeromonas infection. The diversity of the host intestinal microbiota is considerably greater than that captured by 16S rRNA high-throughput sequencing. However, it remains unclear which microbial taxa within the gut contribute to specific functional roles. Early studies reported that synthetic microbial communities could comprise anywhere from a few to dozens of bacterial strains [ 20 , 41 ]. It is also uncertain whether commonly identified differential microbes participate in resistance against Aeromonas hydrophila infection in crucian carp. Therefore, constructing synthetic microbial communities offers a promising strategy to address this question. In the present study, we identified three symbiotic bacteria—Cetobacterium, Paraclostridium, and Pseudomonas—that were enriched in the intestines of carp exhibiting only mild clinical symptoms. Based on established protocols [ 20 ], we assembled a minimal synthetic microbial community comprising these three strains. Notably, this consortium significantly enhanced the resistance of crucian carp to A. hydrophila, indicating its potential as a key microbial formulation for disease prevention and treatment. Synthetic microbial communities had demonstrated the capacity to fulfill targeted objectives, including environmental remediation, disease control, immune modulation, and protection against pathogenic infections in plants, humans, and animals. Although these communities have been extensively studied in plant disease management [ 42 , 43 ], relatively limited research has explored their application in mammals and, more notably, in aquatic animals. For example, Cheng et al. developed a synthetic microbial community of 119 bacterial strains that conferred protection against intestinal infection by pathogenic Escherichia coli [ 20 ]. Consistent with this, our study provided evidence that a simplified community comprising Cetobacterium, Paraclostridium, and Pseudomonas could protect crucian carp from Aeromonas infection. To elucidate the underlying mechanism, we performed transcriptome analysis on the intestinal tissues of fish treated with the synthetic microbial community. The results revealed significant upregulation of immune-related pathways, particularly the intestinal immune network for IgA production. IgA, a non-inflammatory antibody, plays a critical role in mucosal immunity and contributes to the maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity [ 44 , 45 ]. Additionally, the activation of tight junction signaling pathways was observed, which supports barrier preservation and limits the translocation of opportunistic pathogens [ 46 ]. These findings were further validated by immunological assays showing enhanced non-specific immunity and elevated expression of proteins associated with barrier function. In addition, although our in vitro co-culture assays demonstrated the absence of antagonistic effects among the three bacterial strains, the specific interaction mechanisms operating within the host intestine remain to be elucidated. It is conceivable that these strains act synergistically through metabolic complementarity, nutrient cross-feeding, and interspecies signaling within the gut microenvironment. For instance, Cetobacterium somerae was a key commensal in freshwater fish that synthesizes vitamin B12 and other cofactors critical for both host metabolism and microbial community stability [ 25 ]. The vitamin B12 produced by C. somerae may facilitate amino acid and nucleotide biosynthesis in cohabiting taxa, thereby enhancing the metabolic potential of Pseudomonas stutzeri and Paraclostridium bifermentans. Pseudomonas stutzeri was known for its versatile nitrogen and carbon metabolic pathways and strong capacity for redox homeostasis, which could provide metabolic intermediates and signaling molecules that sustain the growth of obligate anaerobes [ 47 ]. Such metabolic flexibility allows Pseudomonas to occupy a keystone role, bridging aerobic and anaerobic processes within the gut ecosystem [ 48 ]. Meanwhile, Paraclostridium bifermentans was capable of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production and modulation of gut metabolic environments, which not only strengthens epithelial integrity but also suppresses pathogen colonization through competitive nutrient utilization [ 39 , 49 ]. Collectively, these results suggested that this synthetic microbial community confers protection against pathogenic bacterial infections by stimulating intestinal immunity and reinforcing epithelial barrier defenses. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms through which the synthetic microbial community modulates immune and barrier functions remain to be elucidated. Future investigations employing metabolomics and single-cell transcriptomics will be essential to uncover these mechanisms, ultimately guiding the rational design of synthetic microbial communities with defined functional capabilities. 5. Conclusion In summary, this study revealed significant differences in the composition of the intestinal microbiota among crucian carp exhibiting distinct phenotypic symptoms following Aeromonas infection. Subsequent fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) effectively restored the microbial community structure and enhanced the host's resistance to Aeromonas hydrophila. Further analysis identified Cetobacterium, Paraclostridium, and Pseudomonas as the core taxa contributing to resistance against A. hydrophila infection. Based on these findings, a simplified microbial community comprising these three strains was constructed. Feeding trials demonstrated that this consortium effectively mitigated pathogenic bacterial infections by activating intestinal immune responses and strengthening epithelial barrier function. These results underscore the potential of synthetic microbial communities for disease control in aquaculture. Moreover, they highlight the critical role of symbiotic gut bacteria in modulating gut immunity and maintaining microbial homeostasis, and laying a foundation for the development of targeted microbiome-based therapies for fish diseases.
