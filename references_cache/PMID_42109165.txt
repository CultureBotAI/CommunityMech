Specific human gut bacteria can degrade and utilise mucin glycans from the 
intestinal mucus layer. Consequently, these available glycans affect the gut 
microbiota and vice versa. Therefore, we studied the influence of mucin type on 
mucosal microbiota composition and mucin glycan degradation using an in vitro 
synthetic community consisting of mucin glycan degraders and cross-feeders 
commonly residing in the human colonic mucus layer. This community was incubated 
with different mucin substrates resulting in unique microbial community 
compositions, glycan degradation patterns, and metabolite production profiles 
for each condition. Porcine gastric mucin O-glycans were completely degraded by 
a diverse community with high relative abundance of Akkermansia muciniphila, 
Ruminococcus gnavus, Ruminococcus torques, and Faecalibacterium duncaniae. 
Presence of porcine gastric mucin and bovine submaxillary glands mucin resulted 
in A. muciniphila dominating the community. Availability of sialylated 
structures resulted in an increased relative abundance of A. muciniphila and 
Bacteroides fragilis, but Ruminococcus spp. decreased in relative abundance. 
This indicates that available sialic acid considerably influences community 
composition and function. Furthermore, glycan degradation and metabolite 
production were also affected by mucin type. This novel approach clearly 
demonstrated the interaction between mucin type and the microbiota composition 
and shows new insights in how these affect each other.

ABSTRACT Specific human gut bacteria can degrade and utilise mucin glycans from the intestinal mucus layer. Consequently, these available glycans affect the gut microbiota and vice versa. Therefore, we studied the influence of mucin type on mucosal microbiota composition and mucin glycan degradation using an in vitro synthetic community consisting of mucin glycan degraders and cross‐feeders commonly residing in the human colonic mucus layer. This community was incubated with different mucin substrates resulting in unique microbial community compositions, glycan degradation patterns, and metabolite production profiles for each condition. Porcine gastric mucin O‐glycans were completely degraded by a diverse community with high relative abundance of 
Akkermansia muciniphila, Ruminococcus gnavus, Ruminococcus torques, and Faecalibacterium duncaniae. Presence of porcine gastric mucin and bovine submaxillary glands mucin resulted in 
A. muciniphila
 dominating the community. Availability of sialylated structures resulted in an increased relative abundance of 
A. muciniphila
 and 
Bacteroides fragilis
, but Ruminococcus spp. decreased in relative abundance. This indicates that available sialic acid considerably influences community composition and function. Furthermore, glycan degradation and metabolite production were also affected by mucin type. This novel approach clearly demonstrated the interaction between mucin type and the microbiota composition and shows new insights in how these affect each other. The human gut outer mucus layer hosts specific microbes. Using a synthetic mucin glycan‐degrading microbial community to mimic this mucosal microbiome, we show that mucin type affects microbial community composition, glycan degradation and metabolite production.

The inner mucus layer is not colonised by microbes (Johansson et al.  2008 ), whereas the outer mucus layer is inhabited by a specific microbial community that is distinct from the luminal microbiota (Eckburg et al.  2005 ). The colonic mucus layer consists of mucins, which are heavily O‐glycosylated proteins that are secreted by goblet cells to form an intricate polymeric network (Hansson  2020 ). This network is formed through intermolecular disulfide bridging between mucin monomers (Pelaseyed et al.  2014 ). O‐linked glycosylation takes place in the Golgi apparatus by the action of various glycosyltransferases (Larsson et al.  2011 ). N‐acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc) is transferred to a serine or threonine residue on the protein backbone, followed by elongation with N‐acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc), galactose (Gal), fucose (Fuc), sialic acid (Sia), and/or sulphate (Tailford et al.  2015 ). A subset of the mucosal microbiota can degrade mucin glycans and utilise them for their growth (Glover et al.  2022 ). These mucin glycan‐degrading bacteria include 
Akkermansia muciniphila
 (Derrien et al.  2004 ), 
Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron
 (Martens et al.  2008 ), 
Ruminococcus gnavus
 (Crost et al.  2013 ), and 
Ruminococcus torques
 (Schaus et al.  2024 ). The niche of such microbes that live in close contact with the host makes them an important, but also difficult to study subject. Much of the current knowledge on the human gut microbiota originates from stool samples, in which mucosal microbes are underrepresented (Juge  2022 ). Therefore, we applied an in vitro synthetic microbial community to study mucin glycan degradation and microbial ecological interactions driven by distinct mucin types. To simulate differences in mucin glycan availability, two different commercially available mucin types were used as model systems, namely porcine gastric mucin (PGM) and bovine submaxillary glands mucin (BSM). PGM mostly comprises of MUC5AC, MUC5B and MUC6, and BSM of MUC5B and MUC19 (Giedraitis et al.  2024 ; Hoorens et al.  2011 ; Rulff et al.  2024 ). Both mucin types have a unique repertoire of O‐glycans. PGM consists mostly of neutral core 1 (Galβ1‐3GalNAcαSer/Thr) and core 2 (GlcNAcβ1‐6(Galβ1‐3)GalNAcαSer/Thr) O‐glycan structures. These structures mostly terminate with α1‐2 linked fucose, β1‐4 linked Gal, or β1‐3 linked GlcNAc (Jin et al.  2019 ; Karlsson et al.  1997 ; Quintana‐Hayashi et al.  2018 ), but some also end with blood group A (GalNAcα1‐3(Fucα1‐2)Galβ1‐3GlcNAc) or α2‐3 or α2‐6 linked sialic acid (Bechtella et al.  2024 ; Safferthal et al.  2024 ). Due to extensive processing, PGM has a low abundance of sialylated and sulphated O‐glycans (Jin et al.  2017 , 2019 ; Karlsson et al.  1997 ; Quintana‐Hayashi et al.  2018 ). In contrast to PGM, BSM is less fucosylated but more sulphated and heavily sialylated (J. Kim et al.  2020 ; Vos, Weber, et al.  2023b ). BSM mostly consist of core 3 and core 5 O‐glycans and the O‐glycans are extensively sialylated with α2‐3 and α2‐6 linked Sia (Kim et al.  2020 ). The most abundant O‐glycan structures consist of two or three monomeric units (Vos, Hooijschuur, et al.  2023a ). The availability of specific mucin glycans influences the gut bacterial composition as gut bacteria produce different enzymes which influences the cleavage of glycan linkages and the utilisation of monosaccharides (Bell et al.  2023 ; Bell and Juge  2021 ; Belzer  2022 ). It has been shown that mucin sialylation (Yao et al.  2022 ), mucin fucosylation (Kashyap et al.  2013 ), and a deficiency of core 1 mucin O‐glycans (Sommer et al.  2014 ) affected the gut microbiota composition in mice. Furthermore, there is an interplay between altered mucin glycosylation and gut microbiota in inflammatory bowel diseases (Kudelka et al.  2020 ; Larsson et al.  2011 ; Png et al.  2010 ; Zhang et al.  2021 ). To assess the influence of a changing glycan environment on microbial community composition and on mucin glycan degradation by the mucosal microbiota, we grew a synthetic mucin‐degrading community in vitro with PGM, BSM, a combination of PGM and BSM, and PGM supplemented with sialylated glycans. The synthetic community was based on previous work (Berkhout et al.  2024 ) and included mucin glycan degraders, secondary degraders, and hydrogen consumers. This community produced a rich repertoire of mucin‐targeting enzymes, downstream metabolic pathways, and cross‐feeding interactions. In this study, we assessed the microbial community composition, the degradation of mucin glycans, and the production of metabolites during growth over time. The effect of mucin type variation in PGM and BSM on glycan degradation patterns, community composition, and metabolite profile by the synthetic mucin‐degrading community was studied. 2 Experimental Procedures 2.1 Strains and Growth Medium The mucin glycan‐degrading synthetic community (MDSC) used in this study consisted of the strains

The inoculum was prepared by combining precultures in a 1:1 ratio based on OD600 and bottles were inoculated at 5% (v/v). The experiment was carried out in triplicate and consisted of four conditions and a control. The conditions were PGM, BSM, PGM + BSM, and PGM + SG. A mixture of PGM and BSM without bacteria was used as negative control. Serum bottles were incubated without shaking at 37°C for 24 h and 1% v/v was then transferred to fresh medium. In total, the community was transferred four times to adapt to the carbon source and reach a stable composition. Samples were taken at t = 0 h of the initial bottle, and at every following batch endpoint (t = 24 h, end of first transfer; t = 48 h, end of second transfer; t = 72 h, end of third transfer; t = 96 h, end of fourth transfer; and t = 120 h, end of fifth transfer). Plots were generated with ggplot2 (Wickham  2016 ) and minor adaptations to improve visualisation were made in Adobe Illustrator. 2.4 Release of O‐Glycans Attached to the Protein Backbone Using Reductive β‐Elimination and Clean‐Up by Solid Phase Extraction 
O‐glycans still attached to the protein backbone were released by reductive β‐elimination and clean‐up was performed by solid phase extraction (SPE) as described previously (de Ram et al.  2024 ). In short, 750 μL of 1 M sodium borohydride (NaBH4, caplets 98%, Sigma Aldrich) in 0.05 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥ 98% pellets, Sigma Aldrich) was added to 250 μL of each autoclaved bacterial supernatant sample (three replicates). Samples were homogenised, 1 μL of 1 mg/mL 1,4‐α‐d‐maltopentaose (MP5, 99% degree of polymerisation (DP) 5, Sigma Aldrich) was added as internal standard, and samples were incubated overnight (18–22 h) at 40°C at 300 rpm in a Thermomixer. Then, samples were acidified with 30 μL of glacial acetic acid (ULC/MS‐CC/SFC grade (≥ 99%), Biosolve Dieuze, France), and clean‐up was performed using C18 and PGC SPE (Sep‐Pak Vac 6cc C18 reversed phase (RP) SPE cartridges 500 mg 6 mL Waters Eschborn, Germany, and Supelco Supelclean Envi‐Carb porous graphitised carbon (PGC) SPE cartridges 250 mg 3 mL, Sigma Aldrich) after which samples were dried in a Savant centrifugal evaporator (Thermo Scientific) and washed 3× with methanol (MeOH, ULC/MS‐CC/SFC grade (≥ 99%, Biosolve)). As method control, crude PGM (mucin type III from porcine stomach partially purified powder, Sigma Aldrich) was used. Reproducibility as well as minimal variation (SD < 3.0%) of the method was shown previously and is applicable for current experiments as well (de Ram et al.  2024 ). 2.5 Release of N‐Glycans Attached to the Protein Backbone Using Enzymatic Treatment and Clean‐Up by Solid Phase Extraction 
N‐glycans still released to the protein backbone were released by enzymatic treatment and clean‐up by SPE was performed as described previously (de Ram et al.  2024 ). In short, 350 μL of 200 mM ammonium bicarbonate (NH4HCO3, BioUltra ≥ 98, Sigma Aldrich) and 65 μL of 2% sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS, ≥ 97%, Sigma Aldrich) were added to 250 μL of each autoclaved bacterial supernatant sample (one replicate). Samples were homogenised, 1 μL of 1 mg/mL MP5 was added as internal standard, and proteins were denatured for 10 min at 65°C. Then, 100 μL 200 mM NH4HCO3, 100 μL 4% IGEPAL (CA‐630 molecular biology grade, Sigma Aldrich), and 15 μL PNGase F enzyme (100 U/mL, from 
Elizabethkingia meningoseptica
 proteomics grade, Sigma Aldrich) were added, and samples were incubated at 37°C overnight (18–22 h). Thereafter, ethanol (EtOH, 100%) was added to yield a 80% EtOH solution. The solutions were stored at −20°C for 4 h, then centrifuged at 14.000×g for 30 min, and the supernatant was purified by PGC SPE as described previously (de Ram et al.  2024 ). 2.6 Analysis of Released O‐Glycans by PGC‐LC–MS/MS Analysis To study O‐glycan degradation by the bacteria over time, released O‐glycans were analysed by porous graphitised carbon liquid chromatography coupled to mass spectrometry (PGC‐LC–MS/MS) as described previously (de Ram et al.  2024 ). In short, samples were transferred to LC–MS vials (BGB 0.2 mL PP short thread vials 32 × 11.6 mm and BGB ND9 short thread screw caps with slitted septa silicone/PTFE, Thermo Scientific San Jose, USA) and were injected onto a Vanquish ultra‐high pressure liquid chromatography system (Thermo Scientific) equipped with a PGC Hypercarb guard column (10 × 2.1 mm, particle size 3 μM, Thermo Scientific) and a PGC Hypercarb analytical column (150 × 2.1 mm, particle size 3 μM, Thermo Scientific). The mobile phases used were 10 mM NH4HCO3 in water (ULC/MS‐CC/SFC grade (≥ 99%), Biosolve) (A) and 10 mM NH4HCO3 in acetonitrile (ACN, ULC/MS‐CC/SFC grade (≥ 99%), Biosolve):water 40:60 (B), and O‐glycans were eluted with a flow rate of 200 μL/min using a gradient from 2% B to 60% B in 40 min after which the column was equilibrated for 10 min using starting conditions (2% B). The set‐up was coupled via a HESI source to a Velos Pro ion trap MS (Thermo Scientific). Data acquisition was performed in negative ion mode (full MS and MS/MS data dependent). 2.7 Analysis of Released N‐Glycans by MALDI‐TOF MS Analysis To follow the N‐glycan degradation by the bacteria over time, the released N‐glycans were analysed by matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionisation time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI‐TOF MS analysis) as described previously (de Ram et al.  2024 ). In short, 1 μL of 2,5‐dihydroxybenzoic acid matrix (DHB, ≥ 99.5%, Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany, 20 mg/mL in 50:50 ACN:water +0.1% trifluoroacetic acid) (TFA, ULC/MS‐CC/SFC grade (≥ 99%), Biosolve) was spotted manually on a MALDI MTP target plate (Bruker Daltonics) followed by addition of 1 μL of sample. The spots were dried using a hairdryer. MALDI‐TOF MS measurements were performed in positive ion mode on an Autoflex MaX instrument (Bruker Daltonics) calibrated with maltodextrin (DP 1–20, potato starch (≥ 99%), Avebe Veendam, The Netherlands). 2.8 Glycan Representation, MS Data Analysis, and Construction of the Heatmaps GlycoWorkBench v1.1 was used for glycan representation and visualisation. The glycan structures were represented according to the Symbol Nomenclature for Glycans (Varki et al.  2015 ). Representative glycan structures were exported from GlycoWorkBench, adjusted in PowerPoint, and used in the figures for visualisation and clarity of the structures identified in MS data. O‐glycan structures were assigned based on careful review of the full MS and fragmentation (MS/MS) data, review of literature regarding mucin O‐glycans (Bechtella et al.  2024 ; Hayes et al.  2011 ; Jin et al.  2017 , 2019 ; J. Kim et al.  2020 ; Windwarder and Altmann  2014 ), and using the MS/MS search tool from UniCarb‐DB (Campbell et al.  2014 ). N‐glycan structures were assigned based on careful review of the MALDI‐TOF mass spectra and review of literature regarding mucin N‐glycans (Gallego et al.  2023 ; Van Der Post et al.  2014 ). Glycan MS data analysis was performed in a stepwise manner. First, a qualitative analysis was performed to compare the type of glycan identified by MS among the different samples. Second, a semi‐quantitative approach was used to compare the type of glycans present in each sample. For this, the glycan ratios and intensities of the identified glycans were used. The glycan ratios were calculated based on the total peak area (O‐glycans) or peak intensity (N‐glycans) of identified glycans and compared between the samples. The O‐glycan intensities were based on peak areas after manual peak integration using Xcalibur Qual Browser and Xcalibur Quan Browser software v4.5 (Thermo Scientific). The N‐glycan intensities were based on peak height after manual peak integration using flexAnalysis v1.4 (Bruker). Lastly, the peak area or peak intensity of added internal standard maltopentaose DP5 (MP5) was reviewed for each sample and consequently compared with the peak area or intensity of the other identified glycan peaks. This was used for reproducibility and semi‐quantitative purposes. The constructed heatmaps were made with GraphPad Prism v9.3.1. For each sample, the intensity at 0 h was set to 100% and the intensities (O‐glycans based on peak area and N‐glycans based on peak intensity) at timepoints 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h (only 120 h for N‐glycans) were set relative to this 100%. Glycan degradation was then visualised by comparing the increase/accumulation or decrease at the timepoints relative to 0 h. O‐glycan degradation of three replicates was visualised by using the average of the replicates. 2.9 Analysis of Produced Metabolites Using HPLC
 Metabolite production by the synthetic bacterial community was assessed for the different conditions with high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Samples were pre‐treated with the Carrez method as described before (Berkhout et al.  2024 ) and diluted 1:1 with 0.1 M H2SO4. Metabolites were measured on a Shimadzu LC2030C HPLC (Kyoto, Japan) with a HI‐PLEX H column (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The flow rate was 1 mL/min of 0.01 N H2SO4 and the temperature was set to 45°C. Standards used for quantification included lucose, succinate, lactate, formate, acetate, propionate, butyrate, 1,2‐propanediol and ethanol. Data were analysed with Chromeleon (ThermoFisher Scientific). To assess statistically significant differences in metabolite concentration between conditions, the wilcox_test function from ggpubr was used (Kassambara  2023 ) and adjusted p‐values (p.adj) were calculated by applying a Benjamini‐Hochberg correction. 3 Results 3.1 Mucin Type Significantly Affects Synthetic Community Composition To assess the effect of mucin glycan type on microbial community composition and mucin glycan degradation, a mucin glycan‐degrading synthetic community (MDSC) consisting of 14 bacterial species was grown in sequential batch in four distinct conditions: porcine gastric mucin (PGM), bovine submaxillary glands mucin (BSM), a mixture of PGM and BSM, and PGM with additional sialylated glycans (SG: 3′‐sialylgalacto‐N‐biose and 6′‐α‐sialyl‐N‐acetyllactosamine ratio 1:1). MDSC consisted of primary mucin degraders and cross‐feeders that scavenge released glycans, metabolites and/or gases (Berkhout et al.  2024 ). The sequential batch fermentation consisted of 24 h incubation of the community with the substrate, after which 1% v/v was transferred to fresh medium to adapt the community to the different conditions and obtain a stable community composition. The synthetic community showed comparable growth between conditions PGM, PGM + BSM, and PGM + SG as measured by cell count (qPCR) (Figure  S1A ) and ΔOD600 (Figure  S1B ). In contrast, MDSC growth on BSM was limited, as the cell count was approximately 105 times lower compared to the other conditions and no increase in OD600 was observed (Figure  S1B ). After four transfers, the composition of the community differed as a result of mucin type condition. The species‐level composition of the four conditions during the experiment is shown in Figure  S2 and Table  S3 . Replicates did not significantly differ from each other according to PERMANOVA (F = 0.33, p value = 0.98, Table  S4 ), whereas time did significantly affect the community composition (F = 8.11, p value = 0.0001, Table  S5 ). Therefore, differences in community composition were assessed at 96 and 120 h, after the community had stabilised. Figure  1 shows the relative abundances of the community in three different mucin conditions at t = 96 and 120 h. The highest concentration of metabolites was produced during incubation with PGM + SG, which contained additional sialylated glycans compared to PGM. Compounds that only occur at t = 0 h or at a concentration < 0.5 mM are not shown. 4 Discussion The influence of a changing glycan environment on human gut microbes was studied by incubating a mucin glycan‐degrading synthetic bacterial community in vitro with four distinct mucin substrates: PGM, BSM, PGM + BSM, and PGM + SG. Each condition induced a unique microbial community composition which was also reflected in the degradation of the O‐glycans and the production of metabolites. These results confirm the impact of different glycan structures on the microbiota composition in vitro and stress the dynamics of mucin glycan‐microbe interactions in the gut (Belzer  2022 ; Fekete and Buret  2023 ; Xia et al.  2022 ). This study is limited to in vitro experiments, the use of non‐human mucin substrates, and a selection of bacterial species often present in the human gut mucus layer. Therefore, the results cannot be extrapolated directly to the situation in the human gut, but, they do give insight in the influence of mucin glycan structures on the microbiota composition. The impact of different mucin glycans on bacterial community composition and glycan degradation has not been demonstrated in this way before. A diverse mucin glycan landscape was created by including both PGM and BSM, which are physically and structurally different (Fogg et al.  1996 ; Stie et al.  2024 ). The combination of PGM + BSM, which has not been used before, contained a more diverse mixture of O‐glycans compared to solely PGM or BSM and this indeed influenced the bacterial community and O‐glycan degradation (Figure  1 and Figure  7 ). Incubation of the MDSC with PGM + BSM resulted in a less diverse community compared to PGM. Incubation with PGM + BSM resulted in dominance of 
A. muciniphila
 (relative abundance > 0.85 at 96 and 120 h), whereas in PGM the relative abundance of 
A. muciniphila
 was < 0.5. This was also reflected by the increased propionate production on PGM + BSM, which could be the result of fucose degradation by 
A. muciniphila
 (Duan et al.  2023 ). PGM as sole substrate sustained a more diverse community, with a significantly higher relative abundance of mucin glycan degraders 
R. gnavus
 and 
R. torques
, as well as the butyrate‐producing cross‐feeder F. duncaniae. The diminished relative abundance of 
R. gnavus
 during incubation of the MDSC with PGM + BSM is remarkable, as 
R. gnavus
 possesses a competitive sialic acid release and metabolism strategy (Bell et al.  2019 ; Tailford et al.  2015 ). Another important factor for the growth of 
R. gnavus
 and 
R. torques
 on mucins is their predominantly extracellular fucosidase activity (Crost et al.  2013 ). The lower abundance of fucose at 0 h in PGM + BSM compared to PGM could contribute to the lower abundance of 
R. gnavus
 and 
R. torques
 on PGM + BSM. This was also reflected in the metabolite production showing lower ethanol and formate production in PGM + BSM which could be related to 
R. gnavus
 and 
R. torques
 (Kim et al.  2023 ; Moore et al.  1976 ). Lastly, the availability of specific core structures could impact the microbial composition. PGM + BSM contains a wider variety of core structures compared to solely PGM or BSM. Depending on the produced enzymes by the different bacteria, specific core structures could be better accessible to certain species. In the Supporting Information Discussion, we more closely examine the potential relationships between O‐glycans of PGM and BSM and microbial enzymes targeting these O‐glycans within MDSC. Addition of sialylated glycans 3′‐sialylgalacto‐N‐biose, often present terminally linked to mucin O‐glycans, and 6′‐α‐sialyl‐N‐acetyllactosamine, often present terminally linked to both O‐ and N‐glycans and sialyl Lewis antigens, to PGM (PGM + SG) also significantly affected the community composition. The relative abundance of 
A. muciniphila
 and 
B. fragilis
 significantly increased on PGM + SG as substrate compared to PGM. B. fragilis is capable of releasing and metabolising sialic acid and a unique pathway for sialic acid utilisation has been described (Bell et al.  2023 ; Brigham et al.  2009 ; Ravcheev and Thiele  2017 ). Therefore, 
B. fragilis
 might benefit from the additional sialic acid. A lower relative abundance of 
R. gnavus
, 
R. torques
, and F. duncaniae was observed on PGM + SG compared to PGM which might have similar reasons as mentioned for PGM + BSM. In addition, sialidases are only encoded by a specific subset of the human gut microbiota (Bell et al.  2023 ; Juge et al.  2016 ; Yao et al.  2022 ). The incubation of the MDSC on only BSM resulted in limited bacterial growth (qPCR and OD600, Figure  S1 ). Bacteroides spp. and 
P. vulgatus
, present in the MDSC, are unable to ferment BSM (Salyers et al.  1977 ) but enzymes produced by 
A. muciniphila
 have been shown able to release monosaccharides from BSM (Bakshani et al.  2025 ) and cleave the protein backbone (Medley et al.  2022 ). Addition of BSM to a rich medium also enhanced butyrate production by a community including 
A. muciniphila
 (Shuoker et al.  2023 ). However, 
A. muciniphila
 is unable to grow on BSM alone, even with the addition of L‐threonine. It could be speculated that the MDSC cannot grow with BSM as the sole substrate due to energetic constraints. Another option is that a specific component which the community needs for growth could be missing or is inaccessible in BSM. Interestingly, 
A. muciniphila
 dominated the community when grown on PGM + BSM, thus it is likely that 
A. muciniphila
 can degrade BSM‐derived O‐glycans. It could also be that the community is unable to generate sufficient energy for growth from BSM as its only mucin source. This could be related to the structural features of the O‐glycans present in BSM such as present core structures, monomeric composition, and level of fucosylation, sialylation, and sulphation (J. Kim et al.  2020 ; Vos, Weber, et al.  2023b ). This suggests that the presence and/or accessibility of specific monomers, glycan linkages, or glycan structures are crucial factors regarding bacterial growth. The significant increase in relative abundance of 
A. muciniphila
 in both PGM + BSM and PGM + SG is remarkable. Even though 
A. muciniphila
 possesses sialidases and can release sialic acid from mucin, it cannot grow on sialic acid nor utilise it (Ottman et al.  2017 ; Shuoker et al.  2023 ). It could be hypothesised that sialic acid removal is important for symbiont 
A. muciniphila
 for initiation of further mucin glycan degradation. The presence of sialic acid could stimulate mucin glycan degradation and growth by 
A. muciniphila
. Binding of 
A. muciniphila
 to LacNAc mucin O‐glycans after sialic acid removal has been shown recently (Elzinga et al.  2024 ) and possibly desialylation and subsequent binding is an important strategy for 
A. muciniphila
 to access the mucin O‐glycans. Several 
A. muciniphila
 enzymes require desialylation prior to action (Crouch et al.  2020 ; Kosciow and Deppenmeier  2020 ; Medley et al.  2022 ; Shon et al.  2020 ; Taleb et al.  2022 ) and mutation of an 
A. muciniphila
 GH33 sialidase strongly impaired its growth on mucin (Davey et al.  2023 ). This indicates that desialylation is an important aspect for 
A. muciniphila
 and its mucin glycan‐degrading capacities. Possibly, the desialylation capacity of 
A. muciniphila
 exceeds that of the other bacterial species present in the MDSC. Differences in sialic acid cleavage and subsequent metabolism could also be influenced by the linkage of sialic acid within the glycan structure or by the type of sialic acid present. For example, 
R. gnavus
 can utilise 3′‐sialyllactose (3′‐SL, α2,3‐linked sialic acid) but not 6′‐sialyllactose (6′‐SL, α2,6‐linked sialic acid) (Crost et al.  2013 ), whereas 
A. muciniphila
 can cleave both 3′‐SL and 6′‐SL but does not utilise the released sialic acid (Kostopoulos et al.  2020 ; Luna et al.  2022 ). BSM contains N‐glycolylneuraminic acid (Neu5Gc) in addition to N‐acetylneuraminic acid (Neu5Ac) and SG contains both α2,3 or α2,6 linked sialic acid (Neu5Ac) in equal amounts. Therefore, the higher relative abundance of 
A. muciniphila
 and the lower relative abundance of 
R. gnavus
 on PGM + SG could be related to a higher abundance of α‐2,6‐linked sialic acid. Therefore, it could be that 
A. muciniphila
 is mostly responsible for sialic acid cleavage in PGM + SG, matching with its dominant relative abundance as shown in Figure  1 . The effect of mucin glycosylation on gut microbiota composition has been shown before in in vivo studies regarding expression of blood group antigens throughout the intestine (Larsson et al.  2009 ) and between individuals (Gampa et al.  2017 ; Mäkivuokko et al.  2012 ). Furthermore, the different levels of fucosylation and sialylation throughout the intestine (Robbe et al.  2003 ) support the hypothesis that specific O‐glycans are important in the selection of the microbiota (Vaga et al.  2020 ). Distinct O‐glycan profiles have also been associated with specific diseases such as ulcerative colitis (Larsson et al.  2011 ). Such alterations may lead to—or be caused by—a different microbiota composition (Kudelka et al.  2020 ; Png et al.  2010 ). By using a novel approach, we showed that O‐glycan core structures, length, and decorations as present in PGM and BSM influence the composition of a synthetic gut community in vitro. This stresses the importance of connecting mucin glycan structures with the composition of the microbiota. Even though this study is limited to non‐human mucin and a selection of gut bacteria, it is a first step in understanding the direct impact of specific glycan structures on different bacteria. 4.1 Concluding Remarks We have demonstrated that mucin type affects the composition and function of a synthetic community in terms of glycan degradation and metabolic profile. As the microbiome composition, the mucin O‐glycan supply, and their interactions are closely related to gut health, this study further highlights the necessity of better understanding the effect of mucin type and essential bacteria (Clark and Mach  2023 ; Paone and Cani  2020 ). Alterations in microbiota composition were shown in this study as a result of different mucin sources varying in length of the mucin glycans, the ratio between different mucin core structures, and the abundance of terminal substituents (fucose, sialic acid, and sulphate). Vice versa, the composition of the microbiome also affects the mode of mucin glycan degradation and utilisation. Therefore, it is important to investigate the variety of mucin O‐glycans and the abundance of terminal substituents between individuals to better understand how this differs and what the variety entails. This should also be correlated to the gut microbiota to study their balance and interactions. As we have shown that the type of mucin is vastly impacting the gut microbiota, supplementing with glycans could potentially be used to improve gut health and prevent diseases (Fekete and Buret  2023 ). More research is needed to further understand the effect of mucin glycosylation changes in relation to the microbiota composition (Belzer  2022 ; Johansson et al.  2013 ; Simon and Gorbach  1984 ; Valdes et al.  2018 ). This study accentuates the elegance of applying synthetic communities to understand mucin‐driven microbial interactions. The use of synthetic communities to answer microbial ecological questions enhances replicability and decreases complexity compared to in vivo studies or in vitro faecal fermentations (Berkhout et al.  2022 ). Here, we have shown that application of an in vitro synthetic community is a simple and replicable way of assessing differences in microbial community composition and mucin glycan degradation in distinct conditions. Since differences in mucin glycosylation profiles considerably influence community composition, this should be considered during the design of future in vitro mucus‐microbe interaction studies, as modifications such as sialylation, fucosylation, sulphation, and O‐glycan core structures are variable between mucin types (Bechtella et al.  2024 ; Larsson et al.  2009 ). Commercial mucin or mucin from non‐human sources can be used as a model system but researchers should be cautious in the extrapolation of these results to humans. Furthermore, it remains important to acknowledge the intra‐ and interindividual variation in human gastrointestinal mucin glycosylation during experimental design (Robbe et al.  2003 , 2004 ). It would be interesting for future studies to extend this work by including a broader range of mucin types, such as human MUC2, mucin obtained from mucus originating from various parts of the human GIT from multiple (healthy and diseased) individuals, or synthetic well‐characterised mucins.
