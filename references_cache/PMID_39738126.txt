Sponges harbour complex microbiomes and as ancient metazoans and important ecosystem players are emerging as powerful models to understand the evolution and ecology of symbiotic interactions. Metagenomic studies have previously described the functional features of sponge symbionts, however, little is known about the metabolic interactions and processes that occur under different environmental conditions. To address this issue, we construct here constraint-based, genome-scale metabolic networks for the microbiome of the sponge Stylissa sp. Our models define the importance of sponge-derived nutrients for microbiome stability and discover how different organic inputs can result in net heterotrophy or autotrophy of the symbiont community. The analysis further reveals the key role that a newly discovered bacterial taxon has in cross-feeding activities and how it dynamically adjusts with nutrient inputs. Our study reveals insights into the functioning of a sponge microbiome and provides a framework to further explore and define metabolic interactions in holobionts. Sponges live in symbiosis with diverse microorganisms. This study uses metabolic modelling to show that the microbial symbionts rely on sponge-derived metabolites to maintain community stability and facilitate elemental cycling, and identifies a key microbial clade that drives cross-feeding processes. As one of the most ancient extant metazoans and important parts of benthic ecosystems 3 , 4 , they have emerged as important models for understanding the evolution and ecological consequences of symbiotic interactions with microorganisms 5 , 6 . To date, a number of studies have stressed the need to analyse the sponge microbiome as a whole, rather than just individual symbionts, in order to gain a more comprehensive understanding of symbioses 7 , 8 . Metagenomic, transcriptomic and metabolomic studies have provided insights into the potential metabolic interactions involving various carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur compounds within sponge holobionts 8 – 10 . For instance, metabolomic analyses have revealed the potential carbon and energy sources that some sponges can provide to their microbial symbionts 9 . Furthermore, metagenomics revealed that the sponge can potentially obtain vitamins and carbon sources from its symbionts, while in turn providing them with nitrogen and sulfur compounds 8 . However, these studies have a limited capacity to predict how metabolic interactions change under the different conditions (e.g., the presence of different nutrients) experienced by the sponge microbiome 11 , 12 . In recent years, genome-scale metabolic modeling has been used to study the physiological characteristics of microorganisms as well as their interactions within a community 13 – 17 . Among the established modeling approaches, flux-balance analysis (FBA) has been widely used for in silico studies of microbial metabolisms 18 . This approach is based on the principle of conservation of mass in a network that uses a stoichiometric matrix and biologically relevant objective functions to determine the optimal reaction flux distribution. Different to the conventional genomics-based analyses that characterize a microorganism by using all predicted functional genes, the overall features of a microbial species in a constraint-based, genome-scale metabolic model are reflected only by “activated reactions” (i.e., reactions with fluxes not equal to zero). Therefore, a FBA-based model can display the actual physiological features of a microorganism and its metabolic interactions under certain environmental conditions (e.g., with constraint nutrient availability) 19 . Several bioinformatic tools have been developed to perform FBA 20 , including those that integrate a spatio-temporal analysis like BacArena 21 . To date, constraint-based, genome-scale metabolic modeling has been only applied once to analyse the metabolic processes and interactions in sponges 14 . This study provided insight into the metabolic interactions of the sponge Amphimedon queenslandica and one symbiont (AqS1) but did not investigate the metabolic interactions within more complex microbiomes. In this work, we apply a constraint-based metabolic modeling approach to the microbiome of the shallow-water sponge Stylissa sp. in order to gain further insights into metabolic processes and interactions in sponge holobionts. Eight genome-scale metabolic models, representing seven bacterial and one archaeal species, are first constructed and manually curated based on metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs). Spatio-temporal resolved FBA is then used to model a dynamic system using information on the natural community structure and metabolomic data. This results in an understanding into the functional roles of individual community members, predicts metabolic interactions in the steady-state community and quantifies biochemical processes and responses occurring in the sponge holobiont. Results and discussion The microbial community of Stylissa sp To define the microbiome members of Stylissa sp., we obtained 17 high-quality MAGs (with completeness 95 ± 2.3% and contamination 0.6 ± 0.5%) and 18 medium-quality MAGs (with completeness 80 ± 7.7% and contamination 0.6 ± 0.4%) from our previous metagenomic study of four sponge individuals 7 (Supplementary Data  1 ). De-replication at 99% average nucleotide identity (ANI) resulted in eleven MAG clusters, likely representing separate species based on the criteria proposed by Konstantinidis et al. 22 . The “representative” MAGs (rMAGs) of the eight species recruited a total of 98.3% of the reads that mapped to the 35 MAGs and therefore represent the dominant fraction of this sponge’s microbial community (Supplementary Data  2 ). These encompass the previously described species of Cenoporarchaeum stylissae (phylum Thermoproteota) 23 , Spongiicolonus versatilis, Spongiihabitans thiooxidans and Oxydemutator thiolithooxidans (class Gammaproteobacteria) 24 , as well as here the newly described species Nitrospongiibacter stylissae (phylum Nitrospirota), Parasynechococcus marinus (phylum Cyanobacteriota), Gammaporibacter thiotrophicus (class Gammaproteobacteria) and Deltaporibacter stylissae (phylum Myxococcota, class UBA9160) (Fig.  1 and section “Taxonomic analysis of the Stylissa sp. microbiome” in Supplementary Notes for further details). The observed relative abundances of the eight species based on read mapping ranged from 2.7 ± 1.9% – 28.1 ± 4.1% across four biological replicate samples of Stylissa sp. (Fig.  1 ). Comparative metabolomic analysis reveals substrates for the microbiome We next investigated potential substrates for the microbiome by using a untargeted metabolomic data set for Stylissa sp. from our previous work 9 . We compared the relative abundance of metabolites in the entire sponge tissue and the fractionated microbial cells. Metabolites that had high relative abundances within the entire sponge tissue while being notably diminished or undetected in the microbial cells were assumed to originate from the sponge and are potentially consumed by the microorganisms in the community. Our analysis identified 32 metabolites that were enriched in the sponge tissue relative to the fractionated microbial cells (Fig.  2 and Supplementary Data  3 ). Of note was hypotaurine, a free amino acid in many sponge species 25 that functions as osmolyte in marine invertebrates 26 – 28 and can be the sole source of carbon, nitrogen and energy for bacterial growth 29 . Another metabolite enriched in the sponge tissue samples is laurate (C12H23O2), a medium-chain-length fatty acid that has also been reported as a carbon source for certain bacteria 30 , 31 . To understand how these sponge-derived compounds influence the microbiome and its metabolic interaction, we included them into our constraint-based metabolic modeling. In silico growth tests revealed metabolic properties of symbionts Metabolic models were constructed, curated and tested in silico for the symbionts to understand their metabolic features (see Supplementary Notes). Inspection of gap-filled reactions activated during each model’s in silico growth test only showed metabolic properties that were supported by genomic evidence (Supplementary Data  4 ). Therefore, the growth tests of the curated models describe the likely metabolic properties of the microbial symbionts. We used a minimal nutrient media (i.e., seawater components, supplemented with a sponge-derived carbon source, where relevant) for each model to predict the basic nutritional demands and metabolic outputs of the symbionts (see Supplementary Notes). Briefly, our results showed that the model CenSfMM8 for C. stylissae and the model NitSfMM6 for N. stylissae can grow chemolithoautotrophically by ammonia oxidation and nitrite oxidation, respectively, which is consistent with closely related organisms 32 – 34 . The model ParSfMM2 showed phototrophic growth for P. marinus with a capability of nitrate assimilation (Table  S1 in Supplementary Notes), similar to other cyanobacteria 35 . The model DelSfMM3 predicted the potential of D. stylissae for nitrate respiration and a requirement for organic carbon sources, such as hypotaurine, indicating that it is strictly heterotrophic (Table  S2 in Supplementary Notes). The models for the gammaproteobacterial S. versatilis (SpvSfMM1), S. thiooxidans (SptSfMM4), G. thiotrophicus (GamSfMM5) and O. thiolithooxidans (OxySfMM7) were predicted to be able to utilize hypotaurine as a sole carbon and energy source, as well as different sulfur sources, such as sulfite, thiosulfate and H2S, for chemolithotrophy, indicating their potential for mixotrophic growth (Table  S3 in Supplementary Notes). Modeling approach predicts ecologically relevant interactions of nitrifying community To evaluate if our modeling approach predicts ecological relevant interactions and features, we initially created a metabolic network with a simplified community consisting of the ammonia-oxidizing archaeon (AOA) C. stylissae and the nitrite-oxidising bacterium (NOB) N. stylissae. With this network we wanted to test if and under what condition the ammonia oxidation of C. stylissae was able to support the growth of N. stylissae. The model CenSfMM8 suggested that C. stylissae is capable of producing gaseous nitric oxide (NO) through ammonia oxidation (see Supplementary Notes), however, it has been reported that NO released into aerobic aqueous solutions will primarily be oxidized to nitrite, with limited or no formation of nitrate 36 . As extracellular chemical conversions are not included in the modeling approach used here, the NO-forming reaction was deactivated in model CenSfMM8 to provide nitrite to the environment, as would be expected from ammonia oxidation 37 , 38 . We next determined the ammonia concentration required to maintain a stable community structure. For this, we started simulations with a population ratio of 77:10 for C. stylissae and N. stylissae (based on their naturally observed proportion in Stylissa sp., see Fig.  1 and Supplementary Data  2 ) and provided seawater compounds in unlimited amounts, but omitted nitrite and nitrate. The ammonia concentration was varied from the lowest reported value in natural seawater (0.3 μM 39 , 40 ) to the highest ammonia concentrations reported for sponges (6.03 mM 40 , 41 ). The cell removal rate (CRR, as defined in the methods) was varied from 1 – 60%. To assess whether the microbial community remains stable during the simulations, a p/p ratio was calculated by dividing the population ratio of C. stylissae and N. stylissae at the end of the simulation by that of the initial community. A community structure was considered to be “stable” if its p/p ratio fell within the range of 0.5 – 2. The results showed that even with the lowest CRR (i.e., 1%), the overall biomass of the two-member nitrifying consortium declined at the higher boundary of ammonia concentrations found in natural seawater (2.3 μM) (Fig.  3A ). The microbial community could tolerate increasing CRR (1% – 20%) when ammonia concentrations increased from 23.3 μM to 283 μM, after which its maximum growth rate was reached. These simulation findings show that a stable and actively growing nitrifying community can only be achieved with ammonia concentrations much higher than expected in the ambient seawater, or when the provided ammonia flux must be greater than the lowest flux detected in the sponge Xestospongia muta (0.1 mM h-1) 41 . This is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that nitrification is often observed in sponges where ammonia is constantly released 40 , 42 and that relative abundances of AOA is positively correlated with ammonium fluxes in Caribbean reef sponges 43 . Our modeling approach thus makes predictions broadly consistent with previous observations on nitrification in sponges. As a second test, we explored how the nitrifying consortium of C. stylissae and N. stylissae impacts the growth of P. marinus, which can use nitrate as a nitrogen source for growth (see above). The community models of C. stylissae, N. stylissae and P. marinus were initialized with a population ratio of 77:10:7 as observed in nature (Fig.  1 and Supplementary Data  2 ), and supplied with unlimited seawater compounds, but omitting nitrite and nitrate (Supplementary Data  5 ). Ammonia concentrations were varied from 0.3 μM to 6.03 mM and the CRR was varied from 1 to 50%. If the p/p ratios of C. stylissae (p/p_AOA), N. stylissae (p/p_NOB), and P. marinus (p/p_PHO) fell between 0.5 – 2, then the community was considered stable. The results showed that the three species stably co-exist between 0.033 and 6.03 mM ammonia when the CRR is no more than 10% (Fig.  3B ). However, the constraint-based modeling results indicate that nitrate production does not play a major role in the growth of P. marinus as it mainly assimilates ammonia. This is consistent with previous physiological studies showing that some cyanobacteria prefer ammonia over nitrate as nitrogen source, probably due to reduced state of the former energetically favouring its assimilation 44 . Furthermore, the maximum ammonia consumption rate achieved at 10% CRR (86 μM h-1, Fig.  S1 in Supplementary Figures) is lower than the amount of ammonia released by various sponge species (130 to 330 μM h-1) 40 . Our simulations are thus consistent with previous findings that the symbiotic consumption of ammonia is not limited by the amount of ammonia released by sponges 40 . Organic carbon sources are necessary for maintaining a stable microbial community After these tests, we next performed simulations to determine the potential metabolic interactions of all eight microbial symbionts. Specifically, microbial cells with an average diameter of 0.81 μm were placed in a 40 × 40 grid arena set as a 50 × 50 μm space, thus resulting in a cell density of 5.0 × 1011 cells mL-1. These parameters were based on microscopic observations of Stylissa sp. (see section “Estimating in vivo cell densities via fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) imaging” in Supplementary Notes). In addition, based on the results of the three-species model, the ammonia concentration was set at a non-limiting concentration of 10 mM and the CRR was set at 10% to allow for the community to have ten turnovers over 100 simulation cycles. This turnover rate is also consistent with previous estimates that some sponges may consume up to 10% of their microbial symbionts to balance their nitrogen budget 45 . A 5% death rate was applied and cellular metabolites were retained after lysis. The simulations were started with 1,280 cells (i.e., 80% grid arena occupancy) at the symbiont ratios observed in nature (Fig.  1 and Supplementary Data  2 ). After 100 cycles, the cell numbers generally increased to 1,600 (i.e., full grid occupancy) and the community structure generally stabilized during the last ten cycles (Supplementary Data  6 ). When only inorganic compounds from seawater and 10 mM ammonia were provided, S. versatilis, G. thiotrophicus, and D. stylissae either disappeared from the community or their relative abundance was significantly reduced compared to what is observed in nature (Fig.  4A ). This indicates insufficient cross-feeding (e.g., from the AOA or the photosymbiont) to support the growth of these three organisms. Fig. 4 Community simulation with different organic nutrients. We next added sponge-enriched organic compounds to the simulations, such as hypotaurine, creatinine, allatonin, glycerate and tyrosine (Fig.  2 ). We initially focussed our simulation on a single, specific carbon source to understand (a) if it is sufficient to recapitulate a community structure seen in nature, (b) how various community members contribute to nutrient utilization, and (c) what kind of cross-feeding or metabolic product release occurs. Only the simulations using hypotaurine could generate stable communities that reflected those found in nature (Fig.  4 ), while the communities solely exposed to creatinine, allantoin, glycerate or tyrosine were found to loss one or more community members (Fig.  S7 in Supplementary Notes and see section “Impact of various carbon sources on community structure” in the Supplementary Notes for further details). When hypotaurine was added at concentrations from 0.2–1 mM to the simulations, all species persisted and the final community structure showed Bray-Curtis dissimilarities <0.05 compared to the starting community (Fig.  4A ). Hypotaurine concentrations above 1 mM resulted in increased relative abundances of S. versatilis or D. stylissae, and the community structure substantially diverged from the starting community. In addition, hypotaurine (0.2 mM) alone (i.e., without ammonia supplementation) could not provide sufficient nitrogen to the community and resulted in a loss of C. stylissae and N. stylissae. This indicates, again, that sponge-derived ammonia plays an important role in maintaining the microbiome structure. The sponge microbiome is predicted to be net autotrophic when consuming hypotaurine We next investigated the cross-feeding processes and metabolic outputs within the steady-state communities above. For this, metabolite fluxes of all individuals from the same species were first summed for each simulation cycle. The population fluxes in the last ten cycles were then averaged as the community structure remained stable (Supplementary Data  6 ). Finally, these average fluxes were further averaged across the three simulations of hypotaurine concentrations from 0.2–1 mM, as these conditions resulted in similar final communities. The community simulation revealed that in the presence of hypotaurine, D. stylissae and the gammaproteobacterium S. versatilis grow heterotrophically, while the remaining gammaproteobacterial species perform mixotrophy, undertaking CO2 fixation fueled by sulfite oxidation as well as consuming hypotaurine (Fig.  5 and Supplementary Data  7 ). Previous genomic studies postulated that gammaproteobacterial sulfur-oxidising bacterial symbionts from sponges and gutless marine worms might live mixotrophically, given that their genomes encode or express specific proteases and ATP-binding cassette (ABC)-type transporters, in addition to various pathways for sulfur oxidation 46 , 47 . Our modeling results here support this assumption by showing that autotrophy and hypotaurine-supported heterotrophy is energetically favorable for some sponge symbionts. Sponges have the ability to feed on particulate organic carbon (POC) 43 , 48 , 49 , and we therefore investigated here how much POC in the form of microbial symbionts can be produced by the simulated microbiome. Our results showed that, under steady-state conditions, hypotaurine was consumed at a rate of 30.32 ± 3.08 μM C per h (Fig.  5 and Supplementary Data  7 ). An overall CO2 consumption rate of 12.29 ± 3.09 μM h-1 was estimated from the difference between the CO2 fixation (15.41 μM h-1), which is mainly driven by the autotrophic C. stylissae, N. stylissae and P. marinus, and release by mineralization of organic carbon (i.e., hypotaurine) (3.12 μM h-1) (Supplementary Data  7 ). The carbon is thus retained or assimilated into microbial biomass (i.e., POC) at a rate of 42.62 μM h-1. This finding indicates that the microbiome can potentially act as a carbon sink by providing POC to the sponge host in excess to the carbon it consumes in the form of hypotaurine. In a previous study, an overall (none-genome based) metabolic network model of the deep-sea sponge Geodia barretti was predicted to have a net release of CO2, despite the presence of chemolithoautotrophic processes (i.e. nitrification) 50 . Such difference may be attributed to the inclusion of the sponge host’s respiration rate in the model for Geodia barretti, which we did not model here. Gammaproteobacteria make limited use of sulfite from hypotaurine resulting in sulfur loss The gammaproteobacterial species account for over half of the relative community abundance (Fig.  1 and Supplementary Data  2 ) and have the capacity for sulfur oxidation, which would suggest a substantial degree of sulfur cycling in the Stylissa sp. microbiome. However, our results showed that three of the four gammaproteobacteria are predicted to only release their hypotaurine-derived sulfite, with only S. thiooxidans oxidising intracellular sulfite from hypotaurine and excreting the resulting sulfate with a flux of 4.51 ± 0.9 μM h-1 (Supplementary Data  7 ). Additionally, only S. thiooxidans could reach a stable population via autotrophic growth when only inorganic sulfur compounds were supplied (Fig.  4A ). The Sat-AprABM enzyme complex identified in S. thiooxidans can couple sulfite oxidation with the direct production of ATP (Supplementary Data  8 ). Previous work suggested that in green sulfur bacteria, the Sat-AprABM complex may have replaced a putative sulfite dehydrogenase in sulfite oxidation due to its increased energy conservation 51 . Sufficient energy supply via the Sat-AprABM catalyzed reactions may thus be responsible for the distinctive predicted growth characteristics of S. thiooxidans. Next, we investigated how the sponge holobiont may balance its sulfur content by estimating the amount of sulfur retained in microbial biomass as particulate organic sulfur (POS). Overall, the constraint-based modeling predicts that under steady-state conditions, the microbiome obtains sulfur from the sponge-derived hypotaurine at a rate of 15.16 ± 1.54 μM h-1, as well as from seawater-sourced hydrogen sulfide (H2S) and thiosulfate at rates of 0.09 ± 0.02 and 0.25 ± 0.02 μM h-1, respectively (Fig.  5 and Supplementary Data  7 ). S. thiooxidans, S. versatilis, G. thiotrophicus and C. stylissae are predicted to use the thiosulfate sulfurtransferase (“rhodanese”, EC 2.8.1.1) to transfer a sulfur atom from thiosulfate to hydrogen cyanide (HCN) to yield thiocyanate and sulfite. The produced thiocyanate is predicted to be converted to HCN by a 3-mercaptopyruvate sulfurtransferase (MPST, EC 2.8.1.2) to yield mercapto-pyruvate, which is then used for the biosynthesis of cysteine. Genomic analysis has identified rhodanese in unclassified sponge-associated gammaproteobacteria 52 , and a part of the gene cluster that encodes a rhodanese-related sulfurtransferase has been found in the sponge-associated Cenarchaeum symbiosum 53 . The presence of rhodanese and related enzymes in a variety of sponge-associated symbionts indicates its widespread use for this thiosulfate assimilation pathway in different sponge microbiomes. The microbiome is also predicted to excrete sulfite and sulfate at rates of 10.72 ± 2.05 and 4.51 ± 0.9 μM h-1, respectively (Fig.  5 ). The difference between this and the uptake above represents the assimilation of sulfur into POS, which is 0.27 μM h-1. This means that concentrations of H2S and thiosulfate naturally found in seawater (Supplementary Data  9 ) are sufficient for the sulfur requirements of the microbiome and there is hence no or little reduction of hypotaurine-derived sulfite required. By consuming the generated POS, the sponge host will thus only be able to recoup about 1.8% of the sulfur lost from hypotaurine degradation (Fig.  5 ). NO release by D. stylissae and nitrogen balance in the holobiont The community model predicts that in the steady-state conditions, the NOB N. stylissae produced nitrate at a rate of 13.23 ± 7.79 μM h-1 (Supplementary Data  7 ), which was reduced by D. stylissae to nitrite and further on to NO at a rate of 4.09 ± 1.69 μM h-1 (Fig.  5 and Supplementary Data  7 ). The expression of the NO-forming nitrite oxidoreductase NirK found in metatranscriptomic 10 , 54 and metaproteomic data 55 has previously suggested that sponge-associated microbial communities produce NO. The production of NO and activity of the nitric oxide synthase NOS expressed in several sponge species have been reported to be involved in the temperature signaling cascade 56 , modulating the contraction of sponge cells 57 , 58 , and regulating settlement and metamorphosis of sponge propagules 59 . The NO-generating activity of D. stylissae may therefore have an influence on such process in sponges. The model under the steady-state conditions also predicts that the microbiome takes up nitrogen in the form of the sponge-derived hypotaurine and ammonia at rates of 15.16 ± 1.54 and 98.07 ± 20.95 μM h-1, respectively (Fig.  5 ). In turn, nitrate, nitrite and NO were released at rates of 6.27 ± 8.12, 91.82 ± 20.91 and 4.09 ± 1.69 μM h-1, respectively. The difference between the uptake and release rates represents the nitrogen incorporation rate into the microbial biomass, which is 11.05 μM h-1. The nitrogen retained in microbial biomass is potentially available as particulate organic nitrogen (PON) for the sponge to consume (Fig.  5 and Supplementary Data  7 ), which accounts for 9.8% of the nitrogen contained in the total hypotaurine and ammonia consumed by the microbiome. Such a low nitrogen recovery rate would result in the nitrogen loss of the sponge holobiont and two scenarios could explain this observation. Firstly, nitrogen balances in the sponge holobiont may be achieved by selective feeding on nitrogen-rich food, e.g., Prochlorococcus sp., Synechococcus sp., and protozoans 49 , 60 . However, such preferential feeding on microbial species is not modeled in the current study. Secondly, co-metabolism of N-poor substrates (e.g., laurate, see below) could balance the N budget. Carbon cross-feeding during co-metabolism of laurate and hypotaurine Given the predicted sulfur and nitrogen loss from the sponge holobiont observed above and the unlikely scenario that the microbiome would solely live on hypotaurine, we next evaluated the consumption of sponge-derived N/S-free compounds, such as laurate, a medium-chain-length fatty acid (C12H23O2), which is also relatively abundant in the sponge tissue (Fig.  2 ). Genes for the oxidation of laurate (i.e., genes belong to the fatty acid beta-oxidation pathway) were also detected in the MAGs of S. versatilis, P. marinus, D. stylissae, S. thiooxidans and O. thiolithooxidans (Supplementary Data  10 ). First, we introduced laurate as the sole carbon source into the model to assess its minimal concentration required to meet the carbon demands of the microbiome. The community structure remained similar to what is observed in nature with laurate concentrations of 0.2 mM or higher (Fig.  4B ). We next tested combination of hypotaurine and laurate at various ratios (1:100 to 100:1) and found that when provided at 0.02–0.2 and 0.002–0.2 mM, respectively, the community structure was quite consistent with what is observed in nature (Fig.  4C ). Based on the observation above that hypotaurine at a concentration of ≥0.2 mM does not lead to an efficient recycling of sulfur within the sponge holobiont (Fig.  5 ) and that laurate concentration >0.2 mM can simulate a natural community structure (Fig.  4B ), we further investigated metabolic fluxes when hypotaurine and laurate were provided at concentrations of 0.02 and 0.2 mM, respectively (Fig.  4C ). Under this simulation condition, overall consumption rates of hypotaurine and laurate were predicted to be 3.03 ± 0.52 and 2.32 ± 0.47 μM h-1, respectively (Fig.  6 and Supplementary Data  11 ). S. versatilis, S. thiooxidans and O. thiolithooxidans were predicted to use laurate and hypotaurine both as carbon and energy sources. In contrast, G. thiotrophicus only used hypotaurine as a source of carbon and energy, while laurate was only employed for the synthesis of lipid membranes (i.e., lipopolysaccharides) and cell wall compounds (i.e., core oligosaccharide lipids) as it lacked genes for the fatty acid beta-oxidation pathway (Supplementary Data  10 ). Fig. 6 Predicted metabolic interactions in the microbiome of the sponge Stylissa sp. in the presence of laurate and hypotaurine. The values next to arrows represent the average fluxes during the last ten simulation hours across triplicates for simulations under hypotaurine concentration at 0.02 mM and laurate concentration at 0.2 mM. Abbreviations are the same as in Fig.  5 . In addition, D. stylissae was predicted to only take up and oxidize laurate, but not hypotaurine. This substrate preference may be attributed to the thermodynamic advantage of fatty acid beta-oxidation (standard Gibbs Free Energy (ΔG∘): -277 kcal mol-1) over hypotaurine degradation (ΔG∘: -36 kcal mol-1) for biomass production (Supplementary Data  12 ). The oxidation of laurate by D. stylissae is predicted to generate and release substantial amounts of acetate, which in turn is predicted to be consumed by other symbionts, including the NOB N. stylissae, the AOA C. stylissae and the photosymbiont P. marinus (Fig.  6 and Supplementary Data  11 ). Such cross-feeding observed in silico is consistent with experimental observations that organic substrates, such as acetate or oxaloacetate, improve the growth of NOB 61 , AOA 62 , 63 , and cyanobacteria 64 . Furthermore, P. marinus was predicted to use a formate dehydrogenase H (FDH-H, EC 1.17.98.4) to reduce CO2 to formate, which was then released at a rate of 0.004 ± 0.006 μM h-1, resulting in an accumulated formate concentration of 0.017 μM by the end of the simulation. This is consistent with previous observations that FDH-N (EC 1.2.2.1), the isoenzyme of FDH-H, is involved in formate production under fermentative conditions in the cyanobacterium Oscillatoria limosa 65 , 66 . Here, the released formate was predicted to be used in purine biosynthesis by C. stylissae, N. stylissae and four gammaproteobacterial symbionts (Supplementary Data  11 ), indicating another metabolic cross-feeding. Impact of the co-metabolism of hypotaurine and laurate on the sulfur and nitrogen metabolism of the microbiome The modeling further predicted that the microbiome, and here mainly the gammaproteobacteria, utilized sulfur from hypotaurine at a rate of 3.03 ± 0.52 μM h-1, and acquired sulfite, H2S and thiosulfate from the seawater at fluxes of 35.12 ± 12.50, 0.08 ± 0.02 and 0.32 ± 0.04 μM h-1, respectively (Fig.  6 and Supplementary Data  11 ). The microbiome released sulfate into the seawater at rates of 38.28 ± 12.99 μM h-1. The difference (0.27 μM h-1) between S uptake and release constitutes potential POS available for the sponge host. A substantial increase in sulfite oxidation (38.28 ± 12.99 μM h-1) was observed compared to when only hypotaurine was supplied, and this could be attributed to S. versatilis and G. thiotrophicus requiring additional energy from sulfite oxidation for CO2 fixation. Notably, this increased sulfite demand could be fulfilled by the concentration typically found in seawater (Supplementary Data  9 ). Therefore, the modeling shows how the changes in substrate availability may transform a sponge microbiome from a sulfur source to a sink, with the latter being consistent with previous genomic observations suggesting that some sponge microbiomes may rely on sulfite oxidation 8 , 67 . Lastly, the microbiome is predicted to use nitrogen from ammonia and hypotaurine at rates of 98.64 ± 23.42 and 3.03 ± 0.52 μM h-1, respectively, and release nitrate, nitrite and NO with fluxes of 16.63 ± 6.39, 71.62 ± 28.18 and 2.60 ± 0.93 μM h-1, respectively. The differences between this consumption and release constitute the PON potentially available to the sponge host, which was produced at 10.83 μM N h-1. The co-metabolism of laurate and hypotaurine thus did not lead to a substantial change in nitrogen retention rate within the sponge holobiont (10.83%, Fig.  6 ) in comparison to hypotaurine alone (11.05%, Fig.  5 ). However, acetate cross-feeding in the presence of laurate is predicted to reduce the demand for ammonia oxidation by the AOA, leading to a decreased nitrite production (90.85 ± 23.06 μM h-1, Supplementary Data  11 ) compared to a purely autotrophic growth under hypotaurine-only conditions (102.18 ± 19.54 μM h-1, Supplementary Data  7 ). While simulations with different carbon sources showed an impact on the amount and type of nitrogenous compounds produced by the microbiome, it does not explain how the sponge holobiont balances its nitrogen budget. In addition to the selective feeding on N-rich food mentioned above, we propose that nitrite and nitrate could be denitrified under anaerobic conditions and that the resulting ammonia could then either be assimilated or support aerobic nitrification. Sponges can undergo temporary oxygen depletion (e.g., when pumping is reduced) 68 and future modeling will explore if this can help to completely balance the N budget of the Stylissa sp. holobiont. Implications for sponge holobiont function This work reconstructed genome-scale metabolic networks for a sponge microbiome based on information on the natural community structure as well as metabolomic data. The predicted metabolic interactions in response to host-derived nutrients provide insights into the intricate dependencies and dynamics of relationships within the sponge microbiome. Our modeling therefore goes beyond conventional metagenomic analysis that describe a comprehensive catalog of the functional potential of a microbiome, but cannot answer which metabolic processes or interactions are relevant or critical under certain nutrient conditions 7 , 8 . Specifically, our analysis indicates thresholds and ranges of nutrient conditions (such a minimum ammonia or hypotaurine concentrations) that are required to support a community structure observed in nature. It also demonstrates how the quality of nutrient input (e.g., laurate versus hypotaurine) can have a substantial effect on nitrogen, sulfur and carbon cycling, and even change whether the microbiome might be overall net heterotrophic or autotrophic. Such information is important to predict how sponge holobionts will respond to changing environmental conditions 11 , not only in terms of their own survival and stability, but also in terms of their metabolic outputs that may influence the surrounding ecosystem. The modeling approach was also shown to be useful in defining the role of less-studied symbiont clades, such as the new taxon of Deltaporibacter stylissae within the Myxococcota, which appears in Stylissa sp. to be central in cross-feeding activities and nitrogen cycling. As such modeling can refocus our study of microbiomes on specific clades that are not readily recognized for their ecological function (e.g., AOA or photosymbionts), yet might have key roles in the community. The modeling of the Stylissa sp. microbiome clearly needs further expansion in the future to address questions around the overall carbon, nitrogen and sulfur balances in the holobiont. This can include the addition of more nutrients (including dissolved organic carbon from the seawater), simulation of aerobic and anaerobic niches, and the integration of a genome-scale metabolic model for the sponge host. We envisage that as more data, including specifics of dissolved organic carbon structure in seawater 69 and sponge genomes 70 , become available, modeling approaches, such as the one presented here, will become central to gaining a deeper insights into the functioning and dynamics of microbial symbiosis. Methods MAGs and functional annotation MAGs were retrieved from a previous study of the Stylissa sp. microbiome 7 (NCBI BioProject ID: PRJNA602572). Briefly, four individual sponge samples were collected from the Great Barrier Reef, Queensland, Australia in December 2015. The microbial cells were enriched from the sponge tissue and their DNA was shotgun sequenced, assembled and binned into MAGs. Here, these MAGs were clustered at 99% ANI with dRep 71 . The relative abundances of an rMAG, which is the MAG with highest completeness and least contamination in each dRep cluster, was calculated for each sample with coverM v0.2.0 ( https://github.com/wwood/CoverM ). Functional annotation of MAGs was based on similarity searches against the Cluster of Orthologous Groups (COG, November 2020 release) 72 , 73 and the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Orthology databases 74 using Diamond v0.9.24 75 at an e-value threshold of 0.001. These results were compared with the functional predictions in the gapseq modeling (see below) in order to corroborate or complement the presence of some genes or pathways. Taxonomic classification and phylogenetic analysis Taxonomy was assigned to each rMAG based on the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) release 214.1 76 using GTDB-Tk v2.3.2 77 . The proposed taxonomic names have been registered with SeqCode 78 . The 16S rRNA genes were identified using Barrnap v0.9 ( https://github.com/tseemann/barrnap ). Reference genomes were selected based on BLASTN searches of the 16S rRNA genes against the NCBI RefSeq database as well as by choosing the MAG with the lowest ANI distance in the GTDB-Tk classification results. The ar53 79 and bac120 80 marker genes were aligned with HMMER v.3.3 81 and maximum-likelihood (ML) trees were inferred using FastTree v.2.1.11 82 with 1,000 bootstraps and visualized with iTOL v6 ( https://itol.embl.de ) 83 . Enzyme, transporter and pathway prediction for the construction of metabolic networks MAGs from the same dRep cluster were combined to a “merged MAG” (mMAG) to increase the completeness of metabolic pathways for the species represented by the corresponding cluster. The program gapseq v.1.1 b568a4e was used for metabolic pathway prediction and model construction 84 . Details of the parameters specified in gapseq can be found in Supplementary Methods. Draft genome-scale metabolic models were constructed based on the pathway and transporter predictions and followed with careful manual curations as described in Supplementary Methods. Gap-filling of the final models A process called gap-filling was used to automatically add reactions to the draft models to enable biomass production. This process predicts biomass production under a chemical condition defined by a specified in silico growth medium (see below Defining in silico growth media). Missing reactions that are potentially essential for the in silico growth of the modeled organism but not included in the draft model were automatically “gap-filled” to create the final model (see also Supplementary Methods). The construction of the final metabolic model was considered complete if the in silico growth and metabolic products were broadly consistent with reported growth phenotypes of related reference organisms. FBA-based metabolic simulation To model variations in community compositions and metabolic interactions (i.e., cross-feeding) in the simulated community on a spatial-temporal scale, we performed FBA-based simulations using BacArena v.1.8.2 21 , which tracks the metabolism of populations as aggregations of heterogeneous individuals that have their own metabolism during each simulation step and thus indicates how microbial cells respond to environmental changes and interact with each other according to biologically relevant rules (e.g., growth, lysis). Growth parameters of BacArena are provided in Supplementary Data  13 , with some of them being customized in the current analysis. All metabolites were allowed to diffuse freely with diffusion rates for gaseous and organic compounds measured for biofilms, which can be seen as analogous to the situation where sponge symbionts reside in the extracellular matrix of the mesohyl 85 , 86 . To achieve a more realistic simulation of cell behavior in the growth phase 87 , all simulations were performed using parsimonious FBA (pFBA) to minimize total flux (the rule of “mtf”) of a metabolic model and to generate the flux distributions of each individual. Scripts and their inputs and outputs can be found in the GitHub at https://github.com/zhssakura/STY8.git 88 . Defining in silico growth media An in silico medium approximates the realistic chemical environment, and here we defined several such media for the sponge-associated microbial community (Supplementary Data  5 ) following the guidelines and approaches described by Marinos et al. 89 . Specifically, the in silico media were defined based on the general inorganic composition of seawater (Supplementary Data  9 ) and compounds that are likely derived from the sponge Stylissa sp. based on a previous comparative metabolomic analysis 9 . The details of the metabolomic analysis, including sample collection and processing, gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis, data filtering and normalization, statistical analysis and data visualization were described previously 9 . Differential abundance analysis of compounds or molecular features (MFs) found either in sponge tissue or the fractionated microbial cells was performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test (parametric, normal distribution) (Supplementary Data  3 ). Community simulation The community simulations for co-cultures were initiated with individual cells placed in a grid arena (i.e., 20 × 20 for the two- and three-species scenarios, and 40 × 40 for the eight-species scenario). At the beginning of the simulations, cells were randomly placed into the arena, filled with 80% occupancy and at the proportion of symbionts naturally observed in the samples of Stylissa sp. (Fig.  1 and Supplementary Data  2 ) 7 . The exchange flux boundaries for each nutrient substrate were set depending on simulation interests, which were displayed as the availability of substrate concentrations detailed in the growth medium tables (Supplementary Data  5 ). Generally, mineral and metal ions in seawater were set as non-limiting (e.g., 1 M), while other seawater components were restricted by concentrations close to those found in seawater (Supplementary Data  9 ). A community turn-over process was also simulated via the parameters death rate and CRR in BacArena. The death rate defines a cell’s relative biomass loss if it does not grow due to nutritional restrictions. If the cell’s biomass falls below the minimal dry weight observed for a bacterial cell (Supplementary Data  13 ), it lyses with biomass compounds being released into the simulation arena. A death rate of 0% was specified in the two- and three-species scenarios to prevent the growth of the models being affected by the additional organic compounds introduced by cell lysis, while a death rate of 5% was specified for the eight-species scenario, where metabolites were retained in the arena after cell lysis. The CRR differs from the death rate by randomly freeing up grid positions for other cells to occupy without releasing any biomass compounds. A cell death rate and a CRR were used through a simulation (e.g., 100 iterations with 1 h time intervals per iteration for the eight-species scenario) to allow for a degree of community turnover and reach stable states of the community composition. After each iteration, compounds from the growth media were replenished to mimic the constant flow of seawater through the sponge or an ongoing release of sponge-derived compounds, while metabolic products excreted through cellular metabolism or lysis were retained and thus made available in subsequent simulation cycles. At last, all simulations were conducted in triplicates. Reporting summary Further information on research design is available in the  Nature Portfolio Reporting Summary linked to this article.
