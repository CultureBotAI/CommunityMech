Due to high Sulphur content of coals in Meghalaya, India, coal mining in this
region generates huge amount of acidic waste characterized by low pH and
leaching of toxic heavy metals which is a major environmental problem. This
study involves isolating native Bacillus species from samples of active and
abandoned Rat-hole coal mines in order to mitigate these phenomena. These
species were then evaluated for significant bioremediation potential in the form
of heavy metal binding and adsorption, as well as raising acidic pH to pH 8.0
(basic) under in vitro conditions. Sixteen (16) isolates were initially screened
to develop the microbial consortia and five isolates (05) were finally selected
for this study (sites specific) based on highest heavy metal resistance, and the
ability to reverse or increase acidic pH. The isolates demonstrated resistance
to the heavy metals under investigation (iron, cadmium, and chromium) with
minimum bactericidal concentrations ranging from 1600 mg/L to 2000 mg/L of Fe,
128 mg/L to 1024 mg/L of Cd, and 64 mg/L to 256 mg/L of Cr. The lowest acidic pH
the isolates could grow in in vitro conditions was pH 5.0, at 37 °C, 150 rpm
shaking with or without metal. On average, Bacillus sp. KH5M11 and Bacillus sp.
KHCL13 have the better binding and removal percentage (%) of iron (99.89 ± 0.066
and 99.87 ± 0.098) respectively. Lysinibacillus sp. SK18-4 have better removal
percentage (%) of Chromium (79.903 ± 17.702), followed by Bacillus sp. KHCL13
(76.14 ± 12.233). Bacillus sp. KHCL13 (97.715 ± 1.040) and Bacillus sp. KH5M11
(97.363 ± 1.260) have better removal percentage (%) of Cadmium, as compared to
other isolates. Amongst the consortia, the removal percentage, does not show
significant differences in heavy metal removals. The iron removal percentage (%)
of Consortia-A22ED9 is 92.08 ± 10.281, and Consortia-B18-4M11 is
89.597 ± 10.829. The chromium removal percentage (%) of Consortia-A22ED9 is
74.15 ± 12.906 and Consortia-B18-4M11 is 77.467 ± 12.420. However, both the
consortia have similar removal percentage (%) for cadmium. Consortia-A22ED9
removed 97.587 ± 0.958% and Consortia-B18-4M11 removed 97.673 ± 0.475% of Cd
respectively. Metal binding was confirmed by FTIR and SEM-EDX analysis, where
distinct peaks and clear spectral pointed out to a specific cell surface
functional groups and heavy metals binding. In all cases, the average cells
optical density (OD-600) for all five isolates was high, ranges from 1.99 to 2.3
for isolates and 2.2 to 2.3 for consortia while the average pH remains
relatively above pH 5.0, ranging from pH 5.4 to pH 6.23 for isolates and pH 5.7
to pH 6.3 for consortia. The study concluded that the bioremediation potential
of the isolates under said condition was commendable both as individual isolates
and in consortia and the possible mechanisms of bioremediation is through
adsorption and binding of heavy metal and not solely because of metals
precipitation. We hope with minimum manipulation of coal mines acidic condition
these Bacillus spp. may become employable in bioremediation of heavy metal and
control of acidic pH generation. However, our study is limited only to the in
vitro as we do not have chance to carry out field trial which would be the
future aspects of these beautiful findings. Much more study may be done on this
field for practical applications.

Abstract Due to high Sulphur content of coals in Meghalaya, India, coal mining in this region generates huge amount of acidic waste characterized by low pH and leaching of toxic heavy metals which is a major environmental problem. This study involves isolating native Bacillus species from samples of active and abandoned Rat-hole coal mines in order to mitigate these phenomena. These species were then evaluated for significant bioremediation potential in the form of heavy metal binding and adsorption, as well as raising acidic pH to pH 8.0 (basic) under in vitro conditions. Sixteen (16) isolates were initially screened to develop the microbial consortia and five isolates (05) were finally selected for this study (sites specific) based on highest heavy metal resistance, and the ability to reverse or increase acidic pH. The isolates demonstrated resistance to the heavy metals under investigation (iron, cadmium, and chromium) with minimum bactericidal concentrations ranging from 1600 mg/L to 2000 mg/L of Fe, 128 mg/L to 1024 mg/L of Cd, and 64 mg/L to 256 mg/L of Cr. The lowest acidic pH the isolates could grow in in vitro conditions was pH 5.0, at 37 °C, 150 rpm shaking with or without metal. On average, Bacillus sp. KH5M11 and Bacillus sp. KHCL13 have the better binding and removal percentage (%) of iron (99.89 ± 0.066 and 99.87 ± 0.098) respectively. Lysinibacillus sp. SK18-4 have better removal percentage (%) of Chromium (79.903 ± 17.702), followed by Bacillus sp. KHCL13 (76.14 ± 12.233). Bacillus sp. KHCL13 (97.715 ± 1.040) and Bacillus sp. KH5M11 (97.363 ± 1.260) have better removal percentage (%) of Cadmium, as compared to other isolates. Amongst the consortia, the removal percentage, does not show significant differences in heavy metal removals. The iron removal percentage (%) of Consortia-A22ED9 is 92.08 ± 10.281, and Consortia-B18-4M11 is 89.597 ± 10.829. The chromium removal percentage (%) of Consortia-A22ED9 is 74.15 ± 12.906 and Consortia-B18-4M11 is 77.467 ± 12.420. However, both the consortia have similar removal percentage (%) for cadmium. Consortia-A22ED9 removed 97.587 ± 0.958% and Consortia-B18-4M11 removed 97.673 ± 0.475% of Cd respectively. Metal binding was confirmed by FTIR and SEM-EDX analysis, where distinct peaks and clear spectral pointed out to a specific cell surface functional groups and heavy metals binding. In all cases, the average cells optical density (OD-600) for all five isolates was high, ranges from 1.99 to 2.3 for isolates and 2.2 to 2.3 for consortia while the average pH remains relatively above pH 5.0, ranging from pH 5.4 to pH 6.23 for isolates and pH 5.7 to pH 6.3 for consortia. The study concluded that the bioremediation potential of the isolates under said condition was commendable both as individual isolates and in consortia and the possible mechanisms of bioremediation is through adsorption and binding of heavy metal and not solely because of metals precipitation. We hope with minimum manipulation of coal mines acidic condition these Bacillus spp. may become employable in bioremediation of heavy metal and control of acidic pH generation. However, our study is limited only to the in vitro as we do not have chance to carry out field trial which would be the future aspects of these beautiful findings. Introduction Rapid industrialization and urbanization have led to excessive mineral mining worldwide which in turn has contaminated around 40% of rivers and lake worldwide. Heavy metals like Fe, Cd, Cr, and Mn are present at much higher levels than standards safe for drinking water 1 . The famous unscientific “Rat-hole coal mining” in Meghalaya, India, particularly in East and West Jaiñtia Hills districts (with coal reserve of 40 million tons), has brought economic development to the state but at a cost to the environment. Environmental degradation in the form of land, air and water pollution (both surface and ground water), deforestation, soil erosion, surface runoff and caving in the underground are rampant in coal mining areas. Acid mine drainage (AMD) and acidic sulphide rich waste from these coals are net acidic (pH 2.0–4.0), (due to the fact that coals in Meghalaya is of sub-bituminous tertiary grade coals containing high amount of sulphur and low ash) 2 , 3 , and their direct effect is seen in water quality with observable changes such as color change (from clear to brownish or reddish orange), high concentration of sulphates, metals and metalloids with high conductivity and low dissolved oxygen and high biological oxygen demand (BOD) 4 .The waste waters originating from these mines are characterized by the leaching of toxic heavy metals, such as iron, cadmium, chromium, aluminium, manganese, copper, lead and metalloids like arsenic 5 . Heavy metals are classified in two distinct category (1) metals that are required for growth but became toxic at higher concentration and (2) metals that are purely toxic with no known biological function 6 . Irrespective of biological significance, heavy metals can easily affect living cells, as they can enter cell cytoplasm through the relatively slow and inducible ATP dependent specific metal uptake system which is, expressed only during starvation or special metabolic situation. Metals can also enter the cytoplasm through constitutively expressed, fast, non-specific channels, generally driven by chemiosmotic gradient across the cytoplasmic membrane of bacteria 7 . The toxicity of these metals depends on their bioavailability which is determined by the physicochemical state of the environment and in turn depends on the speciation of the metal ions 8 .Once heavy metals reach the cytoplasm; they interfere with various metabolic processes. For example, copper which is a micronutrient is toxic at higher concentration they interfere with the active sites of cellular enzymes and peroxidation membrane 6 . However, several microorganisms require variable amounts of heavy metals as crucial micronutrients for their growth and development, for examples, Fe3+ is principally required by all bacteria while Fe2+ is essential for anaerobic bacteria 9 . Current research has documented several bacteria and archaea-bacteria from various extreme environments. Heavy metal resistant (Metallophilic) bacteria are ubiquitous in nature and they are found in all extreme environments including metal rich environments 10 and the metal-microbe interaction has led to coexistence of bacteria in nature which has shaped the bacteria to cope up with both toxic and nontoxic metals 8 . Bacteria such as Bacillus spp., (Bacillus subtilis, B. cereus, B. licheniformis), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Sporosarcinas aromensis, Micrococcus sp., Kocuria sp., Staphylococcus sp., Acinetobacter haemolyticus, Streptomyces sp., Stenotrophomonas sp., Cellulosimicrobium sp., Methylobacterium organophilum, Gemella sp., Enterobacter cloacae, Flavobacterium sp., and Arthrobacter sp. have been identified from various heavy metal contaminated sites with great bioremediation potentials 11 . Soil, water and sediments contaminated with heavy metals can be remediated through biologically encoded changes in the oxidation state of metals 12 . Bioremediation is a technique for clean-up or immobilization of toxic contaminants, including heavy metals, hydrocarbons, agrochemicals and other organic toxicants mediated by microbes which depend on active metabolizing proficiencies of microorganisms 13 . Microorganisms as metal accumulators possess an intrinsic novel remediation property for toxic metals in the soil. Rehabilitation and detoxification of polluted soil and water with the use of microbes have emerged as the most safe, easy and effective technology. Toxic products released due to human activities in the environment viz. mining of ores, oil and gas extraction, pesticides, pigments, plastic, organic solvents, fuel and industrial processes have been removed or detoxify successfully by native soil microorganisms and their potential as detoxifier have been explored and harnessed largely 14 . The main features of AMD bioremediation are the abilities of microorganisms to immobilize metals and generate alkalinity, which is reversing the reactions responsible for the genesis of AMD 15 . Microbiological processes that generate net alkalinity (a reductive processes) includes iron and manganese reduction, sulfate reduction, denitrification, ammonification and methanogenesis 4 . Biosorption is a process of bioremediation involving the use of biological materials (bacteria, algae, fungi, yeast, plant biomass) as sorbent for removal of metals through binding between sorbent and the metal ions 16 .The adsorption capability of microbes is dependent on the geochemistry of the system and microbial total biomass. Some metal oxyanions do not interact with microbes and their bioremediation is based on their catalyzed redox conversion to insoluble forms. These reduction or oxidation reactions take place as a result of biomass concentration and enzymatic activity of microbes 12 . Little study and report have been found on the impact of acid mine drainage originating from coals mines of Meghalaya, impacting fish in Garo Hills region affecting genetic diversity of Channa punctata fish through DNA damage 17 . Swer and Singh, (2004), have proposed the used of limestone, calcite, dolomite to neutralized acidic pH of mine waste water 3 . Bacillus pseudomycoides and Bacillus siamensis were reported from Khliehriat, Meghalaya, with strong tolerance towards heavy metals in the order of Iron (Fe) > Manganese (Mn) > Zinc (Zn) > Chromium (Cr) > Lead (Pb) > Copper (Cu) > Cadmium (Cd) in the study reporting on pollution evaluation indices employed to assess the intensity of metal pollution in water systems affected by acid mine drainage from rat-hole coal mines prevalent in North-east India 18 . Due to lack of study dedicated towards bioremediation of these acid mine drainage originating from acidic coal mines of Meghalaya, we therefore, proposed to conduct the study targeting Bacillus spp. since they are the most abundant stress tolerant bacteria found in coal mines regions from both active and abandoned rat-hole coal mines of Meghalaya, India, for the bioremediation of heavy metals and neutralization of acidic pH, by assessing their tolerance towards heavy metals (iron, cadmium and chromium) and low pH, and in binding and removal of heavy metals in addition to their ability to raised pH from pH 5.0 to pH 8 an act of neutralization of acidic conditions of the medium. Preparations of heavy metal stock solutions Heavy metal stock solutions of 10,000 mg/L were prepared in distilled water by dissolving the known amount of iron, cadmium and chromium salts. From stock solutions, various concentrations were made by diluting to required concentrations. All metal salts were checked for solubility and if precipitated, a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid were added to maintain solubility. Metal solutions were sterilized by filtering through 0.25 µ syringe filter paper and stored in dark bottles at 4°C21,22,23. Sample collection and characterization Both AMD and non-AMD soil and water samples were collected from two major coal producing districts in Meghalaya, India at (1) Shkentalang coal fields site SK4AS (N25°20.733ʹ E092°08.700ʹ) and site SK18AS (N25°20.727ʹ E092°08.700ʹ), west Jaiñtia hills districts and (2) Khliehriat coal fields, site KHED-1 (N25°20.439ʹ E092°21.488ʹ), site KH5M-2 (N25°20.426ʹ E092°21.530ʹ) and site KHCL-1 (Control) (N25°20.548ʹ E092°21.533ʹ), in East Jaiñtia Hills, Meghalaya, India, Fig.  1 (A). Sampling distance were 20–30 m apart and non-AMD samples serves as control. Non-AMD samples are those samples collected in forest area far away from mining sites, where no mining or dumping of coals were notices and rightly ascertain after consultation with the mine owners. As per our knowledge the area is free from any coal related activities since the beginning. We therefore consider these samples as free from contaminants. Samples were collected in sterile container, kept in the icebox, transported to the lab and stored in fridge at 4 °C, opened in Biosafety Cabinet and Laminar Air flow only for all experimental purposes following safety and sterility standards, to minimize contamination. Samples were analyzed for heavy metals content, pH and total organic carbon (TOC) and total organic matter (TOM). pH was measured using digital pH meter 21 , and heavy metals content was estimated using Inductively coupled Plasma Optical Emission Spectroscopy (ICP-OES) (Thermo Scientific TMiCAPTM 7600) 22 , following standard acid digestion method 24 by weighing 0.5 g of soil in conical flask, crushed and heating up-to 70 °C with concentrated sulphuric and nitric acid in the ratio of 2:1 until digestion was completed in the fume hood. The flask containing digested samples (before dryness) was removed from the hood, allowed to cool and 20 mL distilled water was added, shaken well and then filtered using Whatman No.1 filter paper. The volume was then adjusted to 50 mL with distilled water. Water samples were concentrated with sulphuric acid prior to analysis. Elemental analysis was done using ICP-OES. Organic carbon was determined through Walkley and Black method 25 . Isolation and molecular approach identifications of acid and heavy metal tolerant bacterial isolates To screen for culturable heavy metal resistant bacteria (Bacillus species) from the coal mine samples, strains were isolated through serial dilution technique using culture plates both in metal free media as well as metal supplemented media, a mixture of iron, cadmium and chromium (1 mg/L each), at pH 5.0 22 . The plates were incubated at 37 °C (for conveniency but later was found to be the optimal temperature for growth of the isolates) and growth was observed from 24 to 72 h. Distinct colonies appearing on metal-supplemented plates were initially selected based on general phenotypic features, including colony morphology, pigmentation, growth pattern, and spore-forming characteristics, which are commonly associated with Bacillus-like bacteria. However, this preliminary screening was performed solely for selection and enrichment, and no taxonomic assignment was made based solely on morphology at this stage. Isolates demonstrating consistent growth in the presence of heavy metals were considered putatively metal-tolerant and were purified through repeated streaking. Pure cultures were maintained on agar slants supplemented with the same concentration of heavy metals used during isolation and stored at 4 °C, with sub-culturing performed every three months. For long term storage, the bacterial isolates were cryopreserved by storing in 30% glycerol at -20 and − 80 °C. Total Genomic DNA was extracted using HiMedia HiPurA bacterial genomic DNA purification Kit (Cat. no. MB505-50PR) (HiMedia, India) as per manufacturer instruction with minor changes. The 16S rRNA gene was amplified from genomic DNA using a forward primer 27F (5ʹ-AGAGTT TGA TCA TGG CTC AG-3ʹ) and reverse primer 1492R (5ʹ-TAC GGY TAC CTTGTTACGACTT-3ʹ) in Thermal cycler (Bio-Rad, USA) 20 . PCR mixture (50 ųL) contained approximately 50 ng of DNA, 4 ųM forward primer (27F), 4 ųM reverse primer (1492R), 1.5 mM of MgCl2 (10X Buffer), deoxynucleoside triphosphates, dNTPs (200 ųM each of dATP, dCTP, dGTP, and dTTP), and 0.6 U of Taq polymerase were used. For negative control distilled water was used as template. Approximately 1300–1420 bp long amplicons were amplified and sequenced. The sequence was analyzed using Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) against the database of type strains of the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) 26 and EzTaxon-e server (available online at http://www.eztaxon.org/) 27 . The Phylogenetic tree were constructed using neighbor-joining method, with 1000 bootstrap replications to evaluate nodal support in the tree 28 using Chromas and Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis software (MEGA version 11) inbuilt with CLUSTAL W 29 . Screening of multi-metal tolerance and biochemical testing (gram staining, catalase and oxidase production) Multi-heavy metal tolerant test was conducted by inoculating 1% of 0.5 OD bacterial cells (freshly grown) in 100 mL LB medium in 250 mL conical flask supplemented with mixture of Fe, Cd and Cr (1 mg/L each) and incubated at 37 °C, for 24 to 72 h, at pH 5.0. The biochemical test of Gram staining and catalase and oxidase was performed to screen for stressed enzymes production by all isolates following method of Prescott and Harley (2002) 30 . Determinations of optimal growth conditions (Media, pH, temperature, shaking speed) and survival of the isolates To study the optimal growth conditions of the bacterial isolates, the isolates were grown in different conditions at constant concentrations of heavy metals (1 mg/L each, mixture) of Fe, Cd and Cr. (A) growth medium, namely (1) Nutrient broth, (2) Muller Hinton broth (3) Luria Bertani broth and Low Phosphate medium (LPM) broth, (B) temperatures (4 to 40 °C); (C) shaking speed (100 to 150 rpm) and (D) pH (2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8). 1% of primary cultures (0.5 optical density, OD-600 nm) were inoculated into each 50 mL broth media and incubated for 24 to 72 h. All tests were performed in triplicates in 250 ml conical flask. Bacterial growth was measured using UV-VIS Spectrophotometer (CECIL, CE7200, 7000 series and SmartSpecTM Plus, Bio-Rad), calibrated with blank (sterile broth) at 600 nm. Bacteria were re-grown in metal free media at pH 5.0 to 7.0 and their tolerance, optimal growth condition and survival ability were recorded 20 , 23 . Growth kinetics study at 1 mg/L (Fe, Cd and Cr) at constant pH 5.0 for 72 h The growth kinetics of the Bacillus isolates SK22, SK18-4, KHED9, KH5M11 and KHCL13 were studied in batch culture containing 50 mL LB medium supplemented with 1 mg/L each of Fe, Cd and Cr (mixture) in 250 mL volumetric flask. The flask without metal solutions served as control. The cultures were incubated for 72 h at 37 °C, in shaker incubator at 150 rpm. Sampling was done at one-hour time interval for 12 h and thereafter 12 h interval was considered till 72 h. The OD was taken and measured at 600 nm in UV-VIS spectrophotometer (CECIL, CE7200, 7000 series). The growth curve of both control and the metal treated cells were plotted and their growth patterns compared. The experiments were performed at constant pH 5.0 in triplicates 21 and compared with Gompertz Growth Model 31 . Determination of Minimum inhibition concentration (MIC), Maximum tolerable concentration (MTC) and Minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of heavy metals against the isolates To determine the maximum potential of isolates in resisting heavy metal toxicity, MIC, MTC and MBC study was conducted through agar dilution methods and spot plate technique in LB agar plates containing varying concentration of heavy metal salts (50 to 2000 mg/L of Fe; 1 to 2048 mg/L of Cd and 1 to 1024 mg/L of Cr). 1% of the primary culture grown in LB broth overnight was inoculated into fresh medium and allowed to grow until it reached an OD of 0.5. Then 10µL of the 0.5 OD culture was spotted into LB agar plates containing heavy metals. All the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 to 72 h. All experiments were performed in triplicates. MIC was determined after 24 h of incubation, while MTC and MBC was determined after 72 h of incubation 32 . The highest concentration of metals tolerates by isolates and highest concentrations of heavy metals inhibiting growth of the isolates was recorded and reported. Bioremediation of heavy metals in-vitro at constant pH 5.0 Bioremediation of heavy metals by both individual isolates and their consortia were studied at pH 5.0 (lowest acidic condition where isolates could grow), in 100 mL LB medium, incubated at 37 °C for 24 h in 250 mL conical flasks at 150 rpm in orbital shaker 33 , 34 . Cell’s density, pH change and unbound heavy metals were measured after the end of the study. Screening of isolates for consortia development through co-culture techniques Co-culture techniques (i.e., growing bacteria in closed proximity or as mix culture) was employed to generate compatible isolates for consortia development. Sixteen (16) Bacillus isolates were employed for the screening. Initially, the isolates were grown in solid media followed by culturing them in broth medium and check for growth inhibition or stimulation among the isolates 33 , 34 . Mother culture was grown in LB medium overnight, and on the next day, they were inoculated into fresh medium and allowed to grow till they reach an OD of 0.5 at 600 nm. Cells density was maintained around 0.5 OD (in all cases of experiment) and equal volume was mixed in separate flask, containing fresh LB broth medium. Soon after transferring, the OD was measure and the mix bacterial co-culture was allowed to acclimatized and grow to a new combine 0.5 OD prior to bioremediation study. (A). On solid media The first conditions were to grow them at close proximity through spot plate technique, where, about 10 µL of the cultures were spotted on same plate; the second conditions were through swab and spot techniques, where, one isolates (as based isolates) were swab using sterile cotton swabs and other isolates were spotted on top of it (for inhibition study). Statistical analysis All experiments were performed with six independent replicates per strain for each metal treatment. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro Wilk and Levene tests, respectively, and the assumptions for parametric analysis were satisfied. Differences among strains were evaluated using one way analysis of variance (ANOVA) separately for each metal and parameter including removal efficiency, optical density at 600 nm (OD600), and pH after 24 h incubation. When significant differences were observed, Tukey honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test was performed for pairwise comparisons. Statistical grouping letters were assigned based on Tukey test results, where strains sharing the same letter were not significantly different at p < 0.05. Statistical power analysis was conducted based on ANOVA effect size estimates to confirm the adequacy of the sample size. All statistical analyses were performed using validated statistical methods. The Meghalaya plateau consist of Archean rock formations which are rich in mineral deposits like coal, limestone, uranium, sillimanite and Jaiñtia hills particularly is composed of Eocene Lakadong sandstone, lime stone, upper cretaceous and shale rocks, corresponding to the characteristics of soils being sampled from rat-hole coal mines of Meghalaya 39 , 40 . Heavy metals accumulate in soil and water due to natural phenomena like weathering of parent rocks, volcanic eruption, land slide and anthropogenic activities 41 . However, anthropogenic activities like mining and smelting, waste disposal and incineration, urban effluents, fertilizer and traffic emission increases the rate of heavy metals accumulation in surface soils, agricultural soil, surface water and underground water contaminating whole environment, hindering normal life forms 42 – 48 . One of the commonly employed approach in the identification of bacteria is identification of bacteria using 16S rRNA gene sequencing, as this approach reliable relying on conserved sequence whereby distantly-related organisms can be accurately aligned to distinct taxonomy, phylogeny and determining of species divergence among bacterial isolates 49 . Microorganisms with their ability to accumulate toxic heavy metals and converting them to nontoxic or harmless form, makes them potential candidates, emerging as the most safe, easy and effective technology for bioremediation of contaminated environment 41 . Bacillus spp., Actinobacteria, Microbacterium, Rhizobium, Pseudomonas sp., Alcaligenes sp., Hydrogenophaga sp. and Thiomonas sp., were commonly reported bacteria from heavy metal contaminated sites 50 – 52 . Bacillus spp. isolated from municipal waste dumping were tolerant to high level of cadmium (1.0 mg/mL) and other heavy metals in the order of Cd2+> Cr6+> Ni2+> Co2+23. B. marisflavi strain CR2 and B. mycoides strain AS2, have been described to tolerate Fe and Cr in the range of 7–9 mM 22 . It was also reported that the increased in abundance of microorganisms such as Sphingomonas (25.60%), Acidobacteria (77.05%), Nitrospira (20.78%), Streptomyces (11.32%), and Gaiella (10.20%) which facilitated the circulation of soil nutrients in coal fly ash amended soils and thus enhance soil sustainability by improving soil microbial functions 53 . Screening of multi-metal tolerance and biochemical testing (gram staining, catalase and oxidase production) All isolates were found to be tolerant to 1 mg/L of Fe, Cd and Cr when grown in medium supplemented with either multi-heavy metals or with single metal. All isolates were Gram positive rods. Except for the isolates KHED9 which was both catalase and oxidase negative, other isolates SK22, SK18-4, KH5M11 and KHCL13 were positive for catalase and oxidase production (Supplementary Table S2). Catalase and oxidase activities were assessed as indicators of the bacterial ability to cope with metal-induced oxidative and metabolic stress and were not used as exclusion criteria for strain selection. Heavy metals are known to induce intracellular oxidative stress by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), and enzymes such as catalase and oxidase contribute to the detoxification of ROS and the maintenance of cellular redox balance under stress conditions. Despite being catalase- and oxidase-negative, isolate KHED9 exhibited high tolerance to Fe, Cd, and Cr under acidic conditions (pH 5.0), comparable to or exceeding that of enzyme-positive isolates. This observation suggests that metal tolerance in KHED9 might be mediated by alternative, non-enzymatic mechanisms, such as cell-surface–mediated biosorption, metal sequestration, or energy-dependent efflux systems, which are well documented in metal-resistant bacteria. KHED9 was therefore included in the study based on its strong phenotypic metal tolerance and its ecological relevance, as it was isolated from active rat-hole coal mine soils characterized by high metal load and extreme acidity. The inclusion of this strain highlights the diversity of bacterial adaptive strategies operating in metal-contaminated environments. It underscores that oxidative stress–related enzymes are supportive, but not exclusive, determinants of metal tolerance. Other isolates may have acquired tolerance or resistance to heavy metals by inducing stress-response enzymes, such as catalase and oxidase, which support survival under metal stress by mitigating oxidative damage rather than directly conferring metal resistance 15 . In a study on cadmium-resistant Ochrobactrum sp. CdSp9 and Bacillus sp. PbSp6 and AsSp9, exposure to 50 µg/mL Cd resulted in increased activities of superoxide dismutase and catalase, indicating the role of antioxidant enzymes in oxidative stress management under metal exposure 54 . Microorganisms are also known to employ metal transporters and energy-dependent efflux systems involving ATPases and chemiosmotic ion/proton pumps, contributing to resistance against metals such as As, Cr, and Cd 55 , 56 . Optimal growth conditions and growth kinetics It was observed that all the isolates were growing better at pH 5.0, (confirmed by having higher OD-600 value at pH 5.0 compared to pH 6.0 and 7.0) on Luria Bertani medium at 150 rpm and at 37 °C (Supplementary Table S3), these conditions were then taken as optimized condition for growth of the isolates (despites environmental strains thought to grew well between 25 and 30 °C, we found that these isolates grew better between 30–40°, with optimal growth at 37 °C). The pH 5.0 represents the lowest pH at which consistent growth and experimental reproducibility were achieved. Below pH 5.0 our isolates fail to produce distinct colonies even after 72 h incubation and cell growth was minimal (Supplementary Fig. S1, representative). However, they remain dormant and viable even up to pH 2.0. This was confirmed by re-plating them into fresh medium with or without heavy metals at pH 5.0 where the isolates produced colony and grew normally again. This proved that the isolates were able to survive the effects of heavy metals and acidic pH simultaneously (Supplementary Table S2). Although the pH of soils and water samples are in the pH range of 2–4, our experiment was based on optimal pH 5.0, (lowest acidic condition we can work with). It was further observed that agar-based media failed to solidify below pH 5.0, which might be due to the effect of strong acidity on agar polymerisation. Low pH is known to disrupt the hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions required for stable gel network formation, thereby preventing proper gelling. Increasing the agar concentration did not restore solidification; instead, higher agar levels caused noticeable growth retardation, likely due to reduced nutrient diffusion and increased medium rigidity. These observations indicate that the failure of solidification was primarily attributable to low pH rather than agar concentration. Consequently, all solid-media experiments were conducted at pH 5.0. Similar constraints on bacterial growth at pH values around 4.8–5.2 have been reported in studies from the same region 57 . Bacteria once adapted to combined metal and pH stress, tend to resume normal growth under permissive conditions 58 . In growth kinetics (Table  1 ; Fig.  3 ), it was observed that bacterial cells grown in absence of heavy metals (control) picked up growth earlier than the metal treated one (Fig.  3 a). The metal treated one had long lag phase compared to the control. However, once the treated cells overcame the effects of heavy metals, they grew normally and reached their peak growth at 36 h. The average time difference in growth between the control and treated was approximately 5 h. Using Gompertz model of multiple strains (control and treated), the times to detection (TTD) at a given standard OD were obtained. The growth kinetics of all strains were successfully described using this Gompertz model, reflecting the characteristic sigmoidal pattern of microbial growth. The fitted curves clearly distinguish differences in lag duration, exponential growth rate, and maximum cell density between control and treated cultures. The variation in maximum OD values further highlights strain-specific responses to treatment, with some cultures reaching a lower final biomass under treated conditions. Overall, the Gompertz model provided a framework to quantify and compare growth behaviour across strains under both control and treated conditions, as visualized in the plotted curves (Fig.  3 b). Gompertz model is widely used in biology to describes growth pattern of animal, plants, bacteria and cancer cells 31 . Growth rate of bacteria slower down by metals effect but did not totally stop cell multiplication and thereby proven of resistance towards metals 34 which was also seen in this study. Bacterial growth is either enhanced or reduced by pH, temperature and other environmental parameters by affecting various enzymatic reaction in the cells 23 . But bacteria once adapted to the toxicity effect of heavy metals and pH they tend to grow normally there after 59 .The generation time of bacteria under metal supplemented condition is longer and depend on concentration of supplemented metals and their toxicity 7 . Regardless of its biological significance, heavy metals may enter the cell cytoplasm through constitutively expressed, fast, non-specific channels, driven by chemiosmotic gradient across cytoplasmic membrane or through ATP dependent specific heavy metal uptake system 6 . The physiological requirement of the biological systems to survive the toxicity of heavy metals is to augment a process of balancing between resistance mechanism and the normal cellular metal metabolism which permits the cell to accumulate metal for maintenance of metal dependent activities while counteracting to excess metal concentrations inside the cells characteristically observed in Escherichia coli and Pseudomonas aeruginosa for cadmium resistance 60 . It is important to note that the pH of acid mine drainage (AMD) in rat-hole coal mining areas of Meghalaya typically ranges from 2.0 to 4.0, representing an extremely acidic environment that limits active microbial processes 2 – 4 . In the present study, the Bacillus isolates remained viable under highly acidic conditions but exhibited active growth only from pH 5.0 onwards, which therefore represents the lowest pH used for experimental investigations. In practical AMD remediation systems, biological treatment is commonly preceded by an abiotic neutralization step in which alkaline materials such as limestone, lime (CaO), calcium carbonate, sodium hydroxide, or magnesium hydroxide are added to neutralize acidity and reduce metal toxicity partially 4 . Alternative approaches, such as anoxic limestone drains (ALDs), are also widely employed to enhance alkalinity through limestone dissolution under anoxic conditions, thereby increasing bicarbonate concentrations and buffering mine water acidity. Once the pH is adjusted to a moderately acidic or near-neutral range, microbial processes, including biosorption, enzymatic reduction, and alkalinity generation, can further contribute to metal immobilization and stabilize the system 4 , 5 , 12 . Therefore, in practical applications, the Bacillus isolates described in this study could be effectively integrated into a two-stage remediation strategy involving initial abiotic pH adjustment followed by biologically mediated metal attenuation. Biosorption may be the first mechanism an organism would employ when interacting with pollutants as it is a fast reaction, quick to reach an equilibrium which involves redox process, electrostatic interaction, ion exchange, surface complexation and precipitation of metals 63 , 64 . Fig. 4 Schematic diagram of representation: Showing the possible mood of heavy metal removal from the medium through metal binding on cell surface expressed functional groups such as of alcohol/phenols, carboxylic acids, aldehydes and alkynes, amide, nitro compound, alkenes and akylhalides and simultaneous acidic pH raising/neutralization (pH 5.0 to pH 8.0) by the bacterial isolates (unknown mechanism yet). In microbial metal remediation systems, metal removal may occur through two major pathways: passive biosorption mediated by non-living biomass and metabolically driven processes associated with viable cells. Passive biosorption involves physicochemical interactions between metal ions and functional groups present on the microbial cell wall, such as carboxyl, phosphate, hydroxyl, and amino groups, and can occur even in inactive or dead biomass through mechanisms including ion exchange, complexation, and electrostatic attraction 12 , 16 , 63 – 65 . In contrast, living microbial cells may contribute to metal attenuation through active metabolic processes, including enzymatic reduction, intracellular sequestration, biomineralization, and modulation of the surrounding microenvironment 12 , 13 , 41 . In the present study, all experiments were performed using actively growing cultures at high cell density, suggesting that the observed metal removal and pH neutralization were primarily associated with metabolic activity rather than purely passive adsorption. Microbial metabolic processes such as sulfate reduction, iron reduction, denitrification, and ammonification are known to generate alkalinity and thereby neutralize pH in acidic environments, particularly in acid mine drainage systems 4 , 5 , 8 . Furthermore, microbial metabolism can alter local geochemical conditions by consuming protons, producing alkaline metabolites, or facilitating redox transformations of metals, thereby contributing to the stabilization and immobilization of toxic metals in contaminated environments 12 , 15 . The observed increase in pH during the bioremediation process may also contribute to metal removal through chemical precipitation, particularly for iron, which readily forms insoluble hydroxides under near-neutral conditions. Therefore, the overall metal removal observed in this study is likely the result of a combined effect of cell–surface–mediated biosorption and pH-driven precipitation. Similar interactions between biological and physicochemical mechanisms have been reported in AMD remediation systems, where microbial activity not only facilitates metal binding but also alters geochemical conditions that promote metal immobilization 4 , 12 . However, in the absence of abiotic controls under identical experimental conditions, it is difficult to quantitatively distinguish the relative contribution of biosorption and precipitation, which should be addressed in future investigations. Although the isolates demonstrated survival under highly acidic conditions, active growth and functional remediation were observed from pH 5.0 onwards, which represents the operational threshold for effective microbial activity in this study. In real-world AMD systems, where pH typically ranges from 2.0 to 4.0, biological treatment is often preceded by an abiotic pH adjustment step using alkaline materials to create conditions conducive for microbial processes 4 . Under such pre-conditioned environments, the metabolically active Bacillus isolates described here could contribute to sustained metal removal and further alkalinity generation. While dead microbial biomass has been reported to adsorb metals efficiently through surface interactions alone 16 , 65 , the simultaneous increase in cell density and gradual rise in pH observed in this study suggests that viable cells played a dominant role in the remediation process. Nevertheless, a comparative evaluation of metal removal by live and dead biomass would provide further mechanistic insights into the relative contributions of passive biosorption and metabolically mediated processes and represents an important avenue for future investigations. Consortia developed through co-culture methods One consortium each from active and abandoned mines sites were developed (Supplementary Fig. S2). Isolates which did not inhibit the growth of one another but rather stimulate the growth of each other were selected for consortia development. On solid media it was found that all the screened isolates showed no signed of inhibition of one another growth(in free state)while little inhibition or competition was seen for some isolates in fused. Those that showed no inhibition were considered as potential candidates for consortia development {Fig. S2(i)}. Furthermore, in broth medium addition of cells filtrate stimulate the growth of one another with no inhibition. Isolates with maximum stimulation is considered for consortia development. Isolate SK22 was highly stimulated by KHED9 and form one consortium, Consortia-A22ED9 (Fig. S2(a)). Isolate KH5M11 was highly stimulated by SK18-4 and form another consortium, Consortia-B18-4M11 (Fig. S2(b)). Microbial consortium can be synergistically employed for cleaning of contaminants 33 . A constructed photosensitized biohybrid system (PBS) of Shewanella oneidensis and CdS with Cr (VI) as the target pollutant results in complete removal of 25 mg/L Cr (VI) within 90 min without an electron donor 66 . However, in the present study, the developed consortia did not demonstrate enhanced metal removal efficiency compared to individual isolates, indicating that true synergistic interactions in terms of removal performance were not evident. Instead, the results suggest that consortium formation may improve the tolerance and stability of certain isolates under metal stress conditions. These findings indicate that, in AMD-like environments, microbial consortia may play a more important role in enhancing ecological resilience and functional stability of microbial communities rather than in achieving higher metal removal efficiency alone. Microbiological processes such as sulfate reduction, iron and manganese reduction, denitrification, ammonification, methanogenesis, are mostly reductive processes that generate net alkalinity, for example, alkali generated from the reduction of both ferric ion and sulfate has a major significance in AMD impacted water treatment 4 . Much of bacteria with potential in attenuation of toxic metal and metalloid have been attributed to sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB), including Desulfitobacterium, Desulfosporosinus, and few members of Actinobacteria and Firmicutes, which have been repeatedly isolated from mining sites 67 , 68 . SRB when combined with coal gangue attained the highest removal percentages of Fe2+, SO42−, and Mn2+ and chemical oxygen, taken as high treatment efficiency in AMD bioremediation 69 . Another SRB isolates revealed near 100% immobilization of Fe2+, Zn2+ and Cd2+ by hydrogen sulfide precipitation after 18 days. The isolates were isolated from loess polluted by coal 70 . Dead sulfate reducing bacteria (SRB) could remove 88.29 mg·g− 1 at pH 3.5 for Fe(III) and 93.25 mg·g − 1 at pH 4.5 for Cu(II) through biosorption with removal percentage increasing with increased in biomass 71 . It was found that SRB from acid mine drainage, had a strong metal removal capacity, for total Fe (0–300 mg/L) removal percentage at 92.16–93.17%, total Cu (0–20 mg/L), the removal percentage was 79.79–81.80%, with metal precipitation appearing as black granules (FeS), and contained small amounts of Fe3(PO4)2−8H2O, FeCO3, Fe(OH)2, Fe(OH)3, and Fe2S 72 . In terms of Fe removal, these studies corroborated with our present finding although in our case precipitation did not occur and removal percentage is higher than those reported, which stands at 95% and above. The consortia of iron reducing bacteria (IRB) studied in iron-reducing bioaugmentation of Sediments at Gambiered Guangdong silk dyeing, saw and increased in iron reduction Fe(II) precipitate by 35, 27, and 61%, respectively. In addition to IRB consortium, Bacillus, Anaeromyxobacter, Clostridium, Pseudomonas, Geothrix and Acinetobacter, also brough about iron reduction 73 . Velásquez and Dussan, (2009) 79 , reporting on “Biosorption and bioaccumulation of heavy metals on dead and living biomass of Bacillus sphaericus”, found that there is no significant differences in biosorption of dead and living cells of B. sphaericus OT4b31 stains, although have high and varying degree of tolerance towards As, Hg, Co, Fe and Cr. A superheated steam activated biochar (SSAB) from Colocasia esculentawas found able to removal 97.34% of Fe2+, 94.89% of Cu2+ and 84.09% of As5+ from aqueous solutions 36 . Our isolates found to have comparable Fe removal when compared to this SSAB, proving the efficiency of these bacterial isolates in vito conditions. Rhodococcus erythopolis isolated from coal mine area, were found to tolerate Cr6+ in the range of 1 mg/L to 100 mg/L. The mechanism involved is accumulation of Cr6+ as Cr3+ both within and outside the cell 80 . Another study suggested that three defined consortia employed for degradation of heavy metals (chromium, zinc and lead) and it was found that consortia showed better heavy metals degradation with 93.78% zinc reduction in 72 h and 86.16% when incubated for 24 hours 68 .The biosorption of copper by heavy-metal resistant bacterial consortium from São Paulo, Brazil, contaminated river, was found to be 450.0 mg/g dry cells, obeying Langmuir model in the fixed-bed bioreactor 81 . In all the cases of bioremediation, starting with pH 5.0, the bacterial isolates were able to increase the pH of the medium from pH 5.0 to pH 8 with high removal percentage of heavy metals, which is essential for mitigating acidic wastewater. This study corroborates with the reports of Johnson and Halberg 15 , where they stated that the basic features of bioremediation of acid mine drainage (AMD) spring from the abilities of some microorganisms to reverse the reactions responsible for the genesis of AMD through a process that generate net alkalinity and immobilise metals. Some Bacillus spp. have high buffering capacity towards pH changes, say pH 4.0 to 9.5, where, B. acidocaldarius, B. stearothermophilus, B. subtilis, B. alcalophilus, B. firmus RAB (an alkalophilic Bacillus) and Escherichia coli, have high buffering capacities. The significant proportion of the total buffering capacity was observed for intact cells and it reflect a protective mechanism for specific macromolecules or processes rather than a part of the mechanisms for bulk pH homeostasis 82 . This similar trend is also seen in our study. In a study of self-mediated pH changes in culture medium, found that an increased in pH from 6.1 to 7.4 facilitate and improved cadmium biosorption by Bacillus cereus CD01 when compared to decrease in pH from 7.0 to 6.1, on 72 h investigation and this increased in pH enhanced biomineralization and bioaccumulation of Cd2+ 83 . Another pH dependent Cd removal was recorded for Bacillus subtilis, where, different functional groups responsible for binding of heavy metals was expressed at different pH, as observed, below pH 4.4, Cd binds predominantly to phosphoryl ligands, whereas at higher pH, carboxyl groups play an important role. However, at pH 7.8, activation of an additional binding site (a phosphoryl site) with smaller Cd-P different from the one that is active at lower pH conditions 84 . Our study exclusively reported on cell surface binding of heavy metals and binding happened even at high pH value, whereas, heavy metals accumulation inside the cells were not carried out through cells fractionating or TEM, which is one of the drawbacks of our analysis. Statistical analysis results As shown in Table  3 , significant differences in removal efficiency, OD600, and pH were observed among bacterial strains under chromium, cadmium, and iron treatments (one way ANOVA, p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S9 to S15). Tukey post hoc analysis identified statistically distinct groups, indicating strain specific variation in metal removal capacity and physiological response. Certain strains and consortia exhibited significantly higher removal efficiency and maintained higher optical density, indicating greater tolerance and metal removal potential (Supplementary Table S16). The statistical grouping presented in Table  3 reflects the results of Tukey HSD comparisons, and the high statistical power values confirm the robustness and reliability of the observed differences among strains. FTIR spectra provide qualitative information about functional groups and it can also provide quantitative information such as the concentration of bacteria in a growth medium 81 . FTIR analysis of samples revealed the presence of functional groups on cells surface and metal binding indicated by the shift in the peaks of FTIR spectra 85 which corroborates to the findings of the present study. SEM and SEM-EDX analysis SEM and SEM-EDX study (Fig.  6 ) revealed that under heavy metals treatments, bacterial cells developed surface deformities such as cells clumping and production of exopolysaccharide (Fe treated), elongation and fusion (Cd treated) and even cells death (Cr treated) whereas control cells showed no such deformities and changes. The spectra from SEM-EDX analysis of samples revealed that there were no heavy metals detected on control cells which were free of heavy metals (Fig.  6 a and d), while bound heavy metals on the surface of bacterial cells SK22 (Fig.  6 b and c) and SK18-4 (Fig.  6 e and f) were detected after treatment with iron with prominent peaks. Cell walls of microbial biomass, principally composed of polysaccharides, lipids and proteins offer abundant metal-binding functional groups, such as carboxylate, hydroxyl, sulphate, phosphate and amino groups 16 , that binds heavy metals and can be detected using EDX 86 . Limitations and drawbacks of the study This finding exclusively provided information about the bioremediation potential of native Bacillus species from Rat-hole coal mining sites. These isolates displayed acids and heavy metal resistant properties and binding of heavy metals on cells surfaces, high rate of heavy metal removal and raising of acidic pH to more higher pH value above pH 5.0, without themselves producing acidic filtrates. The current findings suggests that these isolates might have employed a reductive mechanism in response to heavy metals stress as reported for other studies with similar findings. Although this study is showing promising results under in vitro conditions, however, it has some limitations, as field trial study or a Laboratory scale fermentor was not perform to validate the efficiency in terms of heavy metals removal and reusable of the microbial consortia after 24 h period of bioremediation study (as starter culture for the next batch). Furthermore, passive remediation experiments using dead biomass to compare active and passive bioremediation were not conducted, and advanced analyses, such as cell fractionation or transmission electron microscopy (TEM), were not performed, as the study primarily focused on surface binding mechanisms. These limitations reflect constraints on experimental scope, partly due to restricted laboratory access during the COVID-19 period. Additionally, abiotic control experiments were not included to differentiate between biosorption and pH-driven precipitation mechanisms, which may contribute simultaneously to metal removal. The absence of these controls limits the precise quantification of the relative contribution of biological and physicochemical processes. Nevertheless, the current study provides a strong foundation and enough data to take it to field studies. Additionally, the other risk factor associated with the introduction of biological agent like Bacillus spp. into the environment is that they can act as reservoir of antibiotic resistant genes 87 . Which many of them has been documented and proven that pathogenic bacteria have acquire antibiotic genes from environmental microbial communities 88 . Bacillus genus is known to produced lots of antimicrobial compound and due to these other bacteria thriving in the same vicinity developed and acquire resistant genes against these antimicrobial agents 89 . The Bacillus cereus group, are known to caused food poisoning and their introduction to environment may pose an additional risk 90 , 91 . The experiments were conducted at 37°C based on observed optimal growth under laboratory conditions; however, further evaluation across environmentally relevant temperature ranges (25–30 °C) would provide better insight into field applicability. Implications for AMD bioremediation As described by Johnson and Hallberg (2002), that the main features concerning AMD bioremediation springs from the capabilities of microorganisms to immobilize metals (binding and conversion to less toxic state) and generate alkalinity, which is reversing the reactions responsible for AMD genesis 15 . Our findings in the present investigation very much corroborated with the above statement, where, the Bacillus strains isolated from acidic rat-hole coal mine of Meghalaya, are highly resistant towards heavy metals, survived low pH effects up to pH 2.0 for 72 h, binds and adsorbed metals on cell surface, proven by FTIR and SEM-EDX analysis. At the same time, these isolates, were able to increase the pH of the medium from pH 5.0 to pH 8.0, particularly at lower concentrations of the heavy metals, which is indeed the much-required characteristics especially applicable in bioremediation of acid mine drainage, while, maintaining high cell density in the course of the study. Which are other features to an applicability of these isolates in bioremediation of AMD. Conclusion This original research study throws light on the great potential of Bacillus spp., isolated from least explored rat hole coal mines of Meghalaya, India in adsorption and removal of heavy metals through binding of metals on cell surfaces (even at high pH) and the ability to neutralize and/or raising of acidic pH from pH 5.0 to pH 8.0. Since, cells fractionating or TEM analysis of metal accumulation inside the cells could not be done, we therefore, exclusively reported on cell surface binding of heavy metals only, as an act of metal bioremediation in this case. Through the manipulation of acidic pH of AMD, in the act of raising the pH from pH 2.0 to pH 5.0, through the addition of limited alkali like lime stone, these isolates would become employable in the treatment of AMD. This would reduce the amount of external alkali required to treat AMD associated wastes water in controlled environment, such as large pons and manmade lakes designed especially for treatment of AMD waters. Their commendable growth and multiplication at pH 5.0, high resistant to heavy metals and their growth did not results in acid production proved to be effective and a great asset, in bioremediation of heavy metals and rehabilitation of acidic wastewater or acid mine drainage originated from acidic coal mines, as they could reverse the process of acidic pH generations. In the future, field trial may be conducted in specific experimental set up in large scale utilizing these Bacillus strains in environmental cleanup of acidic coal mines wastes in acid mine drainage affected regions.
