BACKGROUND: Drought, intensified by climate change, poses a mounting threat to
global food security by severely constraining crop productivity. While microbial
inoculants offer promise for drought tolerance, their poor adaptability remains
insufficient for extremely water-deficient environments. Desert plants host
unique drought-adapted microbiomes that remain largely unexplored for
agricultural applications.
RESULTS: Here, we investigated the microbial community of the desert shrub
Caragana korshinskii and identified a core set of drought-responsive strains. A
synthetic microbial community (SynCom) derived from these strains significantly
improved wheat growth under drought stress. Metagenomic analyses revealed that
microbial functions related to biofilm formation, quorum sensing, and carbon
metabolism were enriched, with Pseudomonas identified as a key functional taxon.
Guided by inter-strain interactions in biofilm assembly, we streamlined the
consortium into a five-member synthetic community, where quorum-sensing signals
promoted community-wide biofilm formation. Community biofilm production improved
strain colonization and conferred greater drought tolerance compared to
monocultures. In plants, mechanistic investigations indicated that the
simplified SynCom inoculation universally upregulated MAPK and jasmonic acid
signaling pathways. Furthermore, carbohydrate metabolic pathways such as starch
and sucrose metabolism were specifically activated, suggesting a multi-level
mechanism underlying SynCom-mediated drought tolerance.
CONCLUSIONS: These findings demonstrate that SynCom constructed on the
endophytic flora of desert plants can significantly enhance crop drought
tolerance. Our work highlights the pivotal role of community biofilm synthesis
in facilitating root colonization and activating a multidimensional drought
tolerance network in plants. This study not only gives an ecological perspective
on desert microbiome adaptations but also offers a strategic framework for
developing effective microbial inoculants for arid-region agriculture. Video

Abstract Background Drought, intensified by climate change, poses a mounting threat to global food security by severely constraining crop productivity. While microbial inoculants offer promise for drought tolerance, their poor adaptability remains insufficient for extremely water-deficient environments. Desert plants host unique drought-adapted microbiomes that remain largely unexplored for agricultural applications. Results Here, we investigated the microbial community of the desert shrub Caragana korshinskii and identified a core set of drought-responsive strains. A synthetic microbial community (SynCom) derived from these strains significantly improved wheat growth under drought stress. Metagenomic analyses revealed that microbial functions related to biofilm formation, quorum sensing, and carbon metabolism were enriched, with Pseudomonas identified as a key functional taxon. Guided by inter-strain interactions in biofilm assembly, we streamlined the consortium into a five-member synthetic community, where quorum-sensing signals promoted community-wide biofilm formation. Community biofilm production improved strain colonization and conferred greater drought tolerance compared to monocultures. In plants, mechanistic investigations indicated that the simplified SynCom inoculation universally upregulated MAPK and jasmonic acid signaling pathways. Furthermore, carbohydrate metabolic pathways such as starch and sucrose metabolism were specifically activated, suggesting a multi-level mechanism underlying SynCom-mediated drought tolerance. Conclusions These findings demonstrate that SynCom constructed on the endophytic flora of desert plants can significantly enhance crop drought tolerance. Our work highlights the pivotal role of community biofilm synthesis in facilitating root colonization and activating a multidimensional drought tolerance network in plants. This study not only gives an ecological perspective on desert microbiome adaptations but also offers a strategic framework for developing effective microbial inoculants for arid-region agriculture.

Background Drought, the most widespread abiotic stress limiting plant growth and agricultural productivity, poses a severe threat to global food security. Over the past decade, drought-induced crop losses have exceeded those from all plant pathogens combined, and this crisis is expected to intensify with climate change [ 1 , 2 ]. The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) predicts that by 2050, global warming will expand arid and semi-arid areas by 20–30% [ 3 – 5 ]. These trends highlight the urgent need for strategies to improve plant drought tolerance. Traditional breeding has enhanced crop drought tolerance but is limited by long breeding cycles and restricted genetic diversity, making it difficult to keep pace with contemporary drought stressors [ 6 ]. In contrast, microbial inoculation has emerged as a sustainable and cost-effective approach, as functional strains such as Bacillus and Pseudomonas enhance drought tolerance through phytohormone regulation and metabolite production [ 7 – 10 ]. However, the long-term application of conventional inoculants often disrupts soil microbial balance and reduces efficacy. Thus, the key to advancing microbial technology lies in exploiting novel microbial resources. With the rise of plant microbiome research, numerous core taxa associated with plants have been isolated [ 11 ], yet most efforts remain focused on common agricultural crops (such as rice, wheat, corn, and soybeans), leading to limited microbial diversity. Moreover, land conversion into agricultural systems promotes homogenization of microbial communities, underscoring the importance of exploring natural ecosystems for unique microbial resources [ 12 ]. Desert plants, which thrive under extreme aridity, represent a promising frontier for discovering stress-adapted microbiomes [ 13 , 14 ]. For example, the novel Pantoea sp. strain 11Z, isolated from the desert plant Alhagi sparsifolia, enhanced wheat root elongation rate by 40% under drought conditions [ 15 ]. Such evidence demonstrates the superior adaptability of desert-associated microorganisms compared with typical plant-growth-promoting bacteria from non-arid environments. The Mu Us Desert, a typical semi‑arid to arid transition zone in northern China, hosts a vegetation system that has evolved unique adaptive mechanisms under long‑term drought stress [ 16 ]. This region harbors a variety of drought‑tolerant plants such as Artemisia ordosica, Haloxylon ammodendron, and Salix psammophila, which play crucial roles in windbreak and sand fixation, water retention, and microhabitat improvement. Recent studies have revealed that the root and rhizosphere microbiomes of these plants possess significant functional potential in assisting hosts to cope with water stress. For instance, certain Streptomyces strains isolated from the rhizosphere of A. ordosica can alleviate ethylene‑mediated drought stress by producing 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC) deaminase [ 17 ], while Bacillus taxa colonizing the roots of H. ammodendron have been reported to enhance osmotic adjustment in the host [ 18 ]. These findings collectively highlight the close cooperative adaptation between desert plants and their symbiotic microorganisms, offering a rich ecological repository for mining drought‑resistant microbial resources. Nevertheless, systematic studies on the plant microbiome in this region remain scarce, particularly those employing multi‑omics approaches to elucidate drought‑resistance mechanisms and cross‑host functional transfer. Caragana korshinskii (C. korshinskii) is a xerophytic shrub endemic to northern China and a keystone species in the restoration and stabilization of desert ecosystems. This deep-rooted shrub plays an indispensable ecological role through sand fixation, windbreak establishment, and soil and water conservation, thereby modifying microhabitats and promoting the formation of soil crusts and nutrient cycling [ 19 ]. Its exceptional drought tolerance makes it an ideal model for studying plant adaptation to arid stress. Beyond its ecological value, it also provides high-quality fodder and biomass resources, supporting local economies. Current studies on its drought tolerance have mainly focused on stomatal regulation, such as auxin-mediated control of stomatal development [ 20 , 21 ]. However, the role of its microbiome in drought adaptation remains largely unexplored. Given that the microbiome is often referred to as the “second genome” of plants, systematic characterization of the root-associated microbiota of C. korshinskii offers not only insights into its drought tolerance but also a treasure trove of microbial resources with direct application potential for improving agricultural resilience in arid and semi-arid regions. Synthetic microbial communities (SynComs) have recently emerged as a powerful tool to dissect and manipulate plant–microbiome interactions [ 22 – 24 ]. Unlike single-strain inoculations, SynComs combine cultivable strains into designed consortia, providing functional diversity, environmental stability, and improved colonization capacity [ 25 – 27 ]. Most SynComs have been assembled by selecting taxa enriched under stress conditions, as shown in Arabidopsis drought-tolerant ecotypes [ 28 ]. Importantly, SynComs derived from desert plant microbiomes have demonstrated cross-host effects, such as conferring salt tolerance to tomato in non-sterile soils [ 29 ]. Despite these promising results, the mechanistic basis of “microbial grafting” remains unclear, and the effective use of desert plant-derived microbes to build drought-resilient SynComs is still in its infancy. In this study, we investigated the microbial distribution patterns across different plant compartments of C. korshinskii in a regional desert ecosystem. Based on identified core microbial taxa, we constructed a broad-spectrum drought-resistant SynCom. Using amplicon sequencing, metagenomic analysis, and functional validation, we subsequently designed a simplified five-strain SynCom (SynCom5) that synergistically enhances wheat drought tolerance via community biofilms. Transcriptome analysis revealed the molecular mechanisms underlying SynCom5-induced drought responses. We proposed three hypotheses: (1) C. korshinskii harbors a core group of drought-associated endophytes; (2) SynComs derived from this core group can confer drought tolerance phenotypes across hosts; and (3) a functionally simplified SynCom enhances drought tolerance synergistically through microbial interactions. Our study addresses a critical research gap in leveraging desert plant microbiomes for cross-host drought tolerance and provides both a theoretical basis and a technical pathway for developing new microbial inoculants in arid and semi-arid regions. Materials and methods Sample collection and soil characteristics Field sampling was conducted across eight regions spanning three provinces within the Mu Us Desert (37°–40°N, 106°–111°E) during July 2023. A hierarchical sampling design was implemented, with three replicate sites (maximum linear distance 330 km) established in each region, resulting in 24 sampling locations. At each site, three healthy C. korshinskii plants were randomly selected, yielding a total of 288 samples (4 tissue types × 3 plants × 24 sites). Elevation at sampling points ranged from 700 to 2100 m above sea level. Rhizosphere soil (defined as soil within a 3-mm radius of root surfaces) was collected by gently shaking roots in 30 mL sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Root tissues were removed after 6 min of inversion and shaking (150 rpm), and rhizosphere sediments were obtained by centrifugation (4000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). Root, stem, and leaf tissues underwent surface sterilization with 75% ethanol for 3 min, followed by three rinses with sterile PBS. Samples were then subjected to ultrasonic treatment (40 kHz, 10 min) to remove epiphytic microorganisms, blotted dry with sterile filter paper, and immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. All samples were transported on dry ice and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Methods for measuring soil characteristics and local climate conditions are detailed in the supplementary document. Amplicon sequencing and raw data analysis Genomic DNA was extracted from all samples using the FastDNA™ SPIN Soil Extraction Kit (MP Biomedicals, USA). The V5–V7 variable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using barcoded primers 799 F (5′–AACMGGATTAGATACCCKG–3′) and 1193R (5′–ACGTCATCCCCACCTTCC–3′). Each sample was amplified three times individually to minimize errors, followed by agarose electrophoresis to verify quality and purity. The purified sequences were sequenced on the MiSeq PE300 platform from Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Raw data were analyzed using the EasyAmplicon pipeline [ 30 ]. Paired-end reads were merged using VSEARCH (v2.7.1), with primers removed and quality control performed (quality ≥ 20). Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated using the UNOISE3 algorithm in USEARCH (v11.0.667). The sequences were de-embedded based on the SILVA database (v138.1). ASVs were classified using the RDP classifier (v2.13) against the SILVA 16S database to remove non-bacterial classification components. Finally, equal resampling was performed using the vegan package to generate feature tables with the same dilution (depth 10,000) for subsequent analysis. Sequence processing was conducted based on the USEARCH v11 platform. The α-diversity was assessed by calculating the Shannon index and richness via the “alpha_div” module, and a phylogenetic tree was constructed using the 'cluster_agg' algorithm. Systematic evolutionary α-diversity analysis was then performed using the picante package to integrate phylogenetic information. In β-diversity analysis, the “beta_div” module was used to construct the Bray–Curtis distance matrix, which was visualized through Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA). Permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA, 999 permutations) was conducted using the vegan package’s adonis function to test the significance of inter-group community differences. Methodologies for soil physicochemical characterization and climatic data acquisition Soil physicochemical properties, including pH, electrical conductivity (EC), available nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), potassium (K), total N/P/K, and soil organic carbon (SOC), were determined using standard protocols [ 31 , 32 ]. Specifically, soil pH and EC were measured in supernatants at soil-to-water ratios of 1:2.5 and 1:5, respectively, using a pH meter and a conductivity meter. AN was extracted with 1 M KCl at a 1:10 soil-to-solution ratio and quantified using a continuous flow analyzer. AP was extracted with 0.5 M NaHCO3 (1:20) and analyzed by the molybdenum antimony anti-colorimetric method. AK was extracted with 1 M ammonium acetate (1:10) and measured via flame photometry. TN was determined by the Kjeldahl method. TP was analyzed using the NaOH melting–molybdenum antimony anti-colorimetric method, while TK was assessed by NaOH melting followed by flame photometry. SOC was measured by the potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) oxidation method with concentrated H2SO4. Climatic data for each sampling site were obtained from two independent, globally recognized high-resolution climate databases to ensure accuracy and relevance. Mean Annual Temperature (MAT) data were sourced from WorldClim version 2.1 ( www.worldclim.org ) at a spatial resolution of 30 arc-seconds (~ 1 km2). The Aridity Index (AI), a key metric for assessing site-specific moisture availability and drought stress, was acquired from the Global Aridity Index and Potential Evapotranspiration (ET0) Database v3 developed by the CGIAR Consortium for Spatial Information (CGIAR-CSI, version 3) [ 33 ]. The AI is defined as the ratio of mean annual precipitation (P) to mean annual potential evapotranspiration (PET), calculated as AI = P/PET. Values were extracted at a spatial resolution of 30 arc-seconds for each sampling coordinate. The environmental indicator results for all sampling points and potted soil are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Screening of core microbiota We first established a core microbiome based on root enrichment in C. korshinskii, then identified key bacterial strains with significant functional and taxonomic contributions through random forest modeling and uniqueness–occupancy analysis (Fig. 1 ). BS, bulk soil; RS, rhizosphere soil; RE, root endosphere Identification of root-specific core ASVs based on shared and enriched ASVs To identify the core ASVs in each compartment, we first selected high-abundance ASVs with a relative abundance ≥ 0.01% in at least one sample to reduce the influence of rare taxa and sequencing errors. Then, we used the UpSetR package to visualize the shared and unique ASVs across different compartments. ASVs that were present in at least 80% of the samples within a compartment were considered as shared ASVs of that compartment. Additionally, we performed differential abundance analysis using the edgeR package to identify ASVs that were significantly enriched in each compartment (FDR < 0.05). The compartment-specific core ASVs were defined as the intersection of the shared ASVs and the significantly enriched ASVs in that compartment. Identification of AI-driven functional core ASVs To explore the association between microbial communities and environmental factors, we focused on the aridity index (AI) as a key environmental gradient. We used the pheatmap package to visualize the Spearman correlation between the Shannon index and soil physicochemical indicators. Linear regression analysis between the Shannon index and AI was performed using the ggpubr package. Furthermore, we built a random forest model based on the rfPermute package to identify ASVs that were important predictors of AI. The importance of each ASV was evaluated by Mean Decrease Accuracy through 1000 permutation tests. The overall significance of the model was verified using the A3 package and the randomForest package. ASVs with a Mean Decrease Accuracy greater than 0.001 and a permutation test p-value < 0.05 were considered as AI-driven functional core ASVs. Identification of taxonomic core ASVs by specificity-occupancy analysis We performed specificity–occupancy analysis to identify taxonomic core ASVs that are both specialized and prevalent in each compartment. For each compartment, we calculated two indices for each ASV: (1) Occupancy, defined as the proportion of samples in which the ASV was present within the compartment; (2) Specificity, defined as the ratio of the mean relative abundance of the ASV in the compartment to the sum of its mean relative abundances across all compartments. ASVs with both specificity and occupancy exceeding 0.7 in a compartment were considered as taxonomic core ASVs for that compartment. This dual-threshold approach ensures that the selected ASVs are not only prevalent within the compartment but also highly specific to it. Root endophytic bacteria isolation and identification Due to the high selectivity and cooperativity within the root endophytic microbial community, we therefore isolated bacterial strains from the root tissues of C. korshinskii for downstream studies. Isolation experiments were based on pooled samples from all three plant roots at each sampling site to ensure acquisition of a representative endophyte community from that location. Plant root tissues were surface-sterilized by sequential immersion in 75% (v/v) ethanol for 30 s and 2% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite solution for 2 min, followed by three and five rinses with sterile distilled water, respectively. The efficacy of sterilization was verified by imprinting the final rinse water onto LB agar plates and confirming no microbial growth after 7 days of incubation. Sterilized roots were aseptically sectioned into ~ 5 mm3 fragments, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and ground to a fine powder using a sterile mortar and pestle. The powder was resuspended in sterile 1 × phosphate-buffered saline at a 1:10 (w/v) ratio. The homogenate was subjected to serial ten-fold dilutions up to 10–5. Aliquots (200 µL) of the 10–3, 10–4, and 10–5 dilutions were spread onto four different types of solid media in triplicate: Luria–Bertani (LB), Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB), Reasoner’s 2 A (R2A), and King’s B media. Plates were incubated inverted at 28 °C for 3–7 days. From all replicate plates at each site, a non-exhaustive collection of 50–80 single colonies exhibiting distinct morphological characteristics (e.g., size, shape, color, and opacity) was systematically picked. Each colony was streaked for purity on its respective isolation medium. A single pure colony from each isolate was then inoculated into the corresponding liquid medium and grown overnight at 28 °C with shaking (180 rpm). Genomic DNA from each purified isolate was extracted using the boiling lysis method. The nearly full-length 16S rRNA gene was amplified using universal bacterial primers 27 F and 1492R. PCR products were verified by 1.5% agarose gel electrophoresis. Amplicons from all obtained isolates were purified and subjected to Sanger sequencing by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The resulting sequences were compared against the NCBI non-redundant nucleotide database using BLASTn for taxonomic assignment. Pure isolates were preserved long-term at − 80 °C in their respective liquid media supplemented with 25% (v/v) sterile glycerol. To link isolates with the core microbiome identified via amplicon sequencing, a local BLASTn alignment was performed between isolate 16S sequences and the core ASV sequences. Isolates sharing ≥ 97% sequence identity with a core ASV were considered cultured representatives. For phylogenetic analysis, high-quality 16S sequences from key isolates and their closest ASV matches were aligned and used to construct a maximum-likelihood tree with IQ-TREE (v2.1.2), visualized using the TVBOT tool [ 34 ]. Arabidopsis co-cultivation assay To efficiently screen the isolated bacterial strains for their potential to enhance plant drought tolerance, a co-cultivation assay was performed using Arabidopsis thaliana as a model plant under controlled drought-mimicking conditions. Arabidopsis thaliana (Col-0) seeds were surface-sterilized (70% ethanol for 1 min, 2% sodium hypochlorite for 8 min), rinsed five times, vernalized at 4 °C for 48 h, and sown on 1/2 MS agar medium (pH 5.8, autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min). Seedlings were grown at 22 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark cycle for 4 days. Overnight bacterial cultures (OD600 = 0.8) were centrifuged (4000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C), washed twice with sterile PBS (pH 7.0), and resuspended to OD600 = 0.4. The suspension was mixed with cooled 1/2 MS agar (50 °C) at a 1:100 (v/v) ratio and poured into plates. Arabidopsis seedlings were transferred using sterile forceps, with ≥ 8 plant replicates per plate. After 3 weeks, fresh weight and root length were measured as growth parameters [ 35 ]. Synthetic microbial suspension preparation and plant experiment Based on the random forest model and specificity–occupancy analysis, 11 core microorganisms were screened; combined with Arabidopsis plate experiments (containing 100 mM mannitol to simulate drought), four functional bacteria that significantly promoted plant fresh weight (≥ 20% increase compared to the control) were selected to construct a SynCom containing 15 strains. To understand their properties, these bacterial strains underwent plant growth promotion (PGP) characterization, including phosphate and potassium solubilization capabilities, acetyl-CoA deaminase activity, indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) production capacity, siderophore production, and nitrogen fixation potential. Detailed protocols for these assays are provided in the Supplementary Methods section. Strain activation was performed using TSB liquid medium (28 °C, 180 rpm, 12 h). The bacterial cells were collected by centrifugation at 4500 rpm for 10 min, washed three times with sterile water (10 mL each time) to remove residual medium, and resuspended to an OD600 of 1.0 (approximately 1 × 109 CFU/mL, corrected by plate counting). The bacterial suspensions were mixed in equal volumes, and the final concentration was adjusted to OD600 = 0.8 (approximately 1 × 109 CFU/mL) as the inoculum. The potting substrate was divided into two types according to plant type: C. korshinskii used sterile river sand sterilized by autoclaving (121 °C, 30 min); wheat, cucumber, and cotton used a mixture of 9:1 (v/v) sterilized field soil (treated as above) and river sand. Seeds were disinfected with 70% ethanol for 2 min, treated with 2% sodium hypochlorite for 8 min, rinsed five times with sterile water, and then vernalized at 4 °C for 48 h. They were sown in sterile river sand seedling trays (22 °C, 16 h of light/8 h of darkness) and transplanted into 5 cm diameter pots after 3–5 days. One week after transplanting, 30 mL of SynCom suspension was injected into the root interface of each plant (the control group received sterile water). Soil moisture content was adjusted every 3 days using the weighing method: well-watered (70–80% field capacity), light drought (50–60%), moderate drought (30–40%), and severe drought (20–30%). Sterilized soil was maintained at 30–40% moisture content under drought treatment. Harvested after 4 weeks, roots were washed, dried, and weighed for fresh weight. Root morphology was analyzed using a dedicated root phenotyping system. The root samples were scanned using an LA-S root scanner (Regent Instruments Inc., Canada), and the acquired images were analyzed for parameters including total root length, surface area, and average diameter using the manufacturer’s proprietary image analysis software (WinRHIZO Pro, v. 2023). Metagenomic taxonomic and functional annotation Shotgun metagenomic sequencing was performed on wheat rhizosphere soil following SynCom application. The specific data processing workflow is detailed in the supplementary material. Taxonomic assignment was performed using DIAMOND (v0.9.24) to align the non-redundant gene set against the NCBI NR database (e-value ≤ 1e − 5) [ 36 ]. Functional annotation was completed by aligning against the KEGG database (gene functional pathways), CAZy database (carbohydrate-active enzymes), and COG database (coordinated ortholog groups), respectively [ 37 ]. Analysis of α-diversity (Shannon index, Chao1 index) and β-diversity (Bray–Curtis distance) of the metagenomic functional database followed the QIIME 2 workflow for amplicon sequencing. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe, v1.0) was implemented with LDA scores > 2.5 to identify differentially abundant functional features. Sankey diagrams were generated to visualize associations between microbial taxa and functional genes, elucidating the taxonomic origins of genes involved in specific metabolic pathways. SynCom simplification and pot culture experiment To streamline SynCom members, we eliminated antagonistic microbial combinations based on strain interactions and biofilm synthesis. The specific experimental steps are outlined in the supplementary material. Based on pairwise interaction profiles and synergistic biofilm-forming capacities, a simplified SynCom5 was constructed. The selected strains exhibited compatible interactions (no antagonism) and demonstrated enhanced biofilm formation (> twofold increase in OD570) during co-culture compared to monocultures. The wheat pot culture experiment for SynCom5 was the same as the previous method. Based on the original treatment group, a heat-inactivated SynCom5 (autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min) treatment group was added as a control. Four weeks after inoculation, the roots were gently rinsed with sterile water to remove loosely attached soil. Fresh root segments (2 cm) were stained with a 0.005% (w/v) Nile red solution in the dark for 15 min, then washed three times with PBS. Biofilms were visualized using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Leica Stellaris 8) with excitation/emission wavelengths of 543/572 nm. Image analysis of red fluorescence density was performed using ImageJ software. RNA sequencing and qPCR validation RNA sequencing was performed on wheat root tissues harvested at 4 weeks post-inoculation with SynCom5 under drought conditions. Wheat roots were washed with sterile water and total RNA was extracted using a plant total RNA extraction kit (Tiangen, DP441). The extracted RNA was then sent to Meiji Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for double-ended sequencing using the Illumina NovaSeq X platform. Raw data were quality-controlled using Fastp (v0.23.4), and low-quality reads were removed. The data were then aligned to the Triticum aestivum genome (IWGSC_RefSeq_v2.1) using HiSat2 (v2.2.1), and gene expression levels were calculated using RSEM (v1.3.3). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.38.3), with a screening criterion of FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC|≥ 1. KEGG pathway enrichment was performed using the Python scipy (1.9.1) package with BH correction. Differentially expressed genes were clustered using k-means clustering (k = 5) and then subjected to GO enrichment using Goatools (v1.2.3). Finally, a GO co-occurrence network diagram was constructed using aPEAR (v0.1.2). Using RNA from the same batch, cDNA was synthesised via a two-step reverse transcription reaction using the PrimeScript RT kit (Takara, RR047A). At the same 4-week stage of wheat cultivation, quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) validated the expression patterns of key drought-response genes identified through RNA sequencing. qPCR validation was performed on the LightCycler 96 system using the SYBR Green method, with TaActin as the internal control. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are detailed in Supplementary Table S2. Results Characteristics of microbial community composition across C. korshinskii compartments To investigate the microbial community characteristics of the desert plant C. korshinskii across geographic gradients, plant-associated samples were collected from 24 sites spanning eight regions in the Mu Us Desert of China and subsequently subjected to microbiome sequencing analysis (Fig. 2 A and Fig. S1). β-diversity analyses revealed significant differences in bacterial community composition among the rhizosphere soil (RS), root endosphere (RE), stem endosphere (SE), and leaf endosphere (LE) (Fig. 2 B and Fig. S2A). Mantel tests revealed that microbial community structures across different plant compartments showed significant correlations with multiple environmental factors (Fig. S2B). Distance-decay relationships further demonstrated that differences in community composition exhibited significant linear correlations with geographic distance, climate, and soil properties (Fig. S3). Collectively, these findings underscore the pivotal role of environmental filtering in shaping microbial community composition throughout different compartments of C. korshinskii. Notably, RE microbial communities were less affected by site variation than other compartments (Table S3). Additionally, the RE exhibited the lowest α-diversity and phylogenetic conservatism (Fig. 2 C, D and Fig. S4). Neutral model analysis revealed that deterministic processes dominated the assembly of the RE community (R2 = 0.241) (Fig. 2 E). The RS community (R2 = 0.75) exhibited the highest model explanatory power, indicating a dominant role of stochastic processes. The SE (R2 = 0.445) and LE (R2 = 0.403) communities showed moderate fitting explanatory power, suggesting their community structure is influenced by both stochastic and deterministic processes. Interestingly, the RE network exhibited the highest modularity and was entirely composed of positive correlations (Fig. 2 F, and Table S4). Collectively, while environmental filtering drives the overall microbial community structure of C. korshinskii, the RE emerges as a uniquely selective and specialized microbial niche with distinct synergistic interactions. Inoculation with SynCom significantly altered the wheat rhizosphere microbial community under drought, enriching genera such as Pseudomonas and Massilia (Fig. 5 A–C, Fig. S11, and Table S8). Targeted tracking of SynCom strains via 16S rRNA sequence matching confirmed their colonization of wheat roots (Fig. 5 D and Fig. S12). Metagenomic analyses revealed that SynCom inoculation also altered the functional composition of the rhizosphere (P < 0.01) (Fig. S13). Pathway analysis (Wilcoxon test, P < 0.05) revealed that SynCom inoculation enriched functional pathways of carbon and nitrogen metabolism involving carbon fixation, carbon degradation, nitrogen fixation, and denitrification (Fig. S14–S15). Furthermore, several enrichment pathways were significantly associated with root characteristics. For example, starch and sucrose metabolism pathways showed significant associations with root projected area (PA), surface area (SA), and total length (TL). The relative abundance of Pseudomonas also exhibited a significant positive correlation with plant fresh weight (P < 0.005). Discussion This study investigated the drought‑tolerant shrub C. korshinskii from the Mu Us Desert, whose microbiome‑mediated adaptation mechanisms remain largely unexplored. In contrast to prior studies in this region that primarily isolated single drought‑tolerant strains, we adopted a community‑level approach to examine how SynCom enhance plant drought resilience [ 38 ]. This work not only advances the understanding of desert plant–microbiome interactions but also provides a methodological basis for harnessing complex microbial communities to enhance crop resilience in arid regions. Desert plants have evolved unique ecological strategies to cope with extreme drought, in which root endophytic microbiota play a pivotal role. Our study demonstrated that, although environmental factors significantly influenced microbial community composition, the root endophytic microbiota of C. korshinskii exhibited greater stability and phylogenetic conservatism, indicating that its assembly is predominantly shaped by host selection (Fig. 2 B–F). This pattern is consistent with previous reports highlighting the conserved and functionally specialized nature of plant compartment-specific microbiomes [ 39 , 40 ]. Drought-response analysis further revealed that C. korshinskii roots enhanced microbial recruitment under intensified aridity (Fig. 3 E, F), echoing the co-adaptation concept of desert plant–microbiome interactions [ 14 , 41 ]. Key drought-responsive taxa were identified using random forest modeling (Fig. 3 G), providing a basis for constructing a drought-resistant SynCom. Thus, the findings collectively support our core hypothesis that C. korshinskii achieves adaptive evolution in desert environments through the assembly of a core root endophytic microbiome shaped by both environmental constraints and host selection. SynCom has emerged as a powerful approach to dissect plant–microbe interactions and improve crop stress resilience [ 42 ]. Most current designs rely on the “cry for help” strategy, which focuses on microbial taxa enriched under stress conditions [ 43 , 44 ]. However, this approach is often confounded by multiple stress signals, making direct applications less predictable. To design a more robust and generalizable drought-tolerant SynCom, we integrated three complementary screening strategies using the native microbiome of the desert-adapted shrub C. korshinskii: (i) random forest modeling to identify key ASVs associated with aridity gradients; (ii) specificity–occupancy analysis to select taxa that are both prevalent and ecologically specialized within plant compartments, thereby filtering out transient environmental noise and capturing a stable core microbiota [ 45 ]; and (iii) a plate-based co-culture assay to directly isolate drought-tolerant bacterial strains (Fig. 4 A, B). This multi-tiered framework ensures that selected members are not merely stress-responsive but are also persistent, host-adapted specialists. Furthermore, by augmenting this native core with drought-tolerant, growth-promoting bacteria isolated from a model plant (Arabidopsis), we aimed to construct a SynCom with enhanced and transferable drought-resistance traits. Functional validation demonstrated that the designed SynCom significantly enhanced drought tolerance not only in the native host (C. korshinskii) but also significantly improved the drought tolerance of non-host crops under controlled conditions (Fig. S9). Critically, this beneficial effect persisted in non-sterile soil, highlighting the SynCom’s resilience and practical relevance beyond gnotobiotic systems (Fig. 4 C, D). Physiological analysis revealed that a key mechanism in wheat was SynCom-induced remodeling of root architecture (Fig. 4 E), a trait strongly linked to improved water-foraging efficiency [ 46 ]. Together, these results validate that a SynCom, rationally designed through integrated ecological screening of a resilient plant microbiome, can confer cross-host drought tolerance. Our study thus provides a novel methodological framework for constructing translatable plant probiotics and delivers a promising microbial consortium for sustainable agriculture in arid regions. At the application level, overly complex microbial communities may lead to competition among members, thereby weakening functionality [ 47 ]. Therefore, simplification is necessary while maintaining core effects. This study successfully simplified SynCom into a core five-strain consortium (SynCom5) based on inter-strain interactions and functional screening. The key feature of this simplified microbiota lies in its enhanced collective biofilm formation capability, which is closely correlated with a significant increase in wheat biomass post-inoculation (Fig. 5 E–G). Further mechanistic insights reveal that the quorum-sensing molecule AI-2 plays a central role in this emergent property at the community level, coordinating extracellular polysaccharide secretion and biofilm construction (Fig. 6 , Fig. S19). These findings indicate that designing SynCom5 relies not only on core strain selection but also on regulating their collective interactions and key signaling pathways. Under different treatment conditions, the microbial communities exhibited distinct ecological behaviors (Fig. S20). Under well-watered conditions, SynCom5 members exhibited greater and more rapid declines in abundance, potentially due to heightened competition from the native microbial community. In contrast, SynCom5 demonstrated greater persistence under drought conditions, suggesting it gained a relative survival advantage when water limitation suppressed the growth of native microbes. Notably, the abundance of microbial community members stabilized after approximately 2 weeks under both treatments, potentially linked to biofilm formation, thereby contributing to the maintenance of community ecological stability. Functionally, the simplified SynCom5 retained high consistency with the original SynCom15 and conferred robust drought tolerance in wheat, as evidenced by improved biomass accumulation, enhanced root development, and favorable physiological indices, including reduced lipid peroxidation and elevated antioxidant enzyme activities (Fig. 7 A). These results indicate that SynCom simplification did not compromise its intrinsic drought-protective capacity, but instead preserved key community-level functions. Transcriptomic analyses further revealed that SynCom5 activates a conserved drought-responsive regulatory framework centered on mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and jasmonic acid (JA) signaling pathways. The coordinated upregulation of MAPK cascade components (e.g., MAPKKK17 and MAPK6) together with JA biosynthesis and signaling genes (e.g., JAR1 and JAZ family members) suggests that SynCom5 triggers a MAPK–JA signaling module that functions as a central hub for microbial-induced stress responses. MAPK signaling is known to integrate external stimuli and amplify stress signals, while JA signaling mediates transcriptional reprogramming through degradation of JAZ repressors and activation of MYC-type transcription factors [ 48 , 49 ]. Together, this signaling axis likely underlies the enhanced antioxidant capacity and stress preparedness observed in SynCom5-inoculated plants. Importantly, MAPK–JA signaling alone does not fully explain the improved drought performance of SynCom5-treated plants. Under drought conditions, SynCom5 additionally induced pronounced transcriptional reprogramming of metabolic pathways, particularly those involved in carbohydrate metabolism, including starch and sucrose metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, galactose metabolism, and fructose and mannose metabolism (Fig. S23). Such pathways are critical for carbon redistribution, osmotic adjustment, and energy homeostasis during water limitation [ 50 ]. As important osmotic regulators, sucrose and starch polymers not only maintain cellular water potential but also directly enhance drought tolerance through osmotic protection mechanisms. The enrichment of these pathways indicates that SynCom5-mediated drought tolerance involves not only rapid stress signaling but also longer-term metabolic adjustment that sustains root growth and cellular function under drought stress. Beyond host-derived signaling and metabolic responses, microbial community traits of SynCom5 play an essential and complementary role in enhancing drought tolerance. Metagenomic analyses demonstrated that biofilm formation and quorum sensing pathways were significantly enriched following SynCom inoculation, with Pseudomonas contributing disproportionately to these functions (Fig. 5 E–G). Biofilm formation is a key emergent property of microbial consortia that enhances root colonization efficiency, protects microbial cells from environmental stress, and stabilizes microbe–plant interactions in the rhizosphere. In SynCom5, AI-2–mediated quorum sensing coordinated cooperative behaviors among community members, leading to enhanced extracellular polysaccharide production and robust biofilm formation on root surfaces (Fig. 6 C–G). Collectively, our findings support a multidimensional model in which SynCom5 enhances drought tolerance through the integration of microbial community-level traits (biofilm formation and quorum sensing), host stress signaling (MAPK–JA), and metabolic reprogramming (carbon and sugar metabolism). Rather than relying on a single linear pathway, SynCom5 establishes a coordinated drought tolerance network that links microbial cooperation with plant physiological and transcriptional responses. This integrative mechanism provides a conceptual framework for understanding how simplified synthetic microbial communities can achieve robust and transferable stress resilience in crops. While this study demonstrates the efficacy of the simplified SynCom5 in enhancing wheat drought tolerance, its introduction as an exogenous consortium necessitates consideration of its integration into and impact on resident soil microbiomes. Our current metagenomic and amplicon analyses (Fig. 5 ) reflect the impact of the initial 15-member SynCom, showing that such an inoculation can enrich beneficial genera like Pseudomonas without collapsing overall diversity under drought. Although this study did not directly assess the ecological impact of SynCom5, its design principles can be reasonably inferred. SynCom5 was streamlined specifically to maximize a key community-level function: AI-2-mediated biofilm formation (Fig. 6 ). This strong cooperative trait is likely to be a critical determinant of its ecological behavior [ 51 , 52 ]. Biofilm formation can enhance microbial cohesion and root colonization success (Fig. S19), potentially allowing SynCom5 to occupy a stable niche without aggressively displacing resident keystone taxa, especially if its members fill complementary functional roles. This principle is supported by recent studies showing that synthetic consortia designed around cooperative interactions—such as those based on moderately related, compatible strains—exhibit enhanced stability and integration into resident microbial networks [ 53 ]. However, whether this potential for stable integration translates to long-term field conditions, across seasons, and under diverse abiotic stresses remains an open question. Future research must directly monitor the fate of SynCom5 in agroecosystems, evaluating its persistence, its effect on native microbial network structure and resilience, and its potential to influence broader ecosystem functions. Such studies are essential for transitioning microbiome-based interventions from proof-of-concept to environmentally informed practice. Conclusion Overall, this study establishes a simplified drought-resistant SynCom derived from the endophytic microbiome of Caragana korshinskii roots, which significantly enhances wheat drought tolerance. The simplified SynCom forms community biofilm characteristics through AI-2 mediated quorum sensing, thereby improving rhizosphere colonization efficiency. At the mechanistic level, the system activates a multidimensional drought tolerance network by synergistically regulating MAPK/jasmonic acid signaling and carbohydrate metabolism (Fig. 8 ). In summary, this study highlights the untapped potential of desert plant microbiomes as a sustainable agricultural resource, advances the understanding of SynCom-mediated crop drought tolerance mechanisms, and lays the foundation for the development of next-generation microbial inoculants. Supplementary Materials and Methods
The specific procedure for amplicon analysis
Distance decay models characterized correlations between bacteria community structure and environmental factors: community similarity was quantified using the Bray-Curtis similarity index (1 - Bray-Curtis distance), geographic distances between sampling points (km units) were calculated via the geosphere package, while soil and climatic distances were derived from Euclidean matrices based on physicochemical and climate data, respectively. Linear regression analyzed decay relationships between community similarity and the three distance types (P-values adjusted via FDR correction). Mantel tests were visualized using the linkET package. Neutral community models (NCM) were fitted using the minpack.lm package. The model output provides two key parameters: (1) the goodness-of-fit (R²), which quantifies the proportion of variance explained by neutral processes, and (2) the estimated migration rate (N.m), which reflects the dispersal limitation between the local community and the regional species pool. Co-occurrence network was constructed following this process: low-abundance ASVs (relative abundance &lt; 0.1%) were filtered using the Hmisc package, and the Spearman correlation coefficient (|ρ| &gt; 0.65 and P &lt; 0.05) was calculated. The igraph package was used to generate the topological structure and extract relevant parameters, and the network diagram was finally drawn using Gephi software. The FEAST toolkit was employed for microbial source tracking to analyze the potential source contribution ratios between sample groups.

Scanning Electron Microscope Sample Preparation and Observation
After the potted plant experiment, carefully remove the wheat root system and thoroughly rinse it with sterile water to remove surface impurities. Immerse the treated root tissue in 4% glutaraldehyde fixative and place it in a 4°C environment for overnight fixation. Subsequently, rinse four times with PBS, each time for 10 minutes. Dehydrate the roots using a gradient of 10%–90% ethanol solutions, ensuring the tissue is not exposed to air during the process. After dehydration, treat the samples using critical point drying. Mount the dried root tissue on a conductive adhesive carrier and perform ion sputtering gold coating. Finally, use the FEI Company (USA) Nano SEM-4500 scanning electron microscope for morphological observation and image acquisition.

Metagenomic data processing and gene catalog construction
To investigate the effect of SynCom inoculation on the function of the rhizosphere microbial community, this study used the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform to perform metagenomic sequencing of rhizosphere soil DNA. Raw sequencing data underwent quality control with Fastp (v0.20.0), including adapter trimming and low-quality read filtering (Q&lt;20), followed by host sequence removal via BWA (v0.7.17) alignment against the host genome (e.g., Triticum aestivum). Clean reads were de novo assembled into contigs using Megahit (v1.2.9). Open reading frames (ORFs) within contigs were predicted using Prodigal (v2.6.3) with default parameters. A non-redundant gene set was constructed by clustering ORFs at 95% sequence identity using CD-HIT (v4.8.1), with the longest sequence in each cluster selected as the representative. Gene abundance was calculated using Salmon (v1.3.0) to determine TPM values (the number of reads from a particular gene per million mapped reads).

For solid-phase analysis, all bacterial strains were first revived from glycerol stocks onto TSB agar plates (30°C, 24 hours) and subsequently cultured in TSB liquid medium (30°C, 180 rpm) to mid-exponential phase (OD₆₀₀ = 0.8 ± 0.1). Each strain was then uniformly streaked onto fresh TSB agar plates (1.5% w/v), and after air-drying, 2 μL aliquots of other strains (10⁸ CFU/mL) were spotted at designated positions. Following 5 days of incubation at 30°C, interactions were classified based on the presence (antagonistic) or absence (compatible) of clear inhibition zones around spotted colonies. For liquid-phase analysis, cell-free supernatants were prepared by centrifuging and filtering (0.22 μm) exponential-phase cultures. Each target strain (initial OD600 = 0.05) was exposed to 20 μL of test strain supernatant or sterile TSB (control) in fresh TSB medium, with growth monitored at 30°C (180 rpm) for 24 hours. Inhibition was defined as OD600 &lt; 90% of control values, while OD600 ≥ 90% indicated compatibility. All 20 pairwise combinations were tested in five biological replicates, ensuring statistical robustness. This integrated workflow enabled quantitative classification of inter-strain relationships, distinguishing antagonistic interactions from compatible coexistence patterns.

For biofilm analysis, bacterial strains were revived from glycerol stock solutions onto TSB agar plates (30°C, 24 hours) and cultured in TSB liquid medium until the logarithmic growth phase (OD600 = 0.8 ± 0.1). Aliquots (100 μL) of each culture were transferred to individual wells of a polystyrene 96-well microtiter plate (eight biological replicates per treatment), with uninoculated TSB serving as a sterile control. Plates were incubated statically at 30°C for 24 hours. After removing non-adherent cells with PBS buffer, the biofilm was stained with crystal violet (10 minutes), dissolved with 95% ethanol, and quantified by measuring OD570. For EPS quantification, stationary-phase cultures (OD600 ≥ 1.2) were centrifuged (6,500 × g, 20 minutes, 4°C), and the supernatant was precipitated with 2.5-fold volume of ice-cold ethanol (4°C, 12 hours). The precipitated EPS was collected by centrifugation (10,000 × g, 30 minutes, 4°C), freeze-dried, and weighed.

In vitro AI-2 assay
Autoinducer-2 (AI-2) production was quantified using a modified Vibrio harveyi bioluminescence assay. Bacterial strains were cultured in TSB medium (30°C, 180 rpm) until reaching stationary phase (OD₆₀₀ ≥ 1.2). Cultures were centrifuged (10,000 × g, 10 min, 4°C) to collect cell-free supernatants, which were filter-sterilized (0.22 μm PES membrane) to remove residual cells. Vibrio harveyi MM32 (AI-2 reporter strain) was revived from glycerol stocks (-80°C) onto AB medium agar plates (30°C, 24 h) and cultured in AB medium (30°C, 180 rpm) to mid-exponential phase (OD₆₀₀ = 0.5–0.7). Reporter cultures were diluted 1:5,000 in fresh AB medium, and 90 μL aliquots were mixed with 10 μL of test supernatant or sterile TSB (negative control) in black 96-well microtiter plates. The bioluminescence was measured using the Victor-X3 microplate reader, with each well's measurement time set to 1 second. The AI-2 concentration results were expressed as the ratio of the sample wells to the control wells. All samples were subjected to five biological replicate tests, and the statistical significance was determined using the Student's t-test (P &lt; 0.05).
