consortium using a multi-omics approach across six sampling stages. Sensory
evaluation, physicochemical analysis, volatile profiling (HS-SPME-GC×GC-TOFMS),

phenylethyl alcohol, trans-β-ionone, geraniol, and 1-octen-3-ol, as potentially

trans-β-ionone, and geraniol might play a major role in the floral-fruity

revealed 154 significantly altered metabolites, 38 of which were associated with
these volatile compounds. Metagenomic analysis indicated substantial shifts in
microbial community structure and function, correlated with physicochemical
parameters and volatile profiles. Random forest modeling identified
Sphingomonas, Rothia, and Bacteroides as potentially involved in aroma
formation. These findings provide insights into the metabolic and microbial
dynamics underlying floral-fruity aroma development, offering a scientific basis
for tailored starter culture design.

ABSTRACT
Pu'er tea fermentation relies on complex microbial activities. This study explored aroma formation
in ripe Pu'er tea inoculated with a synthetic fungal consortium using a multi-omics approach across
six sampling stages. Sensory evaluation, physicochemical analysis, volatile profiling (HS-SPME-
GC×GC-TOFMS), non -volatile metabolomics (UHPLC -Q-Exactive/MS), and metagenomic
sequencing were integrated. Inoculation was associated with a distinct floral –fruity aroma.
Combined ROA V and VIP analyses identified four volatile compounds, namely phenylethyl
alcohol, trans-β-ionone, geraniol, and 1 -octen-3-ol, as potentially important aroma -active
contributors. Among them, phenylethyl alcohol, trans-β-ionone, and geraniol might play a major
role in the floral –fruity character, and their accumulation appeared associated with tea moisture
content. Nonanal exhibited a high ROA V but a low VIP value. Non -targeted metabolomics
revealed 154 significantly altered metabolites, 38 of which were associated with these volatile
compounds. Metagenomic analysis indicated substantial shifts in microbial community structure
and function, correlated with physicochemical parameters and volatile profiles. Random forest
modeling identified Sphingomonas, Rothia, and Bacteroides as potentially in volved in aroma
formation. These findings provide insights into the metabolic and microbial dynamics underlying
floral–fruity aroma development, offering a scientific basis for tailored starter culture design.

Introduction
Pu'er tea is a geographical indication product from Yunnan, China. It is made from the leaves of
Camellia sinensis var. assamica grown in specific regions. Based on processing methods, Pu'er tea
is divided into raw tea and ripe tea. Ripe Pu'er tea undergoes a unique microbial fermentation. This
process gives the tea a characteristic reddish color and a rich, mellow flavor1. Under warm and
humid conditions, these microbes secrete extracellular enzymes. These enzymes drive the
oxidation, degradation, and condensation of tea constituents 2-4. However, traditional spontaneous
fermentation has drawbacks. The microbial community is often unstable. The long and unstable
fermentation cycle often compromises product quality and batch consistency 5. Inoculation with
defined microorganisms has therefore emerged as a rational strategy to overcome these limitations.
Despite extensive research on ripe Pu'er tea fermentation, two critical know ledge gaps remain.
The mechanisms governing aroma formation (especially a distinct floral–fruity aroma character)
are still poorly understood. Most prior studies focused on general chemical changes or microbial
diversity surveys. They did not establish mec hanistic links between specific microbes and the
formation of important aroma-active compounds. For example, phenylethyl alcohol, a floral aroma
compound derived from L-phenylalanine, is known to accumulate during fermentation6. However,
the specific microbial enzymes and metabolic pathways responsible for its formation in Pu'er tea
remain unclear. Furthermore, the metabolic pathways and microbial drivers underlying desirable
aroma formation have not been fully elucidated. The mechanisms by which inocu lated synthetic
microbial community orchestrate aroma development remain largely unexplored.
Enhanced fermentation with synthetic microbial community offers a promising approach to
address these gaps. Inoculating specific microorganisms into unsterilized raw materials has proven
effective in various fermentation systems7. For example, inoculation with Aspergillus niger PW-2

accelerated the fermentation process of dark tea and enriched its floral aroma compared with
spontaneous fermentation8. In another case, inoculation with Debaryomyces hansenii altered the
microbial community structure and improved the sensory quality of Pu'er tea, resulting in a more
mellow taste and a distinct fungal aroma 9. Aspergillus niger is a Generally Recognized as Safe
(GRAS) species. It is a dominant fungus in Pu'er tea fermentation10. Studies report that A. niger
can change the microbial composition and metabolic activity. This leads to alterations in amino
acid-related metabolites and better flavor characteristics7. However, single-strain inoculation often
yields limited sensory improvements. Synthetic communities frequently outperform monocultures.
Co-culture of Lactobacillus helveticus and Kazachstania unispora enhanced both acidification and
flavor complexity in koumiss11. Similarly, mixed fungal fermentation promoted the synthesis of
thearubigins and theaflavins, accelerated the catabolism of polyphenols and carbohydrates, and
increased the diversity of volatile organic compounds12. These findings demonstrate the advantage
of multi-strain consortia over single strains.
In this study, we applied a synthetic fungal consortium composed of Saccharomyces cerevisiae,
Rhizopus arrhizus, and Aspergillus niger at a ratio of 2:1:2 (w/w). This specific combination and
ratio were selected based on our prior work. Our previous study indicated that co -fermentation
with these three fungi at this ratio generated a unique and pronounced floral –fruity aroma in ripe
Pu'er tea13-14. This aroma profile represents a clear departure from the typical stale, woody, or
earthy notes of traditionally fermented ripe Pu'er tea. However, the underlying formation
mechanism of this desirable aroma during fermentation remains unknown.
Aroma is a primary determinant of tea quality. Multi-omics approaches have been instrumental
in decoding flavor formation mechanisms. Volatile metabolomics revealed the origins of honey-
like aroma in Xinyang black tea15. Yang et al. (2024) used non-targeted metabolomics and volatile
ARTICLE IN PRESS
ARTICLE IN PRESS

profiling to reveal how a citrus-floral aroma forms in green tea16. Metagenomic studies have
further identified key functional microorganisms. Members of Aspergillaceae and
Trichocomaceae were found to play important roles in shaping tea characteristics17. In Baijiu
fermentation, combined metagenomic and metabolomic analyses uncovered the synthesis pathway
of n-propanol. The study identified Lactobacillus panis as a key microbe involved18. Despite these
advances, the mechanisms of aroma formation in Pu'er tea fermentation remain poorly understood.
This is especially true for the development of the distinct floral–fruity character.
To fill this gap, we aimed to profile the dynamic changes of volatile and non-volatile metabolites
throughout fermentation and to characterize microbial community succession and functional shifts
using metagenomics. A further objective was to identify the key microorganisms and metabolic
pathways responsible for potentially important floral–fruity aroma compounds formation through
integrated multi-omics analysis and machine learning. We monitored environmental factors and
physicochemical parameters throughout the process. V olatile and non-volatile metabolites were
analyzed using headspace solid-phase microextraction combined with comprehensive two -
dimensional gas chromatography –time-of-flight mass spectrometry (HS -SPME-GC×GC-
TOFMS). and ultra-high performance liquid chromatography coupled with Q -Exactive mass
spectrometry (UHPLC-Q-Exactive/MS), respectively. Metagenomic sequencing was performed to
track microbial dynamics. A Random Forest model was applied to pinpoint microorganisms critical
to aroma development. This work aims to explore the metabolic and microbial dynamics
associated with floral–fruity aroma formation, and to provide a foundation for the industrial-scale
production of high-quality ripe Pu'er tea with desirable flavor characteristics.

Pu’er tea fermentation
Clear changes in leaf color were observed throughout fermentation. The leaves transitioned from
dark green to reddish -brown (Fig. 1A). Corresponding changes were also noted in the infusion
color. It evolved from yellowish-orange to reddish-brown. The taste profile shifted from an initially
bitter and astringent character to a mellow and full -bodied flavor. Notably, a distinct floral–fruity
aroma emerged during the mid-fermentation stage (Table 1).
The ambient temperature remain ed relatively stable throughout fermentation. The pile
temperature and ambient humidity exhibited an overall increasing trend as fermentation progressed.
However, considerable day -to-night fluctuations were observed, particularly in the later
fermentation stages (Fig. 1B). The moisture content of the tea leaves increased rapidly during the
initial fermentation phase. In the later stages, the water content gradually declined (Fig. 1C). The
pH of the tea infusion decreased sharply in the early phase. It then increased and eventually
stabilized, reaching a final value of 5.52 (Fig. 1D).
Dynamic changes of aroma compounds profile during fermentation
A total of 203 volatile compounds were identified across all samples using HS -SPME-GC×GC-
TOFMS. These compounds were classified into nine categories: 38 alcohols, 18 aldehydes, 16
esters, 39 alkenes, 24 aromatic hydrocarbons, 5 alkanes, 9 heterocyclic compounds, 39 ketones,
and 15 others (Table 2). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed clear separation among
samples from different fermentation stages. The first two principal components accounted for 48.5%
of the total variance. PC1 explained 30.8% of the variance and PC2 explained 17.7% (Fig. 2A).
Alcohols and esters increased during the mid -fermentation stage and then declined in the later
phase. The relative content of aromatic hydrocarbons increased progressively with fermentation
time (Fig. 2B and C). Among all detected volatiles, 164 compounds were common to all

fermentation stages. Twelve compounds were un iquely generated during fermentation, and one
compound was found exclusively in fully ripened Pu'er tea.
Relative odor activity values (ROA Vs) were calculated to evaluate the potential contribution of
volatile compounds to the overall aroma. Compounds with ROA V ≥ 1 were identified as potentially
important aroma-active compounds. Five compounds met this criterion (Table 3). trans-β-Ionone
exhibited the highest ROA V throughout fermentation. 1-Octen-3-ol and nonanal declined rapidly
during the early fermentation stage. Geraniol and phenylethyl alcohol increased initially and then
declined in the later stages (Fig. 2D).
To further evaluate these compounds, OPLS-DA was performed using the six fermentation time
points as class variables. The model was validated by 1000 permutation tests. A total of 99 volatile
compounds with VIP > 1 were identified as important variables driving the dynamic changes in
volatile profiles (Fig. S1). Among the five compounds identified by ROA V , geraniol, phenylethyl
alcohol, trans-β-ionone, and 1-octen-3-ol also exhibited VIP > 1; Nonanal showed a VIP < 1.
The temporal accumulation patterns of geraniol and phenylethyl alcohol aligned closely with
the development of floral –fruity aroma as determined by sensory evaluation. Both geraniol a nd
phenylethyl alcohol levels were positively correlated with tea moisture content (Fig. S2). The
decline of geraniol and phenylethyl alcohol in the later fermentation stages coincided with a
sensory shift from floral–fruity to aged and sweet notes (Table 1).
Dynamic profiling of non-volatile metabolites during fermentation
A total of 166 non -volatile compounds were identified using UHPLC -Q-Exactive/MS. These
compounds were classified into amino acids, alkaloids, N-ethyl-2-pyrrolidinone-substituted
flavanols (EPSFs), dimeric catechins, flavonols, flavone glycosides, flavanols, phenolic acids,
organic acids, and others (Fig. 3A). PCA revealed clear metabolic shifts across fermentation stages.

The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained 80.1% of the total variance. PC1
accounted for 61.9% of the variance and PC2 accounted for 18.2% (Fig. 3B). Among the 166

Microbial community dynamics and functional profiling
We performed principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on metagenomic data. This analysis
revealed clear temporal shifts in microbial community succession. The first two principal
components (PC1 and PC2) explained 62.44% of the total variance. PC1 explained 37.5% of the
total variance and PC2 explained 24.9% (Fig. 4A). The Shannon index decreased markedly during
the mid-fermentation stage, reaching its lowest value on day 14, with a slight recovery thereafter
(Fig. 4B). The Chao index increased as fermentation progressed (Fig. 4C).

At the phylum level, Ascomycota and Mucoromycota increased rapidly by day 7. Ascomycota
remained the dominant phylum from D7 to D21. From D28 to D32, a relative increase in
Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, and Arthropoda was noted (Fig. 4D). At the genus level, Aspergillus

Functional analysis based on the KEGG database indicated that microbial metabolism was
predominantly enriched in carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, energy metabolism,

Linking microbial communities with physicochemical properties and aroma formation
Mantel tests revealed that microbial community structure and encoded CAZymes were
significantly correlated with moisture content, pH, and three potentially important aroma -active

Phenylethyl alcohol was significantly positively correlated with the Shannon index (Mantel's p <
0.05; r > 0.25) (Fig. 5A).

β-ionone abu ndance. Rothia was primarily associated with phenylethyl alcohol abundance.
Bacteroides was mainly associated with nonanal levels. Geraniol content was primarily negatively
associated with several microbial taxa.

Discussion
This study provides an integrate d multi-omics view of aroma formation in inoculated Pu'er tea.
We tracked metabolic and microbial changes across fermentation. Several volatile compounds and
microbial genera were found to be associated with the floral–fruity aroma. The leaf color changed
from dark green to reddish-brown (Fig. 1A). The infusion color evolved from yellowish-orange to
reddish-brown. The taste shifted from bitter and astringent to mellow and full -bodied (Table 1).
These changes likely reflect the oxidati ve polymerization of tea polyphenols. This process forms
thearubigins and theabrownins19. It is driven by microbial extracellular enzymes3. The pH declined
sharply early in fermentation and then gradually recovered (Fig. 1D). This pattern suggests a two-
phase metabolic process. Active microbial growth first produces organic acids. These acids are
later consumed or converted. The moisture content trajectory shows a similar dynamic (Fig. 1C).
It reflects the combined effects of water addition, microbial water production, and evaporation20.
The volatile profiles changed markedly during fermentation. Alcohols and esters increased early
and then declined (Fig. 2B, C). This trend coincides with the pH decline. It suggests active
fermentative metabolism by the inoculated Saccharomyces cerevisiae and other microorganisms21.
Among 203 detected volatiles, four compounds were consistently prioritized by both ROA V and
VIP analyses (Table 3, Fig. S1). These compounds are phenylethyl alcohol, trans-β-ionone,
geraniol, and 1-octen-3-ol. They are potentially important aroma -active contributors 22.
Phenylethyl alcohol has a floral note and comes from aromatic amino acid metabolism (Fig. 2E).
Geraniol has a fruity note and comes from terpene biosynthesis. Their accumulation closely
tracked the emergence of floral–fruity aroma during middle fermentation23 (Table 1, Fig. 2D). This
supports their proposed roles in shaping the aroma. They were also positively associated with tea
moisture content (Fig. S2). This is consistent with previous reports that humidity promotes aroma

ARTICLE IN PRESS

formation24. However, the sample size was limited. The association remains preliminary.
A clear sensory shift occurred in the later stages. The dominant aroma changed from floral –
fruity to aged and sweet notes (Table 1). This coincided with the decline of geraniol and
phenylethyl alcohol (Fig. 2D). The shift likely reflects a change in the dominant metabolic regime.
In the middle stages, fungal metabolism drives biosynthesis. Terpene and aromatic amino acid
pathways are active. This leads to the accumulation of floral–fruity compounds25. As fermentation
proceeds, substrates become depleted. V olatilization and microbial transformation also occur.
These processes reduce the abundance of floral –fruity compounds. At the same time, oxidative
and degradative processes become more prominent. Lipid oxidation and polyphenol degradation
take place. They generate compounds associated with aged and sweet notes 26. This dynamic
interplay shapes the evolving aroma profile.
The non-volatile metabolome provided further evidence for these metabolic shifts. Most amino
acids declined during fermentation (Fig. 3A). They likely participate in Maillard reactions and the
Ehrlich pathway27,28. Phenylalanine also declined. It is the direct precursor of phenylethyl alcohol.
This provides a plausible substrate-level link. Flavanols, flavone glycosides, and dimeric catechins
were degraded. They are converted into theaflavins and theabrownins 19,29. These pigments
contribute to color and mellow taste. Lipids generally accumulated by the end of fermentation.
Lipids serve as precursors for 1 -octen-3-ol and nonanal. The decline of these two volatiles (Fig.
2D) may result from reduced enzymatic conversion rather than precursor limitation. Mfuzz
clustering identified coordinated metabolite changes (Fig. 3C, Fig. S4). Cluster 5 was positively
correlated with geraniol and phenylethyl alcohol. Cluster 4 was negatively correlated with trans-
β-ionone. These clusters represent distinct metabolic modules (Table S1). They are temporally
linked to volatile compound dynamics.

The microbial succession pattern, with Aspergillus dominating early and Rasamsonia becoming
dominant from D14 to D21 (Fig. 4E), aligns with previous findings for Qingzhuan tea
fermentation30. The larger dispersion among D28 replicates (Fig. 4A) may be attributed to inherent
biological variability during the transition from mid to late fermentation, when the microbial
community undergoes dynamic reorganization. The predominance of CE10 amo ng CAZyme
families (Fig. S5D) suggests that esterase-mediated transformations play a significant role during
fermentation.
It should be noted that moisture, pH, microbial succession, and metabolite profiles all changed
dynamically over the same fermentatio n period. Therefore, fermentation time may act as a
potential confounding factor in the observed correlations. Some of the associations reported here
may partially reflect co -variation with time rather than direct causal relationships. The genus
Sphingomonas, identified as a major predictor of 1-octen-3-ol and trans-β-ionone abundance (Fig.
5B, E), has been previously reported as a beneficial strain in Liupao tea fermentation 31. Rothia,
primarily associated with phenylethyl alcohol abundance (Fig. 5C), includes species known for
their metabolic versatility, such as Rothia aeria, which can degrade complex organic compounds32.
Bacteroides, mainly associated with nonanal levels (Fig. 5D), is recognized for its ability to break
down polysaccharides33.
It is noteworthy that the aroma -associated genera identified by random forest analysis were
predominantly bacterial (Fig. 5B–E), despite the inoculation of a synthetic fungal consortium. This
pattern may reflect competitive interactions between the inoculated fu ngi and indigenous
microorganisms. The rapid colonization and metabolic activity of the inoculated fungal strains
may have suppressed the growth of other indigenous fungi, thereby reducing overall fungal
community variation during fermentation34. Consequently, bacteria may have become the primary
ARTICLE IN PRESS
ARTICLE IN PRESS

drivers of the remaining microbial dynamics and played a more prominent role in shaping aroma
formation. Alternatively, the inoculated fungi may have facilitated aroma development indirectly
by secreting extracellu lar enzymes that release substrates subsequently utilized by indigenous
bacteria for the synthesis of volatile compounds35. Similar bacteria-driven aroma formation under
fungal inoculation conditions has been suggested in other fermented foods 36. Although the exact
nature of these fungal-bacterial interactions requires further investigation, these findings highlight
the potential importance of cross -kingdom microbial interactions in inoculated fermentation
systems.
In summary, this study presents a comprehensive multi-omics analysis of the dynamic changes
during Pu'er tea fermentation inoculated with a synthetic fungal consortium. The results revealed
that inoculation was associated with the development of a distinct floral –fruity aroma. Several
volatile compounds were identified as potentially important aroma -active contributors based on
combined ROA V and VIP analyses. Metagenomic analysis further indicated substantial shifts in
microbial community structure and function, which were correlated with changes i n
physicochemical parameters and volatile profiles. Random forest modeling identified specific
microbial genera predicted to be involved in the formation of these volatile compounds. These
findings provide insights into the metabolic and microbial dynamics associated with floral–fruity
aroma formation in inoculated Pu'er tea. This work offers a scientific foundation for future studies
aimed at targeted aroma validation and the development of tailored starter cultures. Future studies
employing accurate quant itation and sensory validation are needed to conclusively establish the
relationships between specific microorganisms and aroma formation.
Methods
Sample preparation and fermentation process

ARTICLE IN PRESS

Sun-dried green tea leaves from Lancang County, Yunnan Province, were used as the raw material.
A total of 2000 kg of leaves was thoroughly mixed. The leaves were then divided into three
replicate piles. We used a patented starter culture for inoculation. This starter contained
Saccharomyces cerevisiae (ZL200510010940.X), Rhizopus arrhizus (200710065787.X), and
Aspergillus niger (ZL200510010941.4). The three strains were mixed at a ratio of 2:1:2 (w/w).
The starter was inoculated at a rate of 0.1% (w/w) of the tea leaves. Water was added at 30% (w/w)
of the leaf weight. Each pile was stacked to a height of 1 meter in the fermentation room. The piles
were covered with a moist cloth. Solid -state fermentation proceeded at ambient temperature. We
recorded the temperature and humidity daily at 9:00 a.m., 3:00 p.m., and 9:30 p.m. Measurements
were taken at the pile center and inside the fermentation room.
The fermentation was carried out for a total duration of 32 days. Samples were collected at six
time points: D0 (initial), D7, D14, D21, D28, and D32. Sampling was conducted every 7 days
from D0 to D28. The final sampling was performed at D32, when fermentation was judged to be
complete based on sensory assessment and physicochemical parameters. We used a five -point
sampling method. Samples were collected from the upper, middle, and lower layers of the pile.
The collected samples were pooled and homogenized. Each pooled sample was divided into three
portions. One portion was stored at -80 ℃ for metagenomic sequencin g. A second portion was
used immediately to measure moisture content. The remaining portion was air -dried. This dried
material was reserved for subsequent pH measurements, sensory evaluation , and analysis of
quality components. After each sampling event, t he tea pile was turned and mixed. This ensured
uniform fermentation conditions until the process was complete.
Physicochemical analysis
The moisture content of fresh tea samples was determined gravimetrically. We dried 5 g of material

ARTICLE IN PRESS

at 105 ℃ until a constant weight was reached. This procedure followed the standard method GB/T
8304-2013. For pH measurement, 3 g of the dried sample was used. It was infused with 150 mL
of deionized water at 100 ℃ for 5 min. This step was conducted according to GB/T 23776-2018.
The resulting tea infusion was then filtered. After cooling to room temperature, the pH was
measured using a calibrated pH meter (PHS-25, Inesa Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai).
Sensory evaluation
Sensory evaluation was performed by a pane l of five trained assessors. The evaluation complied
with the standard method GB/T 23776-2018. Briefly, 3 g of tea sample was placed in a cylindrical
evaluation cup. Boiling water (150 mL) was added. The cup was covered and the sample was
infused for 2 min (first infusion). The infusion was then poured into an evaluation bowl for liquor
color assessment. The same leaves were subjected to a second infusion with fresh boiling water
(150 mL) for 5 min. This second infusion was used for aroma and taste evaluati on. The aroma
attributes evaluated included floral–fruity, aged, sweet, fresh, and sour notes. The taste attributes
included bitterness, astringency, smoothness, mellow -thickness, sweet aftertaste, and sourness.
Assessors independently recorded their descriptive evaluations, and the final descriptors represent
consensus attributes agreed upon by all assessors.
Analysis of aroma compounds
HS-SPME extraction
V olatile compounds were extracted using optimized headspace solid-phase microextraction (HS-
SPME). The extraction conditions were established through preliminary experiments. Exactly 1.0
g of uniformly ground tea powder (particle size < 0.5 mm) was weighed. The powder and 5 mL of
freshly boiled deionized water were placed in a 20 mL sealed glass vial. The vial contained a
magnetic stir bar. It was equilibrated at 60 ℃ for 3 min in a thermostatic oscillator. Continuous
ARTICLE IN PRESS
ARTICLE IN PRESS

agitation was maintained at 500 rpm. Extraction then proceeded at 60 ℃ for 60 min using a
conditioned Carboxen/poly dimethylsiloxane (CAR/PDMS) coated fiber (85 µm, 1 cm; Supelco,
Inc., Bellefonte, PA) fiber. After extraction, the fiber was manually desorbed in the GC injector.
Desorption was performed at 250 ℃ for 5 min in splitless mode. Following injection, the fiber
was baked out for 10 min at 260 ℃ to prevent carry-over effects36.
GC×GC-TOFMS analysis
Comprehensive two -dimensional gas chromatography coupled with time -of-flight mass
spectrometry (GC×GC -TOFMS) was performed. We used a LECO Pegasus 4D system. The
system was equipped with a quad-jet dual-stage thermal modulator and a liquid nitrogen cooling
system. Chromatographic separation was achieved using two columns. The primary column was
an Rxi-5Sil MS (30 m × 250 µm × 0.25 µm). The secondary column was an Rxi-17Sil MS (1.9 m
× 100 µm × 0.10 µm).
The primary oven temperature program was optimized as follows: initial temperature 50 ℃ held
for 2 min, then ramped to 265 ℃ at 8 ℃/min, and held for 5 min. The secondary oven was
programmed with a +5 ℃ offset relative to the primary oven. Its program was: 55 ℃ held for 2
min, then ramped to 270 ℃ at 8 ℃/min, and held for 5 min. The modulator temperature was
maintained 15 ℃ above the primary oven temperature throughout the run. The modulation period
was 5 s. This consisted of a 1.0 s hot pulse and a 1.5 s cold pulse.
High-purity helium (99.999%) was used as the carrier gas. The flow rate was maintained at 1.0
mL/min in constant flow mode. The split ratio for sample injection was 10:1. Mass spectrometry
was performed in electron impact mode at 70 eV . The ion source temperature was set to 220 ℃.
The transfer line temperature was 250 ℃. The mass acquisition range was m/z 33–500. Spectra
were acquired at a rate of 100 spectra per second. The detector voltage was set to 1390 V .

Automatic mass calibrat ion was performed daily using perfluorotributylamine as the calibration
standard36.
Qualitative and quantitative analysis
Raw data were processed using LECO Chroma TOF software (v4.51.6.0). The processing
parameters were optimized as follows : the baselines offset was set just above 1 ; the peak width
was set to 25 s in the first dimension and 0.1 s in the second dimension ; the signal -to-noise
threshold (S/N) for peak picking was set to ≥ 40.
Peak identification was conducted by comparing mass spectra with the NIST 2014 mass spectral
library. A similarity match score of ≥ 750 was required. We also used experimentally determined
Kovats retention indices (RIs). These RIs were calculated using a C8–C40 n-alkane series analyzed
under identical conditions. Tentative identifications required that experimental RIs deviate by less
than 20 units from reference values in the NIST database. For significantly altered volatile
compounds, definitive confirmation was achieved by comparing their retention times and mass
spectra with those of authentic standards analyzed under identical conditions. Compounds
confirmed in this manner are indicated in Table 2. The remaining compounds could not be
confirmed with authentic standards due to the limited commercial availability of certain reference
compounds, and are therefore considered tentatively identified.
Semi-quantitative analysis was performed using the peak area normalization method. For each
sample, the peak area of each identified volatile compound was divided by the total pe ak area of
all identified volatiles in that sample. The resulting values were expressed as a percentage of the
total volatile abundance . This normalization approach was adopted to compensate for minor
variations in sample amount and instrument sensitivity across runs, and to allow relative
comparisons of volatile profiles among different fermentation stages37.

ROA V analysis
Potentially important aroma-active compounds were screened based on relative odor activity
values (ROA V)38. The component with the hig hest contribution was assigned a ROA V of 100.
The ROA V for other compounds was calculated using the following equation:
ROAV = (Ci ÷ Cmax) × (Tmax ÷ Ti) × 100
where T i and T max represent the respective thresholds of each flavor component and the
corresponding thresholds of the most significant contributing component in μg/kg. C i and C max
indicate the relative content of each flavor component and the relative percentage of the la rgest
contributing component respectively. A ROA V value ≥ 1.0 indicates a higher contribution to
volatile flavor making it a potentially important aroma-active component.
Non-targeted metabolomic analysis
A non-targeted metabolomics approach was implemented for comprehensive metabolite profiling.
Tea powder (100 mg) with a particle size < 0.3 mm was accurately weighed. It was then transferred
to a 5 mL centrifuge tube. Extraction was performed using 3 mL of pre -chilled HPLC -grade
methanol. The methanol cont ained 0.1 mg/L lidocaine as an internal standard. Ultrasonic
extraction was carried out in an ice-water bath for 10 min (40 kHz, 500 W). The extract was then
centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 ℃. The supernatant was carefully transferred to a
volumetric flask. The residue was re -extracted using the same procedure. The combined
supernatants were adjusted to exactly 10 mL with methanol. After a second centrifugation at
12,000 × g for 5 min, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm nylon membrane. The filtrate
was then ready for UHPLC-MS analysis39.
Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system. The
system was equipped with a W ATERS ACQUITY HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 150 mm, 1.8 μm).
The column temperature was maint ained at 35 °C. The mobile phase consisted of two solvents.

ARTICLE IN PRESS

Solvent A was water containing 0.1% formic acid. Solvent B was acetonitrile containing 0.1%
formic acid. The flow rate was set to 300 μL/min. The gradient elution protocol was optimized as
follows: 0–3 min, 2% B; 3–15 min, 2%–100% B; 15–17 min, 100% B; 17–17.5 min, 100%–2%
B; 17.5–20 min, 2% B for column re-equilibration.
MS detection was performed on a Q-Exactive mass spectrometer. The instrument was equipped
with a HESI ion source. Data were acq uired in both positive and negative ionization modes. Key
MS parameters were as follows: spray voltage ±3.5 kV , capillary temperature 320 ℃, auxiliary
gas heater temperature 400 ℃, sheath gas flow 40 arbitrary units (arb), auxiliary gas flow 10 arb,
and mass scan range m/z 60–900. The resolution was set to 70,000. Data -dependent acquisition
(DDA) was employed. The top 5 ions were selected for MS/MS fragmentation. Normalized
collision energies were set to 20, 40, and 60 eV40.
Metabolite identification was performed through a multi-step process. Initial peak picking and
alignment were conducted using Compound Discoverer 3.3 software. Database searching was then
performed against HMDB, PubChem, and a custom tea metabolite database. The mass tolerance
was set to 5 ppm. The retention time tolerance was set to 0.2 min. Putative identifications were
confirmed by MS/MS fragmentation pattern matching when reference standards were available.
Quality assurance measures were implemented throughout the analysis. Pooled qu ality control
(QC) samples were analyzed every 10 injections. Data were retained only when QC samples
showed a relative standard deviation (RSD) of less than 30% for peak areas.
Metagenomic sequencing and analyses
Microbial genomic DNA was extracted from each frozen tea sample. We used 500 mg of sample
material and the FastDNA Spin Kit for Soil. An additional bead -beating step was included to
ensure efficient cell lysis. This step consisted of three cycles at 6.0 m/s for 45 s each. DNA quality

ARTICLE IN PRESS

was assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. DNA concentration was measured precisely using
both a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer.
Sequencing libraries were constructed from fragmented DNA. The f ragment size was
approximately 350 bp. We used the NEXTFLEX Rapid DNA-Seq Kit with dual indexing. Paired-
end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed on an Illumina N extSeq 2000 platform. This work
was carried out at Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).
Bioinformatic analysis followed an established pipeline 18. Raw sequencing reads were quality-
filtered using fastp (v0.20.0). The filtering parameters were as follows: -q 20 -u 40 -n 5 -l 50. This
process removed low-quality bases, adapter sequences, and reads containing more than 5% N bases.
De novo assembly was performed with MEGAHIT (v1.1.2). K-mer sizes ranging from 21 to 121
were used. Contigs longer than 300 bp were retained for subsequent analysis.
Open reading frames (ORFs) were predicted from the assembled contigs. We used Prodigal
(v2.6.3) in meta-mode. Genes with nucleic acid lengths of 100 bp or longer were translated into
amino acid sequences. A non-redundant gene catalog was constructed using CD-HIT (v4.8.1). The
clustering threshold was set to 90% nucleotide identity and 90% coverage. Gene abundance was
calculated by mapping clean reads back to the non -redundant gene catalog. This mapping was
performed with SOAPaligner (v2.21) using a 95% identity threshold.
Comprehensive functional annotation was performed using BLASTP alignment. The e -value
threshold was set to < 1e ⁻⁵. Alignments were conducted against three databases: the Non -
Redundant Protein Sequences (NR) database, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes
(KEGG) data base, and the Carbohydrate -Active enZYmes (CAZy) database. Taxonomic
assignment was achieved by parsing the BLAST results against the NR database. We used the
lowest common ancestor algorithm implemented in MEGAN.

Statistical analysis
All data are expresse d as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of triplicate measurements. All
statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.1). Analysis of variance (ANOV A) was
conducted, and Duncan's multiple range test was used for post -hoc comparisons of means.
Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Differential abundance analysis of metagenomic data
was conducted using LEfSe. The threshold for the linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score was
set to > 4.0. Principal component analysis (PCA), UpSet plot generati on, Orthogonal partial least
squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), and random forest analysis were also performed in R.
OPLS-DA and Random forest regression was implemented using the ropls and randomForest
package, respectively . Variable importance in p rojection (VIP) values was calculated, and
compounds with VIP > 1 were considered potentially important contributors to aroma
differentiation. Random forest model was built with 1000 trees to identify key microbial predictors
of aroma compound formation. Model performance was evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation.
