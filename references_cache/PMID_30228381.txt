Cross-exchange of B-vitamins underpins a mutualistic interaction between
Ostreococcus tauri and Dinoroseobacter shibae.

Ostreococcus tauri, a picoeukaryotic alga that contributes significantly to
primary production in oligotrophic waters, has a highly streamlined genome,
lacking the genetic capacity to grow without the vitamins thiamine (B1) and
cobalamin (B12). Here we demonstrate that the B12 and B1 auxotrophy of O. tauri
can be alleviated by co-culturing with a heterotrophic bacterial partner
Dinoroseobacter shibae, a member of the Rhodobacteraceae family of
alpha-proteobacteria, genera of which are frequently found associated with
marine algae. D. shibae lacks the complete pathway to synthesise three other
B-vitamins: niacin (B3), biotin (B7), and p-aminobenzoic acid (a precursor for
folate, B9), and the alga is in turn able to satisfy the reciprocal vitamin
requirements of its bacterial partner in a stable long-term co-culture.
Bioinformatics searches of 197 representative marine bacteria with sequenced
genomes identified just nine species that had a similar combination of traits
(ability to make vitamin B12, but missing one or more genes for niacin and
biotin biosynthesis enzymes), all of which were from the Rhodobacteraceae.
Further analysis of 70 species from this family revealed the majority encoded
the B12 pathway, but only half were able to make niacin, and fewer than 13%
biotin. These characteristics may have either contributed to or resulted from
the tendency of members of this lineage to adopt lifestyles in close association
with algae. This study provides a nuanced view of bacterial-phytoplankton
interactions, emphasising the complexity of the sources, sinks and dynamic
cycling between marine microbes of these important organic micronutrients.

Introduction 47
Photosynthetic picoeukaryotic ( PPE) algae are the main primary producers in many marine and 48
freshwater aquatic ecosys tems, playing a significant role in biogeochemical processes and food-web 49
dynamics (1, 2, 3). Prasinophytes, a paraphyletic gr oup of green algae, are a major group of PPE (4). 50
Members of the Ostreococcus genus in this class have cells typically <1 µm in diameter, making them 51
the smallest free-living eukaryotes described to date. Ostreococcus are found globally in a range of 52
conditions and habitats including the nutrient poor oligotrophic oceans, some of the largest and most 53
challenging biomes on Earth (5). This underscor es their important ecological role and raises 54
interesting questions about how the metabolism of these species equips them for survival. 55
Analysis of several Ostreococcus species reveals highly reduced ge nomes, although they retain 56
the majority of metabolic attributes essential for autotrophic growth, including a complete set of genes 57
for transport and assimilation of ammonium, nitrate and urea (6). However, like many other algae 58
their growth is dependent on the presence of certain organic micronutrients in the environment, 59
specifically thiamine (vitamin B 1) (7) and cobalamin (vitamin B 12) (8), both of which are required as 60
cofactors for enzymes involved in central metabolism. Genomic evidence revealed that O. tauri has 61
only a partial biosynthetic pathway for thiamine, su ggesting it may have lost its ability to synthesise 62
this cofactor de novo (9, 10). The B 12-biosynthetic pathway, comprising over 20 enzyme-catalysed 63
steps, is found only in certain prokaryotes (11). The requirement for this vitamin by B 12-dependent 64
algae is as a cofactor for the essential B 12-dependent enzyme, methionine synthase (METH), rather 65
than the inability to synthesise the vitamin. Those species that can grow independently of an external 66
supply of B 12 encode an alternative methionine syntha se enzyme (METE) that does not use B 12 as a 67
cofactor (8, 12). O. tauri encodes METH but not METE, and thus requires vitamin B12 for growth (8). 68
Algae may acquire vitamins in their natura l environment via different routes. Seasonal 69
upwelling of deep nutrient-rich water may provide a source for some coastal strains (13). However, 70
some vitamins are photolabile and would not persist for long in the water column. Moreover, using a 71
sensitive analytical method Sanudo-Wilhelmy et al (2012) found that ambient concentrations along 72
the coast of California were sign ificantly lower than needed for the growth of vitamin-dependent 73
4

algae such as O. tauri , and were below the detection threshol d in some regions (14). Addition of 74
vitamin B 12 (together with Fe and N) enhanced chlorophyll a levels threefold (beyond stimulating 75
effects of adding just Fe + N) in the eastern bounda ry of the South Atlantic Gyre (15). This highlights 76
the crucial role of this compound in controlling ph ytoplankton growth in natural marine microbial 77
communities, and demonstrates that the vitamin auxotrophy of O. tauri is representative of the natural 78
world, rather than a particularity of this species. 79
Although small populations may persist under limiting micronutrient conditions, many PPEs 80
including Ostreococcus periodically form characteristic blooms, implying that levels of vitamins must 81
increase. Prokaryotes are the ultimate and only source of vitamin B 12 (11). However, structural 82
diversity in the forms of B 12 produced by different prokaryote ta xa has important implications in 83
terms of their bioavailability to eukaryote auxot rophs. In particular, evidence indicates that B 12-84
synthesising cyanobacteria produce a variant, pseudoco balamin, that is considerably less bioavailable 85
to eukaryotic algae (16). This suggests cobalamin-producing heterotrophic bacteria and archaea are 86
likely to produce the majority of the cofactor that is bio-available for algae (12, 17). Further, certain 87
bacterial phyla are more likely to be found associated with algae. In an analysis of over 40 different 88
species of both macro and microalgae, six phyla (Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, 89
Actinobacteria, Verrucomicrobia and Planctomycetes) accounted for the majority of bacteria (18), and 90
both alpha- and gamma-Proteobacteria are almost invariably found in studies of algal-associated 91
microbiomes (19). In particular, species from the family Rhodobacteraceae (20) have been shown to 92
deliver vitamins and other metabolites to marine algae. For example, Dinoroseobacter shibae, a 93
cosmopolitan obligately aerobic bact erium, originally isolated as a symbiont to cultured marine 94
dinoflagellates (21), could supply thiamine and B 12 to its hosts in exchange for a source of fixed 95
carbon (22). Similarly, Ruegeria pomeroyi supported the B12 requirements of the diatom Thalassiosira 96
pseudonana in exchange for organosulphur compounds (23). Analysis of environmental isolates of 97
another diatom, Pseudo-nitzschia multiseries, showed association with a number of bacteria one of 98
which, a Sulfitobacter species, promoted algal cell division by providing indole acetic acid (24). 99
The hypothesis of mutualistic exchange of nutri ents between microorganisms is challenging to 100
test in the field. Most rely on correlations between co-existing species (25, 26), because in the absence 101
5

of tight physical associations, which are not nece ssary for mutualism, nutrient flux is difficult to 102
deduce even using the most advanced methodologies (27). In light of this, simplified laboratory-based 103
systems are invaluable for the study of nutrient cycling between microbes (28), and may provide 104
important clues for our understanding of biogeochemi cal cycling of nutrients in natural systems. In 105
this study, we established a stable co-culture between O. tauri and D. shibae, organisms that are 106
reported to co-exist in marine microbial assemblage s, such as in the North Pacific subtropical gyre 107
(29). Using this system in the laboratory, we exam ined the nature of the metabolic exchange, probing 108
the dynamics and stability of the interaction over successive generations. Our findings demonstrate 109
that there is a complex two-way exchange of B v itamins that underpins this stable mutualistic 110
relationship. 111

Bioinformatics Analysis 170
Sequence similarity searches were performed to assess the presence of vitamin biosynthesis genes in 171
different organisms. Enzymes required for the biosyn thesis of each vitamin were identified from a 172
literature search and reference pathways from the Kyoto Ency clopedia of Genes and Genomes 173
(KEGG) (34). Validated reference protein sequences for enzymes of vitamin biosynthesis were 174
identified from the Universal Protei n Resource (UniProt) archive. BLASTP (35) was used to search 175
for each of these enzymes in the O. tauri (GenBank ID: GCA_000214015.1) and D. shibae (GenBank 176
ID: GCA_000018145.1) genome assembly hosted at the National Centre for Biotechnology 177
Information database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), using the online BLAST server at the European 178
Bioinformatics Institute (EMBL-EBI; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/sss/ncbiblast/). Default parameters 179
including a word size of 3 and the BLOSUM62 scoring matrix were used for all searches. Low 180
complexity regions were also filtered out. Only hits with an expectation coefficient of less than 1 × e -181
15 were taken through for extended analysis. The protein sequences of the putative biosynthesis 182
enzymes were aligned using the ClustalW algorithm (36) with the query protein sequences using the 183

Complementary B vitamin synthesis capabilities of O. tauri and D. shibae revealed by in silico 192
pathway analyses 193
Figure 1a shows the growth requirements of O. tauri and D. shibae in minimal medium with different 194
supplements. O. tauri requires both vitamins B 12 and B 1 (7, 8), as cofactors for cobalamin-dependent 195
methionine synthase (B 12) and several enzymes of intermed iary carbon metabolism including 196
transketolase (B1). D. shibae DFL-12 on the other hand encodes the genes for the biosynthesis of B 12 197
and B 1 (22), and can grow without either supplement. However, D. shibae is known to need 198
exogenous sources of three other B vitamins: biotin (vitamin B7), niacin (B3) and p-aminobenzoic acid 199
a precursor for folate (B9) biosynthesis, whereas O. tauri does not require these molecules for growth. 200
All three of these compounds have vital roles in ce ntral metabolism: biotin is a co-factor for enzymes 201
necessary for essential car boxylation, transcarboxylation and de carboxylation reactions, folate is 202
required for enzymes of 1-carbon metabolism, and niacin is a precursor to the hydrogen carriers 203
NAD+ & NADP +, redox carriers used ubiquitously within the cell (reviewed in 37). Despite these 204
fundamental roles, the exact genetic basis underlying the auxotrophy of D. shibae for these vitamins is 205
currently unknown. We therefore carried out a detailed assessment of the genomes of the two 206
organisms to investigate this, and whether they may be able to support one another’s growth in co-207
culture. 208
The possible biosynthetic pathways of biotin, niacin and pABA are shown in Figure 1b. In 209
bacteria, biotin is synthesised from its precursor molecule pimeloyl-coA by four enzymes: 8-amino-7-210
oxononanoate synthase (KAPAS, BioF) (38), 7,8-diamino-pelargonic acid aminotransferase (DAPAS, 211
BioA) (39), dethiobiotin synthase (BioD) (40), and finally biotin synthase (BioB) (41), which 212
converts dethiobiotin into biotin. In plants and so me algae, the reactions of BioA and BioD can be 213
performed by a bi-functional enzyme, known as BIO1 (42). A BLASTP search for biotin synthesis 214
enzymes in O. tauri identified a significant hit for this bi-functional enzyme, alongside the 215
10

biosynthesis proteins BIOF and BIOB (green dots, Figure 1a; Table S1) supporting the physiological 216
data. In contrast, the genome for D. shibae contains putative genes for only two of the four required 217
enzymes: BioF and BioA (two putative hits were found for BioA) (blue dots, Figure 1b; Table S2). 218
No orthologues of genes for BioD or BioB were identified, explaining the inability of D. shibae to 219
grow without an exogenous source of biotin. 220
There are two biosynthetic routes for synthesis of niacin: i) from anthranilate produced from 221
the degradation of tryptophan (as in animals), or ii) de novo synthesis from aspartate (characteristic of 222
plants). Bacteria have been shown to use both pathways (43). O. tauri does not encode biosynthesis 223
genes from the tryptophan degradation pathway, but has all the genes required to produce 224
nicotinamide mononucleotide through the aspartate pathway, using L-aspartate oxidase (NADB), 225
quinolinate synthase (NADA) and quinolinic acid phosphoribosyl transferase (NADC) (Figure 1b and 226
supporting material in Table S3). Whilst D. shibae has a significant hit for proteins related to aspartate 227
dehydrogenase (nadX, which is functionally equiva lent to NADB (44)) and kynureninase (KYNU) 228
(Table S4), we found no convincing orthologues for any other enzymes necessary for niacin 229
biosynthesis. The lack of biosynthesis genes for the full aspartate or tryptophan pathway in D. shibae 230
corroborates previous physiological evidence that D. shibae cannot make niacin itself (22), and thus 231
requires an exogenous supply of this essential molecule (Figure 1b). 232
The compound p-aminobenzoic acid, an intermediate in the synthesis of folate and its 233
bioactive forms, is produced from chorismate (45) . In bacteria, two enzymes are required for this 234
process. The first, aminodeoxychorismate synthase (ADCS) is made up of two protein sub-units, 235
PabA and PabB (46). Sub-unit II of the ADCS comple x (PabA) acts as a glutamine amidotransferase, 236
transferring the amino group from glutamine to sub-unit I (PabB), which is then used to aminate the 237
chorismate molecule (46). Arabidopsis thaliana, some yeasts, and certain algae have been shown to 238
encode a bi-functional enzyme (PAB-AB) that performs both of these steps (47). In both bacteria and 239
plants, the final enzyme in the pathway is 4-amino-4-deoxychorismate lyase (ADCL, PabC) that 240
catalyses the reversible conversion of aminodeoxychorismate into p-aminobenzoic acid and pyruvate 241
(48). O. tauri has significant hits for PAB-AB and PABC (F igure 1b and supporting material in Table 242
S5), suggesting it is able to synthesise p-aminobenzoic acid. Incidenta lly, separate PABA and PABB 243
11

homologues were also identified. D. shibae also encodes pabA and pabB genes (Table S6). 244
Additionally, a possible pabC gene was identified, but this was annotated as a D-alanine 245
aminotransferase (D-AAT). ADCL and D-AAT are both class IV pyridoxal 5'-phosphate (PLP) 246
dependent enzymes and share similar domain architecture. Using the NCBI conserved domain search 247
function (49), we looked at the affiliation of our hit to reference ADCL and D-AAT sequences (Figure 248
S3). The D. shibae gene (YP_001534484.1, highlighted in red) clusters together with other D-AAT 249
enzymes (green) including that from another member of the Rhodobacteraceae family, Rhodobacter 250
sphaeroides, rather than with ADCL-like enzymes (blue). These results indicate this gene is thus more 251
likely to encode a D-AAT. The inco mplete biosynthesis pathway of D. shibae for p-aminobenzoic 252
acid thus corroborates the observation that this bacterium requires an exogenous source of this 253
molecule for growth. 254
In the present study we did not investigate the secretory pathways, which are associated with 255
the uptake and potential release of these vitamins into the environment. For algal species, vitamin 256
transporters have not been identif ied conclusively so bioinformatics characterisation is not possible. 257
This is with the exception of CBA1, a protein im portant for cobalamin upta ke (50). However, this 258
does not appear to be encoded by O. tauri. Our work therefore only addresses the question of whether 259
the species are capable of metabolic synthesis and modes of exchange – whether passive (such as for 260
example by viral lysis) or active (through transport proteins), remains unknown. 261
In summary, using curated sequences as queries on the O. tauri genome, complete 262
biosynthetic pathways for p-aminobenzoic acid, niacin and biotin were identified, demonstrating 263
potential biosynthetic capacity for eac h of these metabolites. In contrast, D. shibae lacks a complete 264
biosynthetic pathway for all three of these vitamins. 265

To explore further the complementary vitamin synthesis capabilities of O. tauri and D. shibae , we 268
investigated their ability to grow together in the absence of exogenous B-vitamins. This required an 269
axenic culture of both organisms. All cultures of O. tauri available from culture collections are 270
maintained as uni-algal, with one or more contaminating bacteria present. Treatment of O. tauri OTH 271

95 with a cocktail of antibiotics over several su bcultures reduced the load considerably, but one 272
bacterium, Zeaxanthibacterium sp. ( Flavobacteriaceae), was always present. Although 273
supplementation with B1 & B12 was still required, indicating that it was not providing the vitamins to 274
O. tauri, we nonetheless wanted to separate the algal and bacterial cells to avoid confounding factors. 275
Since these are essentially the same dimensions (~1 µm in diameter), we explored the possibility of 276
using fluorescence activated cell sorting (FACS). Initia lly, cells were gated on their size and shape, 277
and then gated further based on their pulse width/pulse area, as described in Methods. This process 278
could distinguish between single cells and doublets or triplets, indicative of bacteria attached to algae. 279
Once settings were established that allowed only si ngle cells to flow through the channel, chlorophyll 280
fluorescence was used to separate algal cells from ba cteria (Figure S2). Cells were treated again with 281
antibiotics for 14 days, following which no bacteria were detected by light microscopy or growth on 282
marine broth plates. 283
Given the non-overlapping B- vitamin dependencies of O. tauri and D. shibae , we then 284
investigated whether a stable co-culture of the two organisms could be established in medium 285
containing none of the five B vitamins, to instig ate the transfer of complementary micronutrients. 286
Cultures were maintained for three sub-cultures (each of 21-25 day duration) to ensure that any rescue 287
effect was long-term and stable. At sta tionary phase, total cell numbers for both O. tauri and D. 288
shibae were taken and compared to control mono-cu ltures grown in parallel (Figure 2). Maximal 289
growth was evident when O. tauri and D. shibae were grown in monoculture with all the necessary B 290
vitamins. However, a si gnificant increase in O. tauri cell density was observed when the alga was 291
grown in co-culture with D. shibae (Figure 2a, white bars) in medium lacking thiamine, B 12 or all B-292
vitamins compared with the respective axenic mono cultures (grey bars) (Student’s t-test p<0.05), 293
although the rescue effect varied in magnitude. We found that whilst D. shibae was able to satisfy the 294
requirements of O. tauri for B 12, the rescue effect was weaker when thiamine was omitted, with the 295

medium. In turn, co-culturing with O. tauri satisfied the requirements of D. shibae for each of niacin, 297
biotin and p-aminobenzoic acid (Figure 2b) , with statistically signific ant differences observed 298
compared to growth in monoculture under the equivalent deficiency profiles (Student’s t-test P<0.05 299

The previous experiment indicated that growth yields of both O. tauri (OTH95) and D. shibae DFL-306
12 were lower in co-culture compared to mono-c ultures. The observation that carrying capacities of 307
organisms engaged in mutualisms are different to mono-culture is common, and has been termed 308
regulation (51). This is particularly well docume nted for plants in association with rhizobial 309
symbionts, where the balance of host to mutualist is carefully maintained, and allows a carefully 310
maintained exchange of resources (e.g. 52). For algal and bacterial systems in mutualism regulation 311
has been observed (53) but often the signalling mechanism remains unclear (reviewed in 54). 312
To determine whether O. tauri and D. shibae actively regulate each other’s growth, we inoculated the 313
two organisms at different relative proportio ns into minimal medium without niacin , p-aminobenzoic 314
acid or B 12; both thiamine and biotin were included to enable reasonable growth rates and minimise 315
the time needed for each experiment (omission of these vitamins had the greatest inhibitory effect on 316
O. tauri and D. shibae cell density in co-culture, respectively (Figure 2)). For those cultures with 317
higher initial algal numbers, the starting inocula tion density for the algae was set to ~2×10 6 cells per 318
ml, with bacterial numbers adjusted accordingly to obtain the different ratios. When bacteria were 319

of the cultures were monitored over time by cell counting as described in the Methods. We found that 321
regardless of starting numbers of either algal or b acterial cells, after 21-25 days in co-culture a ~1:1 322

of algae to bacteria, whereas 25 days were required for O. tauri cells to reach parity from an initial 325

The co-culture between O. tauri (OTH95) and D. shibae DFL-12 demonstrates a novel role for the 346
vitamin cofactors niacin, biotin and p-aminobenzoic acid in nutrient exchange. To assess the broader 347
relevance of this observation, we investigated wh ether the potential to form mutualistic interactions 348
with eukaryotic marine algae based on recipr ocal B vitamin exchanges is a likely common 349
characteristic of algae-associated bacteria. A survey of reports in the literature of bacteria found 350
associated with marine algae from 7 different loca tions identified a total of 28 genera (Table S8). 351
Strikingly, in each location species of the Rhodobacteraceae family (order Rhodobacterales) were 352
found (asterisked in Table S8). We chose 70 specie s from this family that had sequenced genomes, 353
and analysed the gene complement for the presence of the 63 genes involved in the biosynthesis of 354
15

cobalamin, niacin and biotin, using our previously established bioinformatics approach that allows 355
rapid searching of complete bacterial genomes for la rge numbers of genes (16). We were not able to 356
use this method to search for genes encoding pABA biosynthesis enzymes because the close 357
similarity between sequences for other enzymes requires manual inspection of the hits to verify their 358
identity. The results revealed that that the majority (87%) of the species possess the genes required for 359
vitamin B 12 synthesis, whereas those for niacin were found in half, and only 9 (13%) appeared to 360
encode the entire biotin biosynthetic pathway (Supple mentary dataset S1). In total, 29 species (41%) 361
shared the genotype of D. shibae, namely the ability to synthesis vitamin B12 but not biotin or niacin. 362
To assess whether the Rhodobacteraceae exhibit a disproportionately high frequency of this 363
trait compared to other bacteria, we extended the search more generally, using the GOLD database 364
(55), which characterises species from known loca tions. A total of 197 verified marine eubacteria 365
from 39 different orders, including 19 members of the Rhodobacteraceae family, were searched for 366
the presence or absence of these 63 genes (Suppl ementary dataset S2). Only 9 species capable of 367
producing B 12 and at the same time lacking the capacity to synthesise both biotin and niacin were 368
identified, all of which were Rhodobacteraceae. For the remaining 178 species from non-369
Rhodobacteraceae families, 149 (84%) encoded the complete niacin pathway, and 123 (69%) the 370
entire biotin pathway, much higher percentages than for the Rhodobacteraceae (37% and 16%). Thus, 371
using two independent datasets combined with info rmation from previous studies, we find that, in 372
contrast to marine bacteria generally, bacteria of the Rhodobacteraceae family, which includes D. 373
shibae, are characterised by the ability to synthesise vitamin B 12, but not B 3 or B 7 (Figure 5). 374
Members of this family are also commonly found together with eukaryotic marine algae (19, 54). We 375
therefore find it plausible that they could supply algae with their required cobalamin, whilst receiving 376
niacin and biotin in return. 377

Discussion 379
 380
The notion that photosynthetic microorganisms are at the bottom of the food web in aquatic 381
communities, supplying metabolites and energy to heterotrophic bacteria and macro-organisms in 382
16

progressive trophic levels is out-dated (56, 57). In stead, studies on aquatic microbial interactions are 383
unveiling a suite of different lifestyles and cross-feeding interactions at the heart of these complex 384
communities. Vitamins have emerged as important pl ayers in associations between aquatic microbes 385
(e.g. 23, 24, 56, 57), since by definition they are required obligately by auxotrophs for survival, so the 386
demands for them must be met by other species capable of their biosynthesis, even if not directly (58). 387
The findings presented here are the first physio logical demonstration of a bilateral B-vitamin 388
exchange between aquatic microorganisms. Perhap s particularly important is our finding that O. tauri 389
was able to provide D. shibae with niacin, the vitamin for which D. shibae exhibited the highest 390
dependency. Very few studies have attempted to determine the concentration of niacin in oceanic 391
environments: at the time of writing the only available paper is from half a century ago (59) and states 392
niacin was undetectable in the Alaskan surface waters sampled. Our in silico survey found 20% of 393
marine bacteria sampled do not have the genetic capacity to synthesise it de novo (Supplementary 394
Dataset S2), but niacin auxotrophy is significantly enriched in the Rhodobacteracaeae (35/70 species 395
sampled), whilst in contrast these ar e more likely to synthesise vitamin B 12 than marine bacteria 396
generally. Two Roseobacter species, R. litoralis and R. denitrificans, have previously been shown 397
experimentally to require an exogenous source of niacin and biotin for growth (22). These bacteria 398
were originally isolated from the surface of green seaweeds, and were not found in open waters. More 399
recently, another member of the Rhodobacteraceae (20), a Sulfitobacter species named SA11, was 400
identified as a key symbiont with the toxic bloom forming alga Pseudo-nitzschia (24). Previous 401
studies have also identified niacin auxotrophy in ho st-associated bacteria, including in pathogenic and 402
enteric bacteria (60). For example, Shigella flexneri , the pathogen responsible for shigellosis in 403
primates, is a niacin auxotroph, having lost the nadA and nadB genes responsible for synthesis of 404
quinolinate from aspartate, a precursor to niacin ( 61). The central role played by the bioactive forms 405
of niacin as redox carriers and signalling molecu les (62) makes it a crucial micronutrient for cell 406
growth and survival in all organisms. The inabilit y of some microbes to synthesise it is therefore 407
perhaps surprising, especially considering that it requires just 3 enzymatic steps from central 408
metabolites (37). It is tempting to conclude that ni acin auxotrophy in enteric bacteria, commensalists 409
17

and pathogens, as well as in those found associated with algae, may be a common type of genetic 410
streamlining in bacteria that live closely with other organisms. 411
The rescue effect of the mutualistic sy mbiosis that we describe here between O. tauri and D. 412
shibae is stable over multiple generations for both individual B-vitamins and combinations thereof 413
(Figures 2 and 4). D. shibae was found to support the bulk of the B 12 requirement for growth of O. 414
tauri in co-culture but only a small proportion of its thiamine requirement (Fig ure 2a). This matches 415
the known Ks values of thiamine for several algal species, which have been reported to be up to four 416
orders of magnitude higher than those for B 12 (63). Our findings indicate that thiamine is the most 417
important regulator of O. tauri growth. For D. shibae, biotin limitation had the greatest effect on 418
bacterial cell density in co-culture. We also investigated whether the interaction between the algae and 419
bacteria exhibited regulation of population size, a phenomenon observed both in terrestrial systems 420
and aquatic symbioses (e.g. 54, 64). Our findings suggest that regulation between D. shibae and O 421
tauri is likely, as the ratio of bacterial to algal cells reproducibly stabilises at ~ 1:1 during the 422
exponential phase of growth, regardless of initial st arting cell concentrations (Figure 3a). This is a 423
higher proportion of bacterial cells than the 1: 30 measured in another co-culture, between L. rostrata 424
and M. loti (53), and may reflect the fact that L. rostrata is much larger cell than M. loti, whereas O. 425
tauri and D. shibae are essentially the same size. However, as for the L. rostrata/M. loti system the 426
1:1 equilibrium in cell numbers was only maintained when the medium was minimal, without any B 427
vitamins or an exogenous source of fixed carbon. Addition of these micronutrients favoured the 428
growth of both algae and bacteria, disrupting th eir dependency on one another and destabilising the 429
1:1 ratio observed (Figure 3b). This indicates vitamin (and carbo n) availability are partially 430
responsible for the observed regulatory effect. Thia mine and biotin may be particularly important in 431
this regard, given that omission of these v itamins had the greatest inhibitory effect on O. tauri and D. 432
shibae cell density in co-culture, respectively (Figur e 2). However, since bacterial and algal cell 433
density in co-culture is reduced even in the presence of all B vitamins (Figure 2) other factors must be 434
important too. For instance, it remains possible th at the equilibrium observed is reached due to 435
competition of the cells for other nutrients in the medium. This can be excluded by further studies 436
under chemostat or semi-continuous batch culturing, which replenish the demand for macronutrients. 437
18

However, factors beyond nutrient limitation may c ontrol algal-bacterial cell densities too. Other work 438
(65) observed “Jekyll and Hyde” dynamics regulate d algal cell density in co-cultures of the marine 439
alga Emiliania hyxleyi and bacterium Phaeobacter gallaeciensis . In this instance, population 440
regulation occurs via production of the algicidal compound p-coumaric acid. Further experiments into 441
possible signalling between the cells and quorum se nsing is required to c onfirm the presence of 442
regulation in this interaction. 443
Recent evidence has highlighted further complex exchange between algae and bacteria. Several 444
marine bacteria have been demonstrated to synthesise auxins such as indole acetic acid (IAA), which 445
have growth-promoting roles for microalgae (24, 65). Moreover, algal- derived tryptophan may 446
provide the precursor for IAA synthesis. Together with our results, these studies identify a remarkably 447
complex and important aspect of marine microbial ecology that we are only beginning to understand. 448
By providing the genetic basis underlying the physiological observations, a portion of the “meta-449
metabolome” of two organisms in symbiosis (repre senting the combined meta bolic capabilities of the 450
interacting species) can be visualis ed with respect to B-vitamins, highlighting the nodes of exchange 451
(Figure 6). Without considering co-limitation of macronutrients as a factor, when both these 452
organisms are present in oligotrophic waters containing little or no B-vitamins free in solution they 453
could support each other’s growth. Our experiment s add to accumulating evidence of generalised 454
“niche-fitting” (66) betw een microbial species Chlamydomonas reinhardtii and the budding yeast 455
Saccharomyces cerevisiae were shown to form complex physiological structures in the laboratory, 456
providing each other with limiting nutrients (67). Similarly, here, O. tauri could provide and receive 457
B-vitamins from D. shibae . It should be mentioned that the formation of non-specific mutualisms 458
between organisms in co-limiting conditions is not a given; for example, complementary auxotrophs 459
of S. cerevisiae deficient in various aspects of amino acid and nucleotide metabolism regularly fail to 460
rescue each other upon co-culturing (68) even though they are the same species. Consequently, our 461
study makes an important contribution to the study of microbial interactions by providing an example 462
where metabolic complementarity is achieved between partners from different branches of life. The 463
fact that this is mediated by exchange of B vita mins highlights the significance of these compounds, 464
and expands the repertoire of nutrients involved in interactions in natural ecosystems. 465
19

Evidence is accumulating to demonstrate a ra nge of complex ecological interactions within 466
microbial communities, so it is important to cons ider how such interactions may arise in an 467
environment such as the ocean, where species assemblages are likely to be transient due to the fluid 468
and dynamic medium. It remains an open questio n whether these symbiotic interactions are the 469
product of evolutionary history, which presumes a number of generations of co-occurrence with 470
metabolic exchange as the outcome rather than the driver for co-existence, or the opposite (69). Two 471
hypotheses for the evolution of mu tualism within microbes have been proposed, which explore these 472
contrasting scenarios: the Black Queen Hypothesis (70) and the Forager-to-Farmer model (57). The 473
identification and characterization of microbial inter actions at the genetic and physiological levels, as 474
described here, will provide data to enable the hypotheses to be tested and refined. 475
