Soils are often treated as chemically defined reactors, yet the physical
architecture of minerals, organic inputs, and microbiomes jointly shapes where
and how carbon turns over. We used a factorial microcosm experiment to test how
clay mineralogy (fibrous palygorskite vs. swelling bentonite), clay content
(0-20%), calcium carbonate (7.5-15%), litter quality (recalcitrant wheat vs.
labile alfalfa), and microbiome origin (native soil vs. a synthetic
fungi + bacteria consortium) interact to control respiration kinetics and
microbial biomass carbon over 90 days. Clay type and amount acted as primary
filters: increasing clay generally raised cumulative mineralized C, but
palygorskite produced higher C₀, faster mineralization (higher k or lower t₀,
steeper n), and stronger late-stage biomass recovery than bentonite, indicating
a colonizable, catalytic habitat rather than a purely protective matrix. Litter
chemistry modulated this filter: N-rich alfalfa shifted the system toward
facilitation, with rapid, high-amplitude mineralization and large biomass peaks,
whereas high-C:N wheat slowed mineralization, increased sensitivity to clay
content, and emphasized protection and diffusion limitation. Microbiome
composition added a third control: native communities generated higher
cumulative C loss but lower rate constants, while the synthetic consortium drove
faster mineralization and higher biomass on palygorskite. Calcium carbonate
acted as a tuner, enhancing C₀, k and biomass in wheat systems and shifting
mineralization timing in alfalfa systems. Together, these results support a
hierarchical framework where mineral pore architecture sets the habitat filter,
litter quality and microbial traits determine its exploitation, and Ca
availability adjusts the balance between facilitation and protection.

Soils are often treated as chemically defined reactors, yet the physical architecture of minerals, organic inputs, and microbiomes jointly shapes where and how carbon turns over. We used a factorial microcosm experiment to test how clay mineralogy (fibrous palygorskite vs. swelling bentonite), clay content (0–20%), calcium carbonate (7.5–15%), litter quality (recalcitrant wheat vs. labile alfalfa), and microbiome origin (native soil vs. a synthetic fungi + bacteria consortium) interact to control respiration kinetics and microbial biomass carbon over 90 days. Clay type and amount acted as primary filters: increasing clay generally raised cumulative mineralized C, but palygorskite produced higher C₀, faster mineralization (higher k or lower t₀, steeper n), and stronger late-stage biomass recovery than bentonite, indicating a colonizable, catalytic habitat rather than a purely protective matrix. Litter chemistry modulated this filter: N-rich alfalfa shifted the system toward facilitation, with rapid, high-amplitude mineralization and large biomass peaks, whereas high-C:N wheat slowed mineralization, increased sensitivity to clay content, and emphasized protection and diffusion limitation. Microbiome composition added a third control: native communities generated higher cumulative C loss but lower rate constants, while the synthetic consortium drove faster mineralization and higher biomass on palygorskite. Calcium carbonate acted as a tuner, enhancing C₀, k and biomass in wheat systems and shifting mineralization timing in alfalfa systems. Together, these results support a hierarchical framework where mineral pore architecture sets the habitat filter, litter quality and microbial traits determine its exploitation, and Ca availability adjusts the balance between facilitation and protection. Soil respiration kinetics and microbial biomass dynamics are principally governed by intricate interactions among soil mineralogy, organic substrate quality, microbial community composition, and soil chemical environment. Clay minerals-key constituents of soil mineralogy-exert multifaceted controls on organic matter decomposition through their distinctive physicochemical properties such as cation exchange capacity, surface area, porosity, and water retention capacity. These mineralogical traits dictate the sorption and physical protection of organic substrates, shape microbial habitats, and regulate enzymatic access and activity, ultimately influencing carbon mineralization rates 7 – 10 . In particular, bentonite, a smectitic clay characterized by a tri-octahedral layered structure with high swelling potential and strong water retention, often restricts oxygen diffusion and limits microbial colonization by forming dense microenvironments. In contrast, palygorskite, with its fibrous morphology, high porosity, and large specific surface area, promotes substrate accessibility and microbial proliferation through enhanced adsorption and microhabitat heterogeneity 8 , 11 . Clay minerals structure microbial microhabitats and control enzyme access to organic substrates, with fibrous palygorskite promoting microbial proliferation and cellulase activity compared to swelling bentonites that limit O2 diffusion and colonization 12 , 13 . Beyond soil mineralogy, substrate quality profoundly affects decomposition dynamics. Wheat litter, with its elevated carbon content, high carbon-to-nitrogen ratio, and lignified fiber structure, presents a chemically recalcitrant and physically resistant substrate that decomposes slowly and limits microbial nutrient availability. Conversely, alfalfa litter, characterized by lower carbon concentrations, lower C: N ratios, and higher nitrogen content, is more labile and nutrient-rich, fostering rapid microbial metabolism and accelerated mineralization 14 – 16 . The results of Zhang et al., (2008) indicated that the combination of total nutrient (TN) elements and C:N accounted for 70.2% of the variation in the litter decomposition rates and litter quality is the most important direct regulator of litter decomposition at the global scale 17 . Microbial community composition further modulates these processes, with native soil microbiomes typically promoting more balanced and functionally diverse organic matter turnover, while specific fungal or bacterial species may induce more rapid but less stable decomposition patterns due to reduced community complexity and resilience 18 – 20 . The interplay between mineralogy and microbiome influences both the extent and rate of litter mineralization by varying microbial colonization efficiency, enzyme production, and metabolic pathways. Elias et al., (2024) showed that high-quality litter leads to less efficient formation of mineral-associated SOC because it shifts the soil microbial community in ways that reduce carbon use efficiency. Low-quality litter, in turn, promotes the loss of pre-existing SOC, resulting in no net effect of litter quality on total mineral-associated SOC. Instead, mineral-associated SOC formation is governed primarily by soil mineralogy. These results challenge the long-standing hypothesis that high-quality plant litter most efficiently forms mineral-associated SOC and advance our understanding of how mineralogy and litter-microbial interactions regulate SOC formation 12 . Calcium carbonate serves as an additional critical component by buffering soil pH, influencing nutrient availability, and stabilizing organo-mineral complexes that protect carbon from rapid degradation. Increased CaCO3 concentrations have been shown to enhance microbial biomass and enzymatic activity, particularly when combined with specific mineralogical substrates and microbial communities, thus modulating soil respiration and carbon cycling 21 – 23 . Shabtai et al., (2023) showed that Ca additions promote hyphae-forming bacteria, which often specialize in colonizing surfaces, and increase incorporation of litter into microbial biomass and carbon use efficiency by approximately 45% each 23 . Although the roles of mineralogy, substrate quality, microbial community composition and calcium carbonate have been examined individually, their synergistic and antagonistic interactions remain poorly constrained and constitute a major source of uncertainty in predictions of soil carbon dynamics quantified and represent a key uncertainty in predicting soil carbon dynamics 12 . In particular, it is unclear how specific combinations of clay minerals, litter types, microbial communities and CaCO3 jointly modulate microbial activity, carbon stabilization and respiration, beyond the sum of their single-factor effects. Closing this gap is critical for improving process-based models of SOM turnover and underpins the multifactorial experimental design of this study, which explicitly tests how these factors interact to regulate litter decomposition and carbon fluxes 12 . Based on measurements of microbial respiration and biomass, we tested three main hypotheses: (a) the fibrous structure of palygorskite promotes faster litter decomposition and higher microbial biomass compared to the swelling structure of bentonite, (b) this mineral effect will be most pronounced with labile litter (alfalfa) and enhanced by carbonate, and (c) the native microbiome will support more complete but slower decomposition than a simplified synthetic community. Methods Sample collection and deployment We employed two clay types and a calcium carbonate sample from domestic sources (Fig.  1 and Tables 1 , 2 ) to focus on the effects of clay vs. microbial communities on decomposition of two litter types. Bentonite (HS Code: 2508.10) was provided by Pars Pouya Chemical Company (Isfahan, Iran), originating from a natural deposit in Semnan Province, Iran (≈35.6° N, 53.3° E). Palygorskite (HS Code: 2508.50) was supplied from Afatus Company, sourced from a mine near Mashhad, Iran (≈36.3° N, 59.6° E) (Fig.  2 ). Calcium carbonate (HS Code: 2836.50) was collected from a natural limestone deposit in the Khur and Biabanak region, Isfahan Province, Iran (≈33.7° N, 55.1° E). The electron microscope images of the two clays were obtained using the Leo 1430 VP (Fig.  3 ) and their specific surface areas were also determined by a Micromeritics TriStar II Plus surface area analyzer at Isfahan University of Technology, which were 16.476 m2/g and 36.826 m2/g for bentonite and palygorskite, respectively (Fig.  2 ). Prior to use in the incubation experiment, all mineral materials (bentonite, palygorskite and CaCO₃) were sterilized to eliminate indigenous microorganisms. Each material was air-dried, gently ground and passed through a 2-mm sieve, then spread in a thin layer (≤ 1 cm) in heat-resistant glass trays and autoclaved at 121 °C and 103 kPa (15 psi) for 20 min. After cooling to room temperature under sterile conditions, the autoclaving cycle was repeated on two consecutive days to ensure effective sterilization of resistant propagules. Sterilized minerals were stored in sealed, sterile containers at room temperature until mixing with the experimental litter.

Experimental design We constructed an artificial microbial inoculum consisting of Curtobacterium sp., Trichoderma harzianum, Phanerochaete chrysosporium and Pseudomonas fluorescens to contrast with the complex native soil microbiome, in which multiple taxa and interactions are present but difficult to disentangle. The selected taxa correspond to functional groups that are widely reported from agricultural and natural soils and are frequently associated with plant residues and the rhizosphere. The four species are functionally important in SOM cycling and were selected to represent complementary stages of litter decomposition. Curtobacterium sp. and P. fluorescens represent fast-growing, copiotrophic bacteria that colonize fresh substrates and utilize soluble carbon, whereas T. harzianum and P. chrysosporium represent saprotrophic fungi capable of producing a broad spectrum of extracellular enzymes targeting structural carbohydrates and lignin. This combination was therefore intended to capture both rapid mineralization of labile C and the breakdown of more recalcitrant litter components, providing a simplified but ecologically meaningful consortium for testing how mineralogy, CaCO₃ and litter quality shape microbial functioning 31 – 34 . Each microcosm was made of 500 ml glass Jar that created a “microbial microcosm” by restricting environmental disturbance. For our experiment, the microbial microcosms were filled out by litter (wheat or alfalfa, equivalent of 2% carbon w/w), clay (palygorskite or bentonite at three levels of 0, 10, and 20% w/w), calcium carbonate (at two levels of 7.5 and 15% w/w) and quartz sand as a filler to adjust final weight to 100 g. Then the combined mixtures were inoculated with extracted soil microbiome or specific bacterial and fungi communities (Supplementary Fig. 1). Control microcosms consisted of sterilized sand, calcium carbonate and litter inoculated with the native soil microbiome and the specific bacterial and fungal communities, but without added clay, providing a reference for microbial activity in the absence of mineral amendments. Abiotic CO₂ release from CaCO₃ was not separated experimentally; therefore, total CO₂ efflux should be interpreted as a combination of biotic and potential abiotic contributions. A total of 144 microbial microcosms (48 treatment combinations × 3 replicates) were subsequently incubated at 22 °C under controlled moisture conditions corresponding to a matric potential of 33 kPa throughout the incubation period (90 days) and retrieved in batches of 144 at 15, 30, 60, and 90 days post-incubation, for subsequent analysis of microbial biomass and respiration. Microbial respiration and biomass Soil microbial respiration (SMR, mg CO2-C kg–1 soil h–1) was measured using the method by Alef (1995) 35 . Fifty grams of samples was placed in 0.5-L capacity incubation Jars. Ten ml of 1.00 M NaOH solution in 50 ml falcon tubes were placed in each jar as the CO2 trap. Jars were made air tight immediately. Two jars with 1.00 M NaOH but without soil were used as controls. All jars were incubated at 25 °C. Carbon dioxide absorbed in traps was analyzed at 15, 30, 60, 90 days of NaOH placement. Each time fresh NaOH solution (10 ml) was replaced to trap CO2 for the next days. In this method, CO2 evolved from each sample was calculated as the difference between the initial and the CO2 concentration after each measurement period. Basal respiration rate was calculated based on cumulative CO2 evolution over the 90 days period. Microbial biomass carbon (MBC, mg CO2-C kg–1) was also measured by the fumigation-incubation method (Beck et al., 1997; Jenkinson and Powlson, 1976) at 15, 30, 60, 90 days of incubation. Two sets of soil samples (fumigated by chloroform gas for 24 h) and control (unfumigated) were inoculated with 0.1 g of the control samples and then the amount of CO2 released during incubation (at 25◦C for 10 days) of all soil samples were measured. Statistical analysis The experiment was conducted in a completely randomized design with a factorial arrangement, including six factors: clay type, clay percentage, microbiome type, litter residues type, calcium carbonate percentage, and incubation time. The experiment was performed in three replicates, with a total of 576 experimental samples. To describe the CO2 mineralization dynamics of wheat and alfalfa decomposition in the microcosms, two nonlinear models were selected based on both established theoretical assumptions about decomposition kinetics and quantitative model performance comparisons. Results Clay mineralogy as a primary control on C mineralization and microbial biomass The two clays differed strongly in mineralogical composition (Tables 1 and 2 ), with bentonite (smectite group) exhibiting a tri octahedral layered structure, high cation exchange capacity, pronounced swelling, and strong water absorption, while palygorskite showed a fibrous morphology, higher porosity, larger specific surface area, and greater adsorption capacity, further enriched by dolomite and chrysotile. These contrasting properties directly shaped their physicochemical behavior in the microcosms. Across all treatments, clay mineral type and content jointly controlled soil respiration kinetics and microbial biomass carbon (MBC), with palygorskite consistently enhancing both C turnover and microbial recovery relative to bentonite. In wheat litter microcosms, increasing clay content generally raised cumulative mineralized C (C₀), but clay mineralogy determined the kinetics: at 20% addition, palygorskite produced the highest C₀ and a significantly faster mineralization rate constant (k), whereas 20% bentonite yielded the lowest C₀ (p < 0.01; Fig.  4 a–d).

 A similar pattern emerged in alfalfa litter microcosms, where clay content remained the main driver of C₀, but clay type controlled the sigmoidal model parameters. Palygorskite treatments showed higher C₀ and lower inflection time (t₀) than bentonite, indicating an earlier inflection point and thus a faster progression to peak mineralization, together with distinct curve slopes (n) that reflect sharper mineralization pulses (p < 0.01; Fig.  10 a, c–e). Clay mineralogy also structured MBC dynamics over 90 days. In both wheat and alfalfa containing microcosms, palygorskite and bentonite amended soils had higher MBC than the clay free control, with an initial peak shortly after inoculation, a marked decline by day 30, and partial recovery by days 60–90 (p < 0.01; Figs. 4 e–f, 10 f-g). The strongest recovery occurred in 20% palygorskite (and in some cases 10–20% bentonite) at later sampling times, indicating that clay additions-especially palygorskite-provide a favorable matrix for microbial regrowth and longer term biomass persistence. Modulation of mineral controls by litter quality The two litters contrasted sharply in chemistry (Table 4 ): wheat litter had higher C content (34.55%), a very high C:N ratio (76.78), and elevated fiber fractions (NDF 71.73%, ADF 42.67%) together with low N (0.45%), consistent with a more recalcitrant, lignified substrate. In contrast, alfalfa litter contained less C (26.92%), much more N (2.83%), a low C:N ratio (9.51), and lower fiber (NDF 36.6%, ADF 27.3%), indicating a more degradable, nutrient rich material. These differences translated into distinct decomposition dynamics. Alfalfa microcosms showed faster mineralization, with lower t₀ values (earlier inflection points) and altered n, as well as higher C₀ at comparable clay contents than wheat microcosms, particularly when amended with palygorskite (p < 0.01; Figs. 4 , 10 ). Thus, high quality alfalfa litter accelerated the progression to peak mineralization, whereas low quality wheat residues slowed it. Litter quality also modulated MBC responses to clay mineralogy. In alfalfa microcosms, palygorskite supported rapid early increases in MBC, a sharp decline around day 30, and pronounced recovery by days 60–90, with the highest MBC observed in the 20% palygorskite treatment at day 90 (p < 0.05; Fig.  10 f, g). Wheat microcosms exhibited a similar temporal pattern but consistently lower MBC amplitudes, reflecting slower decomposition and reduced nutrient release. Taken together, these patterns indicate that high quality alfalfa litter amplifies the positive effect of palygorskite on both C mineralization and microbial biomass, whereas more recalcitrant wheat residues accentuate mineralogical constraints on decomposition and biomass build up. Influence of microbiome type on C and MBC dynamics across clay minerals Microbiome composition further modulated the mineralogical control on C mineralization and MBC 42 . In wheat litter microcosms, inoculation with the native soil microbiome resulted in 12% higher C₀ but 18% lower k than the fungi + bacteria inoculation, indicating greater cumulative C loss but slower mineralization rates over time (Fig.  5 a–c). In alfalfa microcosms, soil microbiome treatments likewise produced higher C₀ than fungi + bacteria, while exhibiting lower t₀ and n values, consistent with a more gradual but sustained mineralization trajectory (Fig.  11 a–d). Here, lower t₀ again reflects an earlier inflection point and faster approach to peak mineralization, whereas differences in n describe changes in how sharply mineralization accelerates and decelerates around that peak (Figs.  6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 ).

 In contrast, mixed fungal and bacterial inocula generally supported higher MBC in both litter types. In wheat microcosms, fungi + bacteria treatments maintained higher MBC than soil microbiome inocula and showed a pronounced recovery between days 30 and 60 (p < 0.05; Fig.  5 d). A similar pattern occurred in alfalfa microcosms, where fungi + bacteria maintained the highest MBC throughout the 90 days (p < 0.05; Fig.  11 e). Interactions between clay and microbiome highlighted this modulation: in both wheat and alfalfa systems, microcosms containing 20% palygorskite and fungi + bacteria (or, for some metrics, the soil microbiome) exhibited the greatest late stage MBC (days 60–90) together with high C₀ (p < 0.01; Figs. 7 , 13 ). These patterns show that microbiome structure can either enhance or dampen the primary mineralogical effect, depending on litter quality and clay type.

 Modifying role of calcium carbonate on mineral-microbiome-litter interactions Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) acted as a secondary modifier of the clay-microbiome-litter system rather than an independent primary driver. In wheat microcosms, increasing CaCO₃ from 7.5% to 15% significantly elevated both C₀ and k, indicating more total C mineralized and faster mineralization rates, and it also strengthened MBC responses, especially when combined with palygorskite and fungi + bacteria inoculation (p < 0.01; Figs. 6 , 8 , 9 ). The highest C₀ and MBC occurred in treatments combining 20% palygorskite with 15% CaCO₃ and either the soil microbiome or fungi + bacteria, demonstrating that elevated CaCO₃ can amplify the positive effect of palygorskite on C turnover and microbial growth (Figs. 8 d, 9 d). In alfalfa microcosms, CaCO₃ effects were subtler. Calcium carbonate did not significantly change C₀ or n, but higher CaCO₃ (15%) increased t₀ relative to 7.5%, indicating a later inflection point and therefore a delayed-but not diminished-approach to peak mineralization (p < 0.05; Figs.  12 , 13 ). For MBC, CaCO₃ interacted with both clay and microbiome: microcosms with clay had higher MBC than the clay free control at all CaCO₃ levels, and the highest end of incubation MBC was observed at 7.5% CaCO₃ with fungi + bacteria inoculation (p < 0.05; Figs. 14 , 15 ). Overall, CaCO₃ modulated the magnitude and timing of both C and MBC responses but did not overturn the primary controls exerted by clay mineralogy, litter quality, and microbiome type on soil C mineralization dynamics.

 Discussion The results reveal that soil minerals act not only as sorbents and physical protectors of organic matter, but also as habitat engineers that structure microhabitats and thereby regulate litter decomposition pathways and microbial biomass dynamics. By jointly manipulating clay mineralogy and content, calcium carbonate, litter quality, and microbiome composition, our experiment shows that mineral effects on decomposition are conditional: the same mineral surface can either facilitate rapid microbial processing or promote physical and chemical protection, depending on its structure, the traits of the colonizing community, and the quality of the litter substrate. This conditionality adds mechanistic depth to global soil-carbon frameworks that emphasize mineral-associated organic matter as a key sink for anthropogenic C inputs. Minerals as habitat engineers: porous vs. swelling filters A central, and mechanistically intriguing, result is that palygorskite accelerated litter decomposition and enhanced microbial biomass, despite being a clay mineral. Palygorskite consistently yielded higher cumulative mineralized C and faster mineralization rates than bentonite, particularly at higher clay contents, and supported stronger microbial biomass recovery over time. This pattern contrasts with the prevailing paradigm that increased mineral surface area generally slows decomposition by shielding organic matter from microbes. Our data suggest that palygorskite’s fibrous, high-porosity structure creates an open, well-connected pore network that facilitates colonization, diffusion of dissolved organic compounds, and retention of extracellular enzymes on mineral surfaces. The results are consistent with a catalytic or enzyme retaining microhabitat, although direct measurements were not performed 25 , 43 – 50 . Bentonite, in contrast, has a swelling, plate-like structure that can create smaller and more isolated pores as layers expand and contract. In our microcosms this structure appears to favor partial occlusion of litter fragments and reduced aeration, leading to lower mineralization and slower microbial biomass recovery than in palygorskite treatments at comparable clay contents. Thus, our results support a mechanistic distinction: fibrous, porous clays tend to function as open, colonizable habitats that facilitate rapid processing of fresh litter, whereas swelling smectites tend to function as denser matrices that restrict access and enhance protection. The high microbial biomass observed on palygorskite, especially at later incubation stages, is consistent with a facilitation regime in which a substantial fraction of litter C is first routed through microbial biomass before either respiration or stabilization on mineral surfaces 48 , 51 – 56 . Coupled controls of mineral structure, litter quality, and microbiome traits The contrasting behavior of wheat and alfalfa litters, and of native versus synthetic microbial communities, shows that mineral structure is only the first filter in a hierarchy of controls. High-quality alfalfa (N-rich, low C:N, low fiber) decomposed rapidly, with earlier inflection points and steeper mineralization curves than wheat. When combined with palygorskite, this labile substrate supported rapid microbial growth, high early respiration, and strong late-stage biomass recovery. In this configuration, the mineral matrix and substrate chemistry are aligned toward facilitation: porous mineral structure plus easily decomposable litter favor fast-growing populations that quickly exploit accessible C and N 48 , 49 , 55 , 57 , 58 . Recalcitrant wheat litter (high C:N, high fiber) slowed mineralization and made total C turnover more sensitive to clay content. Here, mineral structure governed how quickly microbial communities could rebuild after initial substrate depletion. The stronger dependence of wheat decomposition on clay content suggests that when substrates are more complex, the partitioning between accessible and protected microsites becomes more important than for alfalfa, and protection and diffusion limitation play larger roles 46 , 48 , 49 , 55 , 57 . Microbiome composition adds a third dimension to this hierarchy. Native soil communities generated higher cumulative mineralized C but slower kinetics, consistent with more diverse, functionally redundant consortia that process a broader spectrum of compounds over longer timescales. In contrast, synthetic fungi + bacteria consortia produced faster mineralization and higher microbial biomass, especially on palygorskite, indicative of rapid growth and efficient exploitation of the most accessible carbon pools. In other words, mineral structure filters which microbial traits are expressed: an open, fibrous habitat favors fast-growing, surface-colonizing communities, whereas a swelling, more occluding mineral environment favors slower but more persistent processing by complex native assemblages 48 , 49 , 55 , 57 , 59 – 61 . Calcium carbonate as a tuner of facilitation-protection balance Calcium carbonate modulated, rather than determined, the balance between facilitation and protection imposed by mineral structure and organic substrate. At higher levels, CaCO₃ enhanced total mineralized C, increased mineralization rates, and strengthened microbial biomass responses in wheat microcosms, particularly when paired with palygorskite and the synthetic fungi + bacteria community. These patterns are consistent with calcium reinforcing the habitat-engineering role of minerals by promoting aggregation, buffering pH, and fostering microenvironments where enzymes and substrates are retained close to microbial cells. Under these conditions, Ca does not simply “protect” organic matter; it can increase microbial access and may contribute to stabilization processes by altering pH and aggregation 23 , 48 , 56 , 62 – 64 . In alfalfa systems, CaCO₃ primarily shifted the timing of mineralization (inflection time) rather than the total amount of C decomposed, indicating that when substrate is highly labile, Ca’s main effect is to adjust the rate and sequence of processing rather than the overall extent. The observation that intermediate CaCO₃ levels with fungi + bacteria inocula produced the highest end-of-incubation biomass suggests that there is an optimal degree of Ca-mediated stabilization that balances accessibility with protection: too little Ca, and substrates and enzymes are lost from reactive surfaces; too much, and access becomes limited 23 , 48 , 56 , 64 , 65 . A hierarchical, predictive framework for mineral-microbe-litter interactions Together, these results support a conceptual framework in which C turnover pathways emerge from a hierarchy of interacting controls: (1) Mineral physical structure sets the first-order filter by defining whether soils behave more like open, colonizable “reactors” or more like protective matrices. (2) Litter quality determines how strongly this mineral filter can be exploited: high-quality litter amplifies facilitation on porous minerals, while low-quality litter makes systems more sensitive to occlusion and protection. (3) Microbial community traits modulate how this mineral-litter template is expressed in time, determining whether C is processed rapidly through fast-growing consortia or more slowly and completely by diverse, functionally redundant communities. (4) Calcium availability tunes this system by altering aggregation, pH, and organo-mineral association strength, thereby shifting the balance between accessibility and protection and adjusting the timing of C release. Within this hierarchy, our data allow qualitative prediction of C turnover pathways. Given a fibrous, high-porosity clay, a labile, N-rich litter, and a fast-growing fungal-bacterial community, we expect a fast, high-amplitude C turnover pathway with early inflection, high mineralization rates, and large biomass accumulation. Conversely, a swelling Smectite, recalcitrant high-C:N litter, and a complex native microbiome should yield slower, more protected turnover with later inflection, lower rates, and more gradual biomass dynamics. Intermediate combinations, modulated by CaCO₃, will position systems along this facilitation-protection continuum. By explicitly framing minerals as habitat engineers and emphasizing when they act as facilitators versus protectors, these findings refine the dominant view of mineral-associated organic matter as a static sink. Instead, mineral-microbe-litter interactions are dynamic and context-dependent, and the same mineral surface can underpin either rapid cycling or long-term stabilization depending on its structure, the traits of the microbial community, the quality of litter inputs, and the prevailing Ca environment. Conclusion This study shows that the physical architecture of clay minerals-from the open, fibrous pore network of palygorskite to the swelling, occluding layers of bentonite-creates distinct microhabitats that filter microbial functional traits and regulate access to, and turnover of, organic substrates of contrasting quality, with calcium carbonate further tuning this interplay through its effects on aggregation, pH, and organo-mineral associations. Recalcitrant wheat litter (high C:N, high fiber) channels the system toward protection-dominated regimes in which clay content and CaCO₃ primarily control the extent of mineralization and biomass recovery, whereas labile, N-rich alfalfa litter shifts control toward facilitation, with decomposition timing and rate shape governed mainly by clay morphology and microbial community composition. While clay minerals are often represented in models as uniformly protective matrices, our results demonstrate a conditional duality: the same mineral surface can either facilitate rapid decomposition by hosting fast-growing fungal-bacterial consortia in open pore networks or enhance long-term stabilization by restricting access within swelling, diffusion-limited domains. Future predictive frameworks should therefore move beyond a single “clay effect” scalar and instead represent the functional coupling between mineral surface properties (e.g., specific surface area, porosity, swelling behavior), calcium-mediated organo-mineral binding, and microbial functional guilds (such as copiotrophs vs. oligotrophs and filamentous fungi) 66 , so as to mechanistically capture the context-dependent pathways of carbon persistence and turnover revealed here. Since mechanistic pathways such as enzyme sorption or molecular-scale stabilization were not directly quantified, these interpretations should be viewed as hypotheses supported by the observed respiration and biomass patterns. In addition, abiotic CO₂ evolution from CaCO₃ cannot be excluded, so the carbonate-related effects should be interpreted as net outcomes of both microbial and chemical processes.
