Dataset on microbial community structure in response to microalgal cultivation
in dairy wastewater.

This data article describes a comprehensive dataset investigating the dynamics
between microalgae, bacteria, and pollutant removal in dairy wastewater
treatment. Data was collected from a 12-day laboratory-scale experiment
employing three distinct cultivation systems: monoculture, co-culture, and
sequential culture of four algal/cyanobacterial strains (Chlorella sorokiniana,
Euglena gracilis, Synechocystis sp., and Desertifilum tharense). The generated
dataset includes high-frequency measurements of water quality parameters (COD,
NH4 +-N, TN, TP), algal physiological data (biomass production, total
chlorophyll, biochemical compositions like proteins, lipids, and
polysaccharides), and 16S rRNA gene sequencing data of the associated bacterial
communities, which were fractionated into free-living and tightly bound
phycosphere populations. The reuse potential of this data is significant. It
provides a detailed profile of microbial community assembly driven by different
cultivation strategies and environmental factors, offering a benchmark for
future ecological studies in engineered systems. Researchers in the fields of
wastewater biotechnology, microbial ecology, and synthetic ecology can reuse
this data to validate microbial interaction models, optimize consortia design
for bioremediation, and inform life-cycle assessments of algal-based treatment
processes. The dataset generated in this study is publicly available in the NCBI
BioProject repository under the accession number [PRJNA1265442].

Data was collected from a 12-day laboratory-scale experiment employing three distinct cultivation systems: monoculture, co-culture, and sequential culture of four algal/cyanobacterial strains (Chlorella sorokiniana, Euglena gracilis, Synechocystis sp., and Desertifilum tharense). It provides a detailed profile of microbial community assembly driven by different cultivation strategies and environmental factors, offering a benchmark for future ecological studies in engineered systems. Researchers in the fields of wastewater biotechnology, microbial ecology, and synthetic ecology can reuse this data to validate microbial interaction models, optimize consortia design for bioremediation, and inform life-cycle assessments of algal-based treatment processes. The dataset generated in this study is publicly available in the NCBI BioProject repository under the accession number [PRJNA1265442].  • This dataset provides a detailed profile of the bacterial communities including those attached to the microalgae (phycosphere) and those free-living in the wastewater tracking how they change throughout the wastewater treatment process. By comparing distinct cultivation methods, such as microalgae and cyanobacteria monocultures, co-cultures, and sequential cultures over a 12-day period, the data reveals how cultivation strategy shapes the microbial ecosystem. The inclusion of taxonomic identities, abundances, and predicted functional roles allows others to diagnose the specific mechanisms behind these shifts. • The data directly links the evolving bacterial community structure to key environmental factors, such as nutrient levels (total nitrogen, ammonia nitrogen, total phosphorus, COD, pH) and microalgae-specific factors (chlorophyll, biomass, EPS, and biochemical content). This integrated approach helps researchers build predictive models of how the system's chemistry and biology interact to drive microbial population dynamics, which is vital for optimizing future wastewater treatment systems. • The provision of the complete, raw sequencing data ensures full transparency and reusability. This allows the research community to re-analyze the data with different statistical or bioinformatic tools, validate functional predictions, or integrate it with other datasets for larger-scale comparative studies.
 2 Background Microalgae-based systems demonstrate exceptional efficacy in treating nutrient-rich wastewater. Performance in dairy wastewater,Tetraselmis indica showed removal of COD (87.49%), TN (83.76%), and TP (79.83%) [ 1 ], and Chlorella sp can achieve near-total removal of nitrogen, phosphorus, and over 84% of COD [ 2 ]. Algae-bacteria consortia showed high COD (93%) and NH4+-N (87.2%) removal [ 3 ]. This variability is further illustrated by the range of nitrogen removal (66.0-93.7%) reported in dairy wastewater studies using biofilms and mixed cultures [ 4 , 5 ]. A key factor in this performance is the dynamic relationship between microalgae and associated bacteria, particularly within the algal phycosphere. This microenvironment, shaped by microalgal exudates, is crucial for nutrient cycling, community assembly, and overall system function. The primary research article investigated how different cultivation methods (mono-, co-, and sequential cultures) reshape these bacterial communities and their interactions to influence treatment efficiency in dairy wastewater. It offers visual documentation of culture status and the complete functional profiles of the prokaryotic communities. By compiling the full, raw microbial dataset, we provide a resource for the scientific community to verify our analyses, conduct independent comparative studies, and advance methodological standardization in this field. 3 Data Description This data article provides both the raw sequencing files and processed data analyzing the divergence in structure, function, and diversity of phycosphere-associated versus free-living bacterial communities ( Fig. 1 ). CK is the control. 4 Experimental Design, Materials and Methods 4.1 Wastewater collection and pre-treatment Raw dairy wastewater was collected from a dairy farm in Tianjin, China. To preserve the native microbial community for subsequent analysis, all handling was performed under non-sterile conditions. The wastewater was first filtered through six layers of sterile medical gauze to remove coarse particulate matter and suspended solids. The clarified filtrate was then diluted to 50% (v/v) with deionized water to achieve operational concentrations while maintaining the original nutrient ratios. The characteristics of the pre-treated dairy wastewater were as follows: COD (1960.0 ± 220.0 mg/L), NH4+-N (220.00 ± 2.7 mg/L), TN (733.3 ± 0.1 mg/L), TP (11.06 ± 0.6 mg/L), and pH (8.4 ± 0.1). 4.2 Cultivation Systems Three distinct cultivation systems were established in 500 mL glass bottles with a 250 mL working volume: (1) monoculture (MC) systems (MC1-MC4), (2) co-culture (CC) systems (CC1-CC4), and (3) sequential culture (SC) systems (SC1-SC4). The details description of involve strains showed in Table 3 . All systems were inoculated with an initial biomass concentration of 0.25 ± 0.1 g/L. For the co-culture systems, constituent strains were combined at a 1:1 dry weight ratio. The experiment was conducted over 12 days under controlled environmental conditions at 25 ± 0.9°C and continuous illumination at 6000 ± 100 lux provided by four 36-watt white LED arrays, following an established protocol [ 6 ]. The cultures were shaken three times daily, and no external CO2 was supplemented. Following the 12-day cultivation period, the microbial community was analyzed via 16S rRNA gene sequencing. To differentiate between bacterial fractions, samples were sequentially filtered: first through a 3 µm polycarbonate membrane to capture microalgal cells and their tightly phycosphere-associated bacteria (PS), and subsequently through a 0.2 µm membrane to collect the free-living bacteria (FL) in the supernatant. DNA was extracted from the biomass on both membranes following the methods of Liu et al. [ 7 ]. 4.4 16S rRNA gene amplification and sequencing The hypervariable V3-V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primers 341F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′). A unique 8-base barcode was added to the 5′ end of both the forward and reverse primers to allow for sample multiplexing. The PCR reactions were performed in a total volume of 30 µL, containing 15 µL of Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs), 0.2 µM of each forward and reverse primer, and approximately 10 ng of template DNA. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 98°C for 1 minute; 30 cycles of denaturation at 98°C for 10 seconds, annealing at 50°C for 30 seconds, and elongation at 72°C for 30 seconds; followed by a final extension at 72°C for 5 minutes. The resulting PCR products were purified using magnetic beads. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs) following the manufacturer's instructions. Library quality was assessed using a Qubit fluorometer and an Agilent Bioanalyzer system. Finally, amplicon sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform (PE300) by a commercial service provider (Tianjin Novogene Co., Ltd.). 4.5 Data processing and analysis The raw paired-end FASTQ files were processed using QIIME 2 (version 2020.6). Sequences were quality-filtered, denoised, merged, and checked for chimeras using DADA2 to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Taxonomy was assigned to the ASVs using a pre-trained classifier (e.g., Silva 138 database) with a 99% similarity threshold. The resulting feature table and taxonomy files were used for downstream analysis. Alpha and beta-diversity metrics were calculated within QIIME 2. Functional prediction was performed using FAPROTAX based on the obtained taxonomic assignments. CCA/RDA analysis was performed using the vegan package in R to correlate community variation with environmental factors. Limitations It is important to consider the inherent limitations of 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing. This approach is subject to primer bias, as the specific primer set used (341F/806R targeting the V3-V4 region) can exhibit differential amplification efficiencies for various bacterial taxa. Consequently, the observed community structure may not perfectly reflect the absolute abundance of organisms in the original sample. The use of different primer sets targeting alternative variable regions, or modifications to PCR conditions (e.g., annealing temperature, cycle number, or the use of adjuvants), can potentially yield different community profiles. While this study provides a robust comparative analysis of microbial communities under different cultivation systems, these methodological constraints should be considered when interpreting the results.
