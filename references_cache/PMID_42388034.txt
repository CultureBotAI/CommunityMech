Title: A Bacillus-based synthetic community: integrated growth promotion and control of fusarium root rot and black shank in tobacco.
Source: Pest Management Science, 2026
URL: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42388034/

Abstract:
Soil-borne diseases such as Fusarium root rot and black shank severely threaten tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum L.) production. We assembled a Bacillus-based synthetic microbial community (SynCom) from three well-characterized strains with complementary antagonistic and plant growth-promoting traits: B. amyloliquefaciens G12 (isolated from the tobacco rhizosphere), and two tobacco endophytic strains, B. pumilus Y4 and B. velezensis X25. Prior studies showed that G12 strongly antagonizes Fusarium oxysporum, whereas Y4 and X25 inhibit Phytophthora nicotianae. The SynCom members showed good compatibility (inhibition rate <25%) and the SynCom exhibited higher indole-3-acetic acid production (11.57 +/- 0.24 mg.L-1) than any single strain. Glasshouse assays showed significant disease suppression (82.9% preventive; 65.3% curative), accompanied by enhanced activities of defense-related enzymes and improved root traits. Root activity increased by >40%, and root length and surface area were 1.5-2.0-fold greater than those of the single-strain treatments. Transcriptome analysis identified 12 475 differentially expressed genes and suggested possible involvement of jasmonic acid -related signaling and secondary metabolism pathways in the plant response to SynCom treatment. Field trials confirmed <=40% disease reduction and a 37.5-41.3% increase in middle-leaf-based estimated yield, outperforming a commercial B. subtilis product. Rhizosphere profiling showed no major change in overall community diversity and only minor, nonsignificant shifts in the relative abundance of Bacillus and Fusarium under SynCom treatment.

Three Bacillus strains were used in this study. Strains G12, Y4 and X25 were provided by the Laboratory of Integrated Management of Crop Pests, College of Horticulture and Plant Protection, Henan University of Science and Technology. Their taxonomic identities, confirmed in previous studies, were B. amyloliquefaciens (G12), B. pumilus (Y4) and B. velezensis (X25). All three strains were originally isolated from tobacco plants or rhizosphere soils collected in the Luoyang region, Henan Province, China. Specifically, strain G12 was isolated from the tobacco rhizosphere, whereas Y4 and X25 were endophytes from tobacco tissues. G12 showed strong antagonism to F. oxysporum , and Y4 and X25 inhibited P. nicotianae , the causal agents of tobacco Fusarium root rot and black shank, respectively. Thus, the SynCom was assembled primarily to combine complementary antagonistic activities against the two major tobacco pathogens together with compatible plant growth‐promoting potential. All three displayed significant inhibition in preliminary bioassays. Strains were stored in 20% glycerol at −80 °C and reactivated in L uria– B ertani (LB) medium before use.

For single‐strain fermentation, activated cultures were streaked on LB agar, and a single colony was inoculated into LB broth, incubated at 30 °C and 180 rpm until the optical density at 600 nm (OD 600 ) was 1.0 ± 0.1. For the S yn C om (SC), the three broths were cultured to OD 600 = 0.3, mixed in equal volumes (1:1:1, v/v/v), and further incubated to OD 600 = 1.0 ± 0.1.

Pathogens F. oxysporum (FO) and P. nicotianae (PN) were isolated from diseased tobacco fields and preserved in 20% glycerol at −80 °C. Before experiments, cultures were revived on potato sucrose agar (PSA) and subcultured twice. The tobacco cultivar He Luo No. 1 (LY1306) was supplied by the Tobacco Production Technology Center of Luoyang Tobacco Company (Henan, China).

For co‐cultivation, single‐strain and SynCom broths were inoculated into LB medium and incubated at 25 °C, 180 rpm for 48 h. OD 600 was measured using LB as a blank to assess growth inhibition or enhancement. All cultures were analyzed in triplicate.

Two independent glasshouse experiments were conducted to verify reproducibility. Conditions were maintained at 25 ± 2 °C, 12 h:12 h, light:dark photoperiod and 70–80% RH. Each experiment lasted 21 days. Uniform tobacco seedlings at the cross stage were transplanted into sterilized substrate in 21‐cell trays (15 plants per tray) and acclimated for 7 days.

Treatments included three single Bacillus strains, the SynCom, a pathogen‐only control (FO/PN), a commercial B. subtilis agent (BS) and a chemical fungicide (Thiram). Two application modes were used: (1) curative, where seedlings were inoculated with FO (1 × 10 4 spores·mL −1 ) and PN (1 × 10 4 zoospores·mL −1 ) followed 1 day later by bacterial treatment; and (2) preventive, where bacteria were applied 1 day before pathogen inoculation. Commercial controls were applied once at manufacturer‐recommended dosages.

A randomized block design with three replicates per site was used, each replicate containing three plots. Each plot (100 m 2 ) comprised four tobacco rows with 25 plants per row, giving a planting density of ≈15 000 plants·ha −1 (row spacing 120 cm, plant spacing 55 cm). Two buffer rows separated adjacent plots. Healthy seedlings were transplanted at time point T 0 , acclimated for 7 days, and then subjected to root irrigation treatments. Each plant received 10 mL single‐strain or SynCom fermentation broth near the stem base, with soil immediately covered to ensure infiltration. A second identical treatment was applied 7 days later. The commercial microbial and fungicide controls were applied per manufacturer instructions.

At T 3 , disease incidence and severity were evaluated under natural disease occurrence conditions. The field disease assessment focused on naturally occurring tobacco soil‐borne diseases, mainly Fusarium root rot and black shank, which occurred simultaneously under field conditions. Disease incidence and DI were evaluated using the same grading and DI methods described in Section 2.4 . Control efficacy (CE, %) was calculated as: CE = ( DI c – DI t )/ DI c × 100%, where DI c and DI t are disease indices of the control and treatment groups, respectively. Representative disease symptoms were photographed for each treatment.

Pairwise plate confrontation assays showed that all interactions among the three Bacillus strains exhibited mild inhibition (Type II, I <25%). Control plates [Supporting information Fig. S1(a)–(c) ] displayed normal colony morphology and uniform radial growth, whereas confrontation plates [Fig. S1(d)–(f) ] showed slight growth inhibition at contact zones. Specifically, inhibition rates of G12 against Y4 and X25 were 5.86 ± 0.38% and 3.91 ± 0.77%; Y4 against G12 and X25 were 5.15 ± 2.39% and 4.64 ± 0.57%; and X25 against G12 and Y4 were 18.24 ± 0.93% and 20.99 ± 1.43%, respectively. All values were <25%, confirming mild inhibition [Fig. 1(A),(B) ].

The IAA quantification using the Salkowski method produced a strong linear calibration ( y = 0.0356 x – 0.1938, R 2 = 0.9908) [Fig. 1(D) ]. Measured IAA concentrations were: SynCom, 11.57 ± 0.24 mg·L −1 ; G12, 5.04 ± 0.18 mg·L −1 ; Y4, 10.96 ± 0.36 mg·L −1 ; and X25, 8.09 ± 0.15 mg·L −1 [Fig. 1(E) ]. The SynCom produced significantly more IAA than any single strain ( P < 0.05), suggesting that co‐cultivation may have increased overall IAA production. This pattern is consistent with functional complementation among strains, although the underlying metabolic interactions require further validation.

Regarding disease suppression, both preventive and curative SynCom applications significantly reduced disease incidence and severity relative to the pathogen control (Fig. S2 ). Preventive application was more effective, lowering DI by 82.87% compared to the pathogen control (DI: 54.07–9.26) and by 65.28% relative to curative treatment (DI: 26.67). Although Thiram achieved the highest control efficacy [Fig. 4(C) ], SynCom provided strong protection with improved plant health. Disease severity assessments showed SynCom treatments increased the proportion of healthy plants (grade 0) and reduced severe infections (grade ≥5) compared to single‐strain and the commercial microbial agent [Fig. 4(D) ; S3 ]. In preventive SynCom treatment, 57% of plants were healthy versus 0% in the pathogen control, and disease incidence (≥1) dropped from 100 to 43%.

Qualitative observations showed that roots of SynCom‐treated plants displayed a deeper red coloration than those treated with single‐strain, reflecting higher dehydrogenase activity and metabolic intensity [Fig. 6(A) ]. Among single‐strains, Y4 induced the strongest color, followed by G12 and X25. Under curative conditions, SynCom maintained the highest recovery of root bioactivity, whereas the FO/PN control showed minimal color development. These findings indicate that SynCom mitigated pathogen‐induced inhibition of root respiration and promoted metabolic stability. The TTC reduction curve exhibited excellent linearity ( R 2 = 0.9954), confirming assay reliability [Fig. 6(B) ]. Quantitatively, SynCom significantly enhanced root activity compared with single‐strain treatments [Fig. 6(C),(D) ], showing 20.27–45.38% higher activity under preventive treatment ( P < 0.001) and consistently higher activity under curative treatment, These results suggest that the combined inoculation improved root metabolic activity and recovery under pathogen stress.

Together, these results demonstrate that SynCom consistently enhances growth and disease suppression with strong reproducibility across environments. Evidence from both glasshouse and field trials indicates that SynCom treatment was associated with improved vegetative growth, reduced disease severity, and increased middle‐leaf‐based estimated yield. These effects may be related to plant‐growth‐promoting traits and defense‐associated responses, but the underlying hormonal and resource‐allocation mechanisms require further validation. The consistent performance across both sites supports the potential of SynCom as a promising biofertilization and biocontrol strategy for tobacco production.

Beyond these molecular responses, comparisons between single‐strain BCAs and the SynCom clearly demonstrated the advantages of the community approach. In glasshouse trials, preventive SynCom treatment reduced disease incidence by 82.9% compared with the pathogen control and induced much higher SOD, POD and CAT activities, reaching up to three‐fold above the control and exceeding all single‐strain treatments. SynCom also promoted root development, indicating synergistic enhancement of nutrient uptake and stress tolerance. Field experiments further confirmed this superiority. SynCom achieved 34.7–40.0% disease suppression and 37.5–41.3% yield increases across sites, outperforming all single‐strain and commercial microbial treatments. The consistent yield increases observed at both field sites indicate that SynCom application may provide stable agronomic benefits beyond disease suppression. Although chemical fungicides provided stronger direct suppression, SynCom maintained healthier canopies and more stable productivity. Overall, these results suggest that multistrain integration can enhance efficacy while causing limited detectable disturbance to the native rhizosphere microbiome, offering a sustainable strategy for tobacco disease management.

From an ecological perspective, the limited impact of SynCom on rhizosphere microbial diversity is noteworthy because it suggests that disease suppression and yield improvement were achieved without major detectable disruption of the resident microbial community. In terms of its impact on the local microbiome, SynCom had only limited effects on the rhizosphere microbial community under field conditions and did not significantly alter overall bacterial or fungal beta diversity. This limited effect might be a product of the simple genus‐level composition of SynCom, which may reduce its capacity to interfere with complex native microbial networks, and also may be related to the endophytic origin of most SynCom members. However, its actual colonization patterns in the tobacco rhizosphere and plant tissues remain to be directly verified. The relative abundance of the beneficial genus Bacillus increased slightly from 0.04% in the control to 0.05% under SynCom treatment, whereas the pathogenic genus Fusarium decreased from 12.60% to 12.50%. Although these shifts were modest, they should be interpreted cautiously because they were not statistically significant, and therefore suggest at most limited directional changes in specific taxa rather than clear enrichment of beneficial microbes or suppression of pathogens. 31 Such limited disturbance of the native rhizosphere microbiome may help preserve soil ecological functions and long‐term soil health. 32 A biological control agent that can suppress pathogens while preserving the structure of indigenous microbial communities minimizes ecological risk and maintains system resilience. 33 The negligible alteration in community diversity under SynCom treatment suggests that SynCom application caused limited disturbance to the native microbiome at the sampling endpoint, although the persistence and colonization dynamics of the SynCom members remain to be further determined. 34 Field trials further confirmed that SynCom effectively reduced disease incidence while causing little detectable disruption to overall rhizosphere community diversity at the sampling endpoint, supporting its potential as a sustainable, ecologically compatible strategy for integrated crop protection. However, because the rhizosphere microbiome was assessed at a single field site and one sampling endpoint, the present data do not directly demonstrate temporal stability, resilience after disturbance, or long‐term persistence of the SynCom‐associated community pattern.

Despite the promising results demonstrating SynCom's advantages in disease control and plant growth promotion, its performance under different soil types, climatic conditions and pathogen pressures still requires further validation. Future research should focus on optimizing formulation parameters, including dosage, application frequency and timing, to achieve consistent efficacy across diverse environments. Additionally, inclusion of whole‐plant or plot‐level harvested yield and tobacco quality traits would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of its production value under field conditions. At the same time, it is necessary to assess cost‐effectiveness and long‐term ecological impacts to support its practical adoption. Improving SynCom formulation and carrier stability also will enhance its shelf life and field applicability. At the current stage, this SynCom should be regarded primarily as a function‐guided consortium assembled on the basis of complementary antagonistic activities, limited interstrain growth interference and plant‐growth‐promoting potential. Ecological niche differentiation, rhizosphere colonization ability, persistence and tissue‐colonization complementarity among the assembled SynCom were not directly assessed in the present study and remain to be further validated. Elucidating the causal relationship between SynCom inoculation and root hormonal signaling, particularly through the identification and tracking of key metabolites, will deepen our understanding of plant–microbe interactions and their influence on host physiology. Although this study already has integrated transcriptomic and rhizosphere microbiome analyses, further investigations are needed to determine the specific secondary metabolites produced by SynCom that modulate gene expression in tobacco, inhibit pathogen infection, and alter pathogen abundance in soil. Applying multi‐omics approaches such as metabolomics, metagenomics and single‐cell sequencing will be essential for validating these mechanisms and clarifying the molecular basis of SynCom–host–pathogen interactions. Furthermore, research on co‐adaptation and colonization dynamics between SynCom and indigenous beneficial microbes, along with comprehensive cost–benefit and life‐cycle assessments, will be crucial for developing sustainable, mechanism‐driven and ecologically compatible biocontrol strategies.
