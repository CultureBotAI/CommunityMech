Background The rumen functions as an anaerobic fermentation chamber, housing microorganisms with cellulolytic and proteolytic capabilities that facilitate feed utilization. Fiber-degrading bacteria possess the capability to enhance the productivity of cellulolytic feed. The application of omics technologies has greatly improved our understanding of the rumen microbiome. Determining microbial composition and functional patterns in the rumen does not equate to a comprehensive exploration of rumen microbial resources and their mechanisms of action. This study seeks to integrate high throughput 16S rRNA data with information on culturomics, cellulolytic activities, nutrition, and synthetic microbial communities (SynCom) engineering. The objective is to evaluate the relationship between rumen microbial activity and fiber utilization efficiency in cattle, ultimately aiming to develop a more powerful intervention strategy for the ruminant industry. Results The enrichment culture with various carbon sources led to significant alterations in the composition and structure of rumen microbiota, particularly enhancing those associated with carbohydrate metabolism. Employing the culturomics methodology, 896 strains from 78 species (including 8 novel species) were isolated, resulting in a 10.1% isolation rate relative to the rumen bacterial community. Among them, 35 strains demonstrated boosted cellulose-degrading capability on plates, while 25 exhibited the ability to degrade hemicellulose as well. SynComs of these candidates were prepared based on the ratio observed in rumen microbiota exhibiting high cellulolytic performance. SynCom 3 improved the neutral detergent fiber degradation (NDFD) by 20.39% averagely. Additionally, both in vitro and in situ assessments indicated that the optimization of dose/strain in SynCom 3 significantly improved the in vitro NDFD by 20.56% and increased the in situ NDFD by 7.81%, along with the acidic detergent fiber (ADF, + 11.47%). Genomic analysis revealed that the SynCom 3 functioned well in fiber degradation through the synergistic action of key carbohydrate-active enzymes. Conclusions This study strengthens rumen microbiome research by integrating omics and SynCom engineering within a microbiota-bacteria-enzymes-genes framework, revealing the significance of enzymatic synergy in carbohydrate metabolism. The findings establish a framework for utilizing low-abundance microbes and engineering functional consortia, which are crucial for improving ruminant feed utilization and biomass conversion. Future research should investigate the transcriptomic profiles and the metabolic cross-feeding mechanisms of fiber-degrading strains in the rumen.

Fiber degradation is a complex and essential biochemical process within the digestive system of ruminants, influenced by fiber types [ 4 – 6 ], physiological conditions of the rumen [ 7 ], and the structure and functions of rumen microbiota [ 8 , 9 ]. With the advent of third-generation sequencing technologies like SMRT Cell 8M, full-length sequences of 16S rRNA genes, rather than partial sequences (V4 or V3–V4), are produced, allowing for precise analysis and comprehensive resolution of the composition and function of rumen microbiota [ 10 ]. Modulating the rumen microbiota for beneficial outcomes requires the isolation, identification, and application of specific strains, a process that is both essential and challenging [ 11 ]. Culturomics seeks to gather microbial resources utilizing high throughput culture methodologies [ 12 ]. Isolating traditionally uncultured strains from a specific niche can be achieved efficiently by optimizing culture conditions and medium components [ 13 ], particularly through the discovery of previously unknown species and functional strains. Using 212 distinct culture conditions, 340 bacterial species, including representatives from two rare phyla, were isolated from human stool samples [ 14 ]. The proliferation of rapidly growing bacteria can impede the survival of slower-growing species. Previous studies have addressed this limitation by extending the in vitro cultivation period to improve microbial diversity, structure, and unique OTUs [ 15 – 17 ]. The combination of culturomics and high throughput sequencing has shown notable benefits in microbiology, where molecular tools and culture methods work together to offer a more comprehensive understanding of the microbiome [ 18 – 20 ]. Moreover, high throughput sequencing serves as a method to evaluate culture diversity, thus enhancing the microbial species available for natural product screening [ 21 , 22 ]. Rumen microorganisms, comprising bacteria, fungi, and protozoa, possess the capability to secrete specific enzymes, including cellulase and xylanase, which facilitate the decomposition of feed fiber into oligosaccharides [ 23 ]. Research indicates that rumen microorganisms from the phyla Bacteroidetes (e.g., Bacteroides and Prevotella) and Firmicutes (e.g., Ruminococcus, Fibrobacter, Butyrivibrio, and Ruminiclostridium) are crucial for fiber degradation [ 5 ]. Fibrobacter succinogenes, Ruminococcus flavefaciens, and Ruminococcus albus constituted significant portions of the cellulolytic bacterial community in the cattle rumen, representing 33.0%, 2.6%, and 43.0% of the total population, respectively [ 24 ]. Furthermore, their capacity for fiber degradation surpassed that of other cellulolytic ruminal species [ 25 ]. Studies confirmed that R. flavefaciens is capable of degrading cellulose and cellobiose, but demonstrates inefficiency in glucose utilization [ 26 ]. R. albus primarily ferments glucose to acetate, ethanol, formate, CO2, H2, and an unidentified product [ 27 ]. F. succinogenes was able to hydrolyze various polysaccharides; however, it could only utilize the hydrolytic products derived from cellulose [ 28 ]. Nevertheless, there has been limited attention given to other rumen microbes of low abundance or unclassified taxonomy, which play a significant role in the overall cellulose digestion process [ 25 ]. Numerous studies indicated that the efficacy of an individual strain in fiber degradation in vivo often falls short of expectations; conversely, synthetic microbial communities (SynCom) exhibit greater stability and efficiency [ 29 , 30 ]. Fifteen bacterial strains exhibiting high cellulase activity demonstrated limited filter paper degradation when assessed individually; however, their collective fiber degradation capability was markedly enhanced [ 31 ]. The synergistic effect arises primarily from the release of specific compounds or factors that improve the activity of another strain, or from the complementarity and interaction of enzyme activity among different strains, thus improving overall fiber degradation efficiency. Therefore, this study aims to integrate SMRT Sequencer technology with culturomics technology to investigate the fiber degradation capabilities of culture-rumen bacteria. It seeks to customize the optimal microbial community design based on functional characteristics by combining high-throughput experimental analysis with the biological functions of microbial strains. Materials and methods Animal management and diet This research adhered to the guidelines set forth by the requirements of the Animal Ethics Committee of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Beijing, China). Four postlactating Holstein cows were randomly chosen from a commercial dairy farm located in Beijing, China. Cows were fed a total mixed ration (TMR) twice daily, primarily composed of corn silage and haylage, formulated to meet or exceed nutrient recommendations based on their milk production (NRC, 2001) (Table S1). They were milked three times daily, with an average daily milk yield of 31.50 ± 1.12 kg/day (Table S2). Animals were provided with unrestricted access to feed and clean water continuously throughout the day. Sample collection and treatment Rumen fluid was collected prior to morning feeding using a flexible oral stomach tube and a 50-mL syringe. The device underwent thorough cleaning with fresh warm water between sample conditions, and the initial 50 mL of rumen fluid was discarded to reduce saliva contamination. Subsequently, 100 mL of rumen fluid was obtained from each animal, filtered through four layers of sterilized gauze, and placed into separate sterilized anaerobic containers containing 30% aseptic glycerin. The samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at ‒ 80 ℃ before processing. To access the changes in microbial community for efficient carbohydrate degradation, 1 mL of fresh rumen fluid was inoculated into Hungate anaerobic tubes containing 7 mL of M10 liquid medium (Table S3), with four replicates and incubated statically at 39 °C. Fluid samples were collected at days 0, 7, 14, and 28 as the D0, D7, D14, and D28 groups, respectively. DNA extraction and amplificon sequencing The genomic DNA was extracted from fresh and cultured rumen fluid samples using E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA). The full-length 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primers 27 F (5’- AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-CRGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′), where the barcode is an eight-base sequence unique to each sample. The ABI GeneAmp® 9700 was used to perform PCR amplification under the following conditions: denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 27 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 60 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min; the amplification was then kept at 4°C. PCR products were extracted from the gel and purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA) after being measured by QuantiFluor™-ST utilizing electrophoresis. The purified PCR products were pooled for multiplex sequencing on a PacBio Sequel II platform at Biozeron, Shanghai, China. PacBio raw readings were processed with SMRT Link Analysis software version 9.0 to generate demultiplexed circular consensus sequence (CCS) reads, with the following parameters: minimum number of passes = 3, minimum projected accuracy = 0.99. Raw readings were processed via SMRT Portal to filter sequences based on length (< 800 or > 2500 bp) and quality. Sequences underwent additional filtration by eliminating barcode and primer sequences, chimeras, and any sequences containing 10 consecutive identical nucleotides. Sequences were dereplicated and analyzed using the DADA2 algorithm (recommended by QIIME 2) to identify indel mutations and substitutions [ 32 ]. Trimming and filtering were conducted on paired reads with a maximum of two expected errors per read (maxEE = 1). Following the merging of paired reads and the filtering of chimeras, the phylogenetic affiliation of each 16S rRNA gene sequence, referred to as RSVs, was assessed using the RDP Classifier ( http://rdp.cme.msu.edu/ ) in comparison to the silva (SSU132) 16S rRNA database, applying a confidence threshold of 70% [ 33 ]. Due to RDP’s limitation in taxonomic resolution to the genus level, each sequence was additionally queried against the NCBI 16S rRNA database utilizing BLASTn [ 34 ]. The taxonomy of the highest identity sequence was provided after the resulting results were sorted by e-value, score, and identity in that order [ 35 ]. For each sequence, a possible nearest-neighbor species with > 98.65% identity was chosen; if not, the sequence was noted as “unclassified” for the species categorization [ 36 ]. Lastly, we combined the annotation findings from the RDP and NCBI databases to create the taxonomy table for RSVs from domain to species level. Isolation of rumen microbes Rumen fluid samples were diluted in sterile PBS at 102, 103, and104-fold and subsequently plated on various media, including BHI (Brain heart infusion agar medium; Solarbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China), CA (Columbia blood agar Base medium; Sigma-Aldrich, Shanghai, China), PA and PB agar medium [ 37 ], MBA and MBB agar medium [ 38 ], WIL (Wilkins Chalgren Anaerobe Broth agar medium; Solarbio), and YCFA agar medium [ 39 ] (Table S3). Following cultivation at 39 °C for 3 days in anaerobic bags (Hope Biotechnology, Qingdao, China), individual colonies were selected and streaked on BHI agar medium for purification to obtain pure cultures. Cultured rumen fluid samples (D7, D14, D28) were diluted in sterile PBS at 102, 103, and104 10⁴-fold and plated onto M10 agar medium [ 40 ] (Table S3). Following a 5-day cultivation period in anaerobic bags, individual colonies were isolated and streaked onto M10 agar medium to obtain pure cultures. Strains were identified through colony PCR utilizing the primers 27 F and 1492R. PCR amplification was performed with the following parameters: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 10 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, concluding with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min, and subsequently maintained at 4°C. PCR products underwent sequencing at Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. The identification of 16S rRNA gene sequences to genus level was conducted using the NCBI BLASTn algorithm. The sequences were aligned and compared using the Distance model of MEGA 11 to eliminate redundancy. A neighbor-joining tree was constructed using the Tamura–Nei model with 1000 bootstrap replicates in MEGA 11 [ 41 ], incorporating type species of the same genus. If the similarity of a 16S rRNA gene sequence to a known type strain is below 98.65%, the isolate may present a new species. Screening of rumen strains The CMC-Na agar medium comprised 10.0 g/L CMC-Na, 2.0 g/L KH2P04, 1.4 g/L (NH4)2S04, 0.3 g/L MgS04, 0.3 g/L CaCl2, 1.0 mL/L trace nutrient solution, 1.0 g/L cysteine, 100 mL/L cell-free rumen fluid, and 10.0 g/L agar [ 42 ], and was utilized to screen rumen strains exhibiting cellulase activity. The isolates were cultured in anaerobic BHI liquid medium and subsequently purified on BHI agar medium. The pelleted cells of each strain were spot-inoculated onto CMC-Na agar medium, incubated anaerobically at 39 °C for 72 h, and subsequently stained with 1 mg/mL Congo red solution for 10 min [ 43 ]. Following a 15-min washing with a 1 mol/L saturated NaCl solution to remove excess dye, strains displaying transparent circles were recognized to be potential fiber degraders. The diameters of transparent circles (d) surrounding bacterial colonies were measured with precision using a vernier caliper for quantitative screening. Strains exhibiting transparent circles with a diameter greater than 9 mm were cultured in modified BHI liquid medium, utilizing wheat bran (Solarbio) as the exclusive carbon source, at 39 °C with continuous agitation at 150 rpm for a duration of 5 days. The concentration of reducing sugars in the culture supernatants was quantified using the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method [ 44 ]. In vitro single-strain fermentation experiment The experiment utilized a completely randomized design with four replications for each treatment. Glass bottles (100 mL) were prepared with 0.75 g of ground TMR substrate (Table S4), 45 mL of pre-warmed fermentation medium (Table S5), and 15 mL of filtration-sterilized rumen fluid. Each bottle was inoculated with 1 mL of 1 × 107 CFU/mL of the following strains: B. subtilis 5632, S. equinus 5640, S. pluranimalium 5823, S. infantarius 6074, L. delbrueckii 5812, P. mucosa 6360, S. condimenti 5845, E. gallinarum 6392, A. glycerinitolerans 6345, E. lactis 6088, C. pasinlerensis 5847, L. pontis 5897, and P. acetatigenes 6076. All bacterial suspensions underwent centrifugation (12,000 × g, 10 min) to remove the culture medium, after which the resulting cell pellets were resuspended in an equivalent volume of sterile water. 1 mL of sterile water was utilized as the negative control (CON). All bottles were sealed with a rubber stopper and aluminum crimp cap immediately after loading and were incubated at 39 °C for 48 h [ 45 ]. Gas production was measured at 6, 9, 12, 24, and 48 h of incubation using a 50-mL syringe. After 48 h of incubation, fermentation was halted, and pH was assessed using a calibrated pH meter (MP512-03, Precision Instrument, Shanghai, China). The fermentation fluid was then filtered using 300-mesh nylon bags to isolate filtrate from the residual substrate. The concentrations of NH3-N were assessed by combining 3 mL of filtrate with 1 mL of 25% (w/v) metaphosphoric acid, while gas chromatography was employed to analyze the concentrations of volatile fatty acids (VFAs), including acetate, propionate, butyrate, isobutyrate, valerate, and isovalerate. The dry matter (DM) was analyzed according to method 930.15, using procedures established by the Association of Official Analytical Chemists [ 46 ]. The neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and acidic detergent fiber (ADF) content was determined using a Fibretherm Fibre Analyzer (Gerhardt, Bonn, Germany) [ 47 ]. The filtered residue was subjected to drying at 65 °C for 48 h, after which it was weighed to determine in vitro dry matter degradation (DMD). The NDF and ADF content in the dried residues was determined to calculate in vitro NDF degradation (NDFD) and ADF degradation (ADFD). Constructing SynComs In light of the relationships between rumen microbiota and fiber degrading efficiency (Table S6 and S7), four SynComs were developed based on the abundance patterns derived from sequencing data, featuring highly efficient fiber degraders. Each bacterial suspension was prepared, and 1 mL of bacterial suspension from SynCom 1 to 4 was inoculated into the in vitro fermentation systems as previously described. Sterile water served as the negative control (CON). Rational design was employed to ensure the authenticity of SynComs. A three-factor Box–Behnken experimental design comprising 41 combinations was used to assess the contribution of each strain [ 48 , 49 ], using the fiber degradation rate as the response variable (Table S8). Whole genome sequencing The fiber degrading strains S. equinus 5640, L. delbrueckii 5812, P. acetatigenes 6076, E. lactis 6088, and P. mucosa 6360 had whole genome sequencing at Biozeron. Genomic DNA from the five strains was extracted from cell pellets using a Bacteria DNA Kit (OMEGA) following the manufacturer’s instructions and quantified with a TBS-380 fluorometer (Turner BioSystems, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). A highly qualified DNA sample (OD260/280 = 1.8 ~ 2.0, > 6 μg) was utilized to construct the fragment library. The raw paired end reads were trimmed and quality controlled by Trimmomatic [ 50 ], with parameters set to (SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 MINLEN:75). Clean data were assembled using the ABySS 2.2.0 [ 51 ] with multiple-Kmer parameters and GapClosersoftware [ 52 ]. Mining the sequenced data Bacterial community The rarefaction analysis using Mothur v.1.21.1 [ 53 ] was conducted to reveal the diversity indices of rumen microbiota, specifically the Observed_species and Shannon diversity indices. The beta diversity analysis was performed using UniFrac [ 54 ] to compare the results of the principal component analysis (PCA), conducted with the community ecology package R-forge (Vegan 2.0). Venn diagrams were used to illustrate the common and distinct elements among groups using the website ( http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/links ). The Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States (PICRUSt) program, utilizing the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database, was used to predict the functional changes in microbiota across various samples. Data visualization was performed using the GraphPad Prism software (version 10.2.0, San Diego, CA, USA). Bacterial strains The diversity of bacteria and the correlation analysis of cultured bacteria were examined using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method to construct a phylogenetic tree within the culturomics approach. The 16S rRNA alignment was generated and trimmed using MEGA11. Branches were assessed for robustness through bootstrap analysis with 1000 replicates. The online tool iTOL ( http://itol.embl.de ) [ 55 ] and Excel version 16.78 were used for the display, manipulation, and annotation of phylogenetic trees. Gene models were identified using GeneMark version 4.17 [ 56 ] ( http://topaz.gatech.edu/GeneMark/ ). Subsequently, all gene models were subjected to blast analysis against the non-redundant (NR) database from NCBI, as well as the KEGG, COG, and CAZy databases to do functional annotation by the blastp module. In situ experiment The experiment was conducted in October 2025 at the Changping Research Station of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Beijing, China). All experimental procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Six Huaxi cattle with permanent rumen fistula were used in this trial. The animals were fed a basal diet consisting of corn silage (42%), alfalfa (12%), and premixes (46%), and were randomly assigned into two groups: a control group (CON), which received 500 mL of sterile water, and a treatment group (TRE), which received 500 mL of mixed bacterial suspension. The bacterial suspension contained S. equinus 5640 and E. lactis 6088 (1 × 109 CFU/mL each), P. mucosa 6360 (1 × 107 CFU/mL), and L. delbrueckii 5812 and P. acetatigenes 6076 (1 × 106 CFU/mL each). The concentrations of the mixed bacterial suspension were formulated based on the results of a three-factor Box-Behnken design experiment, which determined the optimal ratio for synergistic effects. Bacterial suspensions were prepared by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellets were resuspended in an equivalent volume of sterile water. Cattles were fed once daily at 18:00 and had free access to drinking water throughout the study. The experiment lasted for 24 days, comprising a 7-day feed adaptation period without any bacterial supplementation, followed by a 17-day bacterial adaptation period during which the CON and TRE groups received sterile water and the miced bacterial suspension, respectively. In the last 3 days of the experiment, the nylon bag method was used to evaluate the degradation characteristics of the diet. Prior to incubation, each nylon bag was numbered, weighted, and filled with approximately 3.00 g dried and ground diet. For each animal at each time point, three nylon bags were deployed in the rumen via the rumen fistula, resulting in a total of nine replicates per treatment group (3 bags × 3 animals). Nylon bags were retrieved at 12, 24, 48, and 72 h after incubation, rinsed with cold water until the runoff was clear, and then dried at 65 °C for 48 h before re-weighing. After drying and weighing, residual samples from three nylon bags of the same sample from each cattle at the same time point were pooled together for determination of DM [ 46 ], NDF, and ADF [ 47 ]. The degradation rate and at each time point was calculated based on these measurements, following the same methodology as described in the In vitro single-strain fermentation experiment. Rumen fluid samples (50 mL each) were collected via the fistula at the same time points, immediately filtered through four layers of sterile gauze for pH measurement, and stored at – 20 °C for analysis of VFAs and NH3-N concentration. Statistical analyses Statistical analyses of DMD, NDFD, gas production, pH, NH3-N, MCP, VFAs were performed using the Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) in R (v. 4.3.1), with a significance level of P < 0.05. Turkey’s multiple comparison test was used to examine the significance of treatments. A two-way ANOVA was applied to evaluate the effects of Treatment and Time on the measured parameters, including DMD, NDFD, and ADFD degradation rates, rumen fluid pH, VFA, and NH3-N concentrations. This analysis was conducted in R (v. 4.3.1) utilizing the stats package, with a significance level of P < 0.05. Cluster analysis was performed using the cluster package in R (v. 4.3.1), with results visualized through ggplot2 and ggrepel packages to optimize label positioning. The R (pheatmap package) was applied to visualize the relationships between fermentation targets and nutrients’ degradation rate via correlation heatmap diagrams. Bar plots and bubble plots were generated using the website ( https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn ), an online platform for data analysis and visualization [ 57 ]. Results Composition and structure of the rumen microbiota The 16S rRNA gene was amplified and sequenced to investigate the changes in bacterial composition during enrichment, utilizing fresh and cultured samples collected on days 7, 14, and 28. After quality trimming, microbial profiling produced 514,704 effective circular consensus sequences (CCSs). A total of 21,496 ASVs were generated across all 16 samples. A total of 738 species were identified, categorized into 335 genera, 131 families, 68 orders, 41 classes, and 23 phyla, using the RDP Classifier based on 16S rRNA database. The observed species count in cultured samples significantly decreased from the D0 group to the D14 group, followed by an increase in the D28 group (Fig.  1 A). The Shannon index in the D0 group was significantly elevated compared to the other three groups (Fig.  1 B). PCA analysis based on UniFrac distance showed that enrichment time had a significant effect on the beta diversity of rumen fluid (P < 0.001, Fig.  1 C). Furthermore, novel and distinct ASVs were identified from various stages of enrichment (Fig.  1 D). Fig. 1 Bacterial communities of four rumen fluid samples determined by the PacBio platform. H Relative abundance of the top 10 genera. I Relative abundance of the top 10 species. D0, D7, D14, and D28 are samples collected at day 0, 7, 14, and 28 of enrichment, respectively. Asterisks and different letters indicate significant differences at P < 0.05 level The rumen bacterial communities showed dynamic changes during enrichment at both the phylum and family levels (Fig.  1 E, F). Bacteroidetes was the most abundant phylum in the D0 group (0.45), but declined sharply by D7 (0.15), followed by a slight increase at D14 (0.17) and a further decrease by D28 (0.13, Fig.  1 G). In contrast, Firmicutes peaked on D7 (0.57) and dropped down to the lowest level by D28 (0.21). Conversely, Proteobacteria increased progressively, reaching predominance on D28 (0.61, Fig.  1 G). At the genus level, the relative abundance of Prevotella, Parabacteroides, Lawsonibacter, Christensenella, and Butyrivibrio constantly decreased. In contrast, the relative abundance of Streptococcus, Klebsiella, Escherichia, and Enterobacter significantly increased and became the dominant taxa in cultured rumen fluid (Fig.  1 H). Notably, Klebsiella spp. (K. pneumoniae and K. aerogenes) exhibited a gradual but sustainable increase from D0 to D28 (Fig.  1 I). KEGG pathway enrichment of the rumen microbiota The rumen microbiota demonstrated functional dominance across enrichment periods, despite consistent dynamic shifts in bacterial composition. Functional prediction using PICRUSt2 (KEGG database) revealed that “metabolism” constituted the predominant pathway at Level 1, followed by “genetic information processing”, “environmental information processing”, “cellular processing”, “human disease”, and “organismal systems” (Fig.  2 A). At KEGG level 2, the “metabolism” pathway exhibited significant enrichment in “carbohydrate metabolism” by day 28 (P < 0.05, Fig.  2 B). Further analysis at KEGG level 3 identified nine “carbohydrate metabolism” pathways, with seven significantly enriched by days 14 and 28 (P < 0.05, Fig.  2 C). The D14 group was associated with “ascorbate and aldarate metabolism”, “butanoate metabolism”, and “glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism”, while the D28 group showed enrichment in “amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism”, “fructose and mannose metabolism”, “galactose metabolism”, and “glycolysis/gluconeogenesis”. Several genera demonstrated significantly positive correlations with carbohydrate degradation functions, suggesting their potential role in the fiber degradation process (Fig.  2 D). These included Aerococcus, Butyrivibrio, Enterobacter, Enterococcus, Escherichia, Eubacterium, Exiguobacterium, Klebsella, Pryramidobacterm, and Streptococcus. Furthermore, correlation analysis at the species level reinforced this functional link, identifying key contributors such as Aerococcus urinaeequi, Butyrivibrio spp. (B. proteoclasticus, and B. sp000526935), Eisenbergiella sp900066775, Enterobacter oligotrophicus, Enterococcus faecalis, Escherichia spp. (E. dysenteriae, and E. flexneri), Eubacterium limosum, Exiguobacterium profundum_A, Kiritimatiella glycovorans, Klebsella spp. (K. aerogenes, and K. pneumoniae), Massilibacteroides vaginae, Phascolarctobacterium faecium, Prevotella ruminicola, and Streptococcus spp. (S. equinus, and S. lutetiensis) (Fig.  2 E). Of them, Enterococcus exhibited positive correlations with “amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism”, “glycolysis/gluconeogenesis”, “fructose and mannose metabolism”, and “pentose phosphate” (P < 0.05, Fig.  2 D). Streptococcus was associated with “glycolysis/gluconeogenesis”, “pyruvate metabolism”, “galactose metabolism”, and “C5-Branched dibasic acid metabolism” (P < 0.05, Fig.  2 D). The predominant phylum identified was Firmicutes, comprising 792 ASVs and 56 species, accounting for 71.79% of the total. In situ efficacy of the rumen fiber-degrading SynCom in cattle The in situ feeding trial demonstrated that the supplementation of the SynCom significantly altered the rumen fermentation profile and enhanced feed fiber degradation in Huaxi cattle. The ruminal degradation rates of DM, NDF, and ADF exhibited a positive trend in the TRE group, with increases of 2.61%, 7.81%, and 11.47%, respectively, at 72 h compared to the CON group, although these differences were not statistically significant (Fig.  8 A–C, P > 0.05). SynCom supplementation profoundly influenced ruminal VFA production. While the total VFA concentration in the TRE group was significantly lower than in the CON group at 12 h (Fig.  8 F, P = 0.0011), it increased progressively over time, becoming significantly higher in the TRE group at 24 h (P = 0.0196) and 48 h (P = 0.0001), and remaining 10.80% higher at 72 h (P > 0.05). This shift was driven by a significantly elevated acetate concentration in the TRE group at 24, 48, and 72 h (Fig.  8 G, P < 0.05), with no significant difference observed in propionate levels. Consequently, the A:P ratio was significantly elevated in the treatment group at 48 h and 72 h (Fig.  8 M, P < 0.05). The concentrations of butyrate, isobutyrate, and isovalerate in the TRE group were also significantly higher than in the CON group at 24 and 48 h (Fig.  8 I, J, and L, P < 0.05). In contrast, valerate concentration in the TRE group was significantly lower than in the CON group at 48 h (Fig.  8 K, P = 0.0013) and 72 h (Fig.  8 K, P < 0.0001). Concomitantly, the NH3-N concentration was significantly lower in the TRE group at 48 h and 72 h (Fig.  8 E, P< 0.0001 and P = 0.0034, respectively), implying improved nitrogen utilization by the rumen microbiota. A potential decrease of rumen fluid pH was observed in the TRE group at 72 h (Fig.  8 D, P = 0.0885), consistent with the overall increase in total VFA production, while pH remained comparable between groups at other time points. CON: sterile distilled water; TRE: SynCom Discussion Rumen is an exquisitely evolved organ that harbors a community of microbial symbionts to facilitate the highly efficient bioconversion of plant biomass to nutrients [ 59 ]. To improve the carbohydrate utilization efficiency in rumen, it is necessary to understand the composition and function of rumen microbiota and undermine the action mechanisms of the key rumen microbes. This study combined PacBio sequencing and culturomics approaches to characterize the bacterial community of rumen and collect the resources of rumen fiber-degrading bacteria. As a result, artificial synthesis of the microbial community was proved to enhance the efficiency of rumen fiber degradation. Whole genome sequencing was employed to demonstrate the crucial role of the rumen microbiota in the fiber-degrading process. The enrichment culture of microbial communities enables the multiple metabolic and catabolic activities of these populations to be studied at the molecular level and explored as potential sources of biotechnological processes [ 60 ]. Our study unveiled variations in the relative abundance of rumen bacteria at distinct taxonomic levels between different enrichment days. At the phylum level, there is a high enrichment of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria, whereas Proteobacteria has barely been abundant in the fresh rumen fluid. This suggests that M10 media (Carbon: nitrogen ratio = 3:1) is capable of enriching the lower diversity of these groups. It has been reported that a few Enterobacter species have a bacterial cellulose biosynthetic mechanism, glucose metabolism, and energy metabolism under aerobic and anaerobic conditions [ 61 ]. The relative abundance of Enterobacter showed a significant increase along with the enrichment and indicated that it was positively related to butanoate metabolism, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, and inositol phosphate metabolism, which is consistent with previous research. Published studies suggested that Streptococcus equinus can degrade fungal mannans and harvest mannose for energy [ 62 ]. As anticipated, Streptococcus equinus showed a correlation with enrichment and C5-branched dibasic acid metabolism in this study. As a result, these microorganisms appear to be intrinsic candidates of rumen carbohydrates utilization. With further research and accumulation of sequencing data, the huge microbial diversity enclosed in the rumen has been gradually revealed [ 63 ]. However, due to the strict anaerobic environment and high complexity of bacterial communities in the rumen, it is often difficult to capture typically rare and unknown species through direct metagenomic sequencing [ 64 ]. In this study, the culturomics approach, which combined the direct-plating method and continuously-incubation method, greatly improved the number of low-abundance but functional bacteria from the rumen, shedding light on the undiscovered novel microbial resources in the rumen, as recently achieved when applied to the gut [ 65 ], lung [ 18 ], marine [ 21 ], and desert soils [ 22 ]. Additionally, the standard culturomics approach based on MALDI-TOF identification of colonies cannot fully capture information on all cultures [ 66 ]. As a result, a total of 896 strains from 78 bacterial species were collected and identified using colony PCR. Compared to the 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing results (738 species), the culturomics approach achieved a 10.1% species isolation rate, indicating that culturomics may have played a great role in uncovering low-abundance microbial species [ 67 ]. The diversity of species isolated through fresh rumen fluid was overall lower than that from enrichment cultivation, although a few species were specific. Some of these original compositions of species might have been inhibited in subsequent enrichment incubation, due to their inability to adapt to changed conditions during the enrichment [ 68 ]. To search for potential highly efficient fiber degradation strains, we conducted a preliminary screening using sodium carboxymethyl cellulose [ 43 ], which revealed that 45.3% of 360 rumen isolates exhibited cellulolytic activity, though only 35 strains demonstrated superior degradation (clearance diameters > 9 mm). Wheat bran is a concentrated source of insoluble fiber. Subsequent functional validation using wheat bran [ 69 ] as a glucose substitute in BHI medium identified 25 strains capable of generating reducing sugars via the DNS assay. The results indicated that Bacillus and Streptococcus exhibited the strongest degradation, with significant intraspecies variability highlighting the need for strain-level evaluation [ 70 ]. The effects of single bacterial strains on rumen fermentation characteristics and feed degradation rate were evaluated through in vitro fermentation experiments. Compared with the control group, strains A. glycerinitolerans 6345, C. pasinlerensis 5847, P. acetatigenes 6076, and S. equinus 5640 can improve rumen DM and NDF degradation efficiency, while the remaining strains have no negative effect on ruminal fermentation. Cellulose degradation in rumen is accomplished jointly by the microbial community, and the synergistic effect among different strains may affect the overall metabolic efficiency. The design of synthetic bacterial communities is usually based on specific functional requirements, such as improving host health, producing specific compounds, or achieving specific metabolic pathways [ 71 , 72 ]. Synthetic bacterial communities typically contain a variety of different bacterial species, which may coexist in the natural environment but are isolated and recombined in the laboratory for research or application [ 73 ]. 16S rRNA gene sequencing indicated the composition and diversity of microbial communities in the rumen, meanwhile showed the correlation and relative abundances of different microorganisms. Therefore, four bacterial communities (SynCom 1 ~ 4) in the rumen were finally constructed. During the 48 h in vitro fermentation process, the addition of SynComs significantly increased the gas production, indicating that SynComs greatly affected the activity of rumen microorganisms. Previous studies reported that the addition of Enterococcus [ 74 ] and Lactobacillus [ 75 ] played a crucial role in increasing the gas production of rumen in vitro fermentation. Additionally, Lactococcus lactis also showed a certain promoting effect in rumen fermentation, especially increasing the gas production in the early fermentation stage [ 76 ]. However, we are most concerned about whether different bacterial combinations can improve the nutrient digestibility of rumen in vitro fermented feed. Results indicated that SynCom 3 could significantly increase the rumen NDF degradation rate and enhance the fiber degrading efficiency compared with the control group, which may also promote the abundance and activity of dominant fiber-degrading bacteria such as Firmicutes and Bacteroides in the rumen natural flora [ 77 ]. Though the composition of SynCom 3 and SynCom 4 was the same, the only difference between these two groups was the concentration of strain P. acetatigenes 6076, and the degradation effect was better at a lower concentration. Therefore, we speculated that changing the concentration and proportion of strains in SynCom 3 could further increase the fiber degradation rate and save production costs by decreasing the concentration of strains. Response surface methodology (RSM) is a set of statistical tools used to design and model the development of experiments, evaluate the effects of different factors, and search for optimal conditions to obtain desirable responses, enabling the prediction of interactions between different process parameters [ 78 ]. Cluster results showed that more than three quarters of the communities could improve the rumen fiber degradation efficiency compared with the control group, and finally, an optimal strain community was obtained, which could increase the fiber degradation efficiency by 20.56%. The genomic analysis provided profound insights into the mechanisms of fiber degradation in the rumen by 5-strains SynCom. The high abundance of genes categorized under the COG and CAZyme databases indicated their robust genetic foundation for synergistic fiber deconstruction. This was further evidenced by the comparison with classic fibrolytic rumen bacteria, which revealed that our SynCom possesses a remarkable 47 unique GH and 19 unique CBM families. The functional potential encoded by these genes was clearly reflected in the enriched KEGG pathways and specific enzyme commissions (ECs). The fiber degradation pathways of the synthetic microbial community in the rumen were analyzed based on ECs' abundance. The subsequent conversion of glucose to pyruvate and finally to key VFAs like acetate, propionate, and butyrate is supported by the enrichment of corresponding metabolic pathways and enzymes. The present study demonstrated that the optimized SynCom enhanced fiber degradation in both in vitro and in situ conditions. The observed 20.56% increase in NDF degradation in vitro showed the direct potential of the SynCom. Although the in situ increases of NDF (7.81%) and ADF (11.47%) degradation rates were more modest, this indicated that the rumen practical environment is more complex and competitive [ 79 ]. The lower efficacy in vivo could be attributed to factors such as experimental conditions [ 80 ] or competitive exclusion by the resident rumen microbiota [ 81 ]. Shi et al. [ 82 ] reported that a SynCom comprising Lactobacillus plantarum, Bacillus subtilis, and Aspergillus niger reduced cellulose and hemicellulose contents in plant material by 18.57% and 21.64%, respectively. In another study, Liu et al. [ 83 ] demonstrated that a SynCom including Bacillus cereus, Bjerkandera adusta, Trichoderma harzianum, Cladosporium cladosporioides, and Cladosporium tenuissimum led to reductions in cellulose and hemicellulose by 26.2% and 14.3% in mulberry branches, while also significantly enhancing the activities of endoglucanase, exoglycanase, and β-glucosidase. Collectively, these studies demonstrate the significant efficacy of synthetic microbial communities in fiber degradation. The degradation of dietary cellulose by rumen microorganisms is accompanied by the production of volatile fatty acids, and probiotics including Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, and Enterococcus can improve the growth performance of animals including ruminants by increasing the digestion rate [ 84 ]. The application of Streptococcus bovis JB1 and HC5 decreased silage pH and NH3-N concentration, while improving the fermentation profile and enhancing the degradation of DM and crude protein [ 85 ]. Enterococcus lactis’s flexibility as a feed additive for livestock translates to improved productivity and health outcomes in piglets, broilers, and hens [ 86 ]. Lactobacillus delbrueckii significantly increased piglet growth performance and meat quality through modulating both intestinal microbiota and immunity in pigs [ 87 ]. Petrimonas mucosa ING2-E5AT, which was isolated from a biogas reactor sample, also had a high ability to degrade polysaccharide and joined sugar and amino acid fermentation pathways [ 88 ]. For the in vitro experiment, a potential probiotic Proteiniphilum acetatigenes GA03 made a higher production of total VFA and acetate concentration compared to the control group (P < 0.05) [ 89 ]. Therefore, the supplementation of optimized SynCom in this study effectively enhances ruminal fiber degradation in cattle. The mechanism appears to be driven by the consortium’s intrinsic fibrolytic capability, which stimulates a synergistic fermentation process resulting in elevated total VFA and acetate production. Conclusion In conclusion, this study systematically mined the rumen microbiota as well as bacterial resources with fiber degrading ability by integrating the high-throughput sequencing, culturomics, and SynCom engineering techniques. Through 16S rRNA gene sequencing, cellulase activity screening, and in vitro rumen fermentation, the core taxa of rumen bacteria and keystone strains with high fiber-degrading abilities were identified, and their correlations with the utilization rate of feed fiber were determined. Moreover, a highly efficient SynCom consisting of P. acetatigenes 6076, S. equinus 5640, L. delbrueckii 5812, E. lactis 6088, and P. mucosa 6360 was constructed, and its molecular and metabolic action mechanisms were revealed through genome sequencing. Finally, in situ experiments confirmed that the SynCom effectively improved rumen fiber degradability and fermentation profiles. These findings confirmed the microbial manipulation strategies feasible and efficient for improved metabolic functions of rumen bacterial communities and specific rumen strains. Future studies will focus on the colonization and interaction of SynCom through transcriptomic analysis and field trials. Supplementary Information
