BACKGROUND: Ecologically derived synthetic communities can provide robust plant 
benefits, yet generalizable rules for assembling multifunctional consortia 
remain limited. We hypothesized that a "top-down" community assembled from an 
ecological core would yield complementary functions and resilience superior to 
reductionist mixes.
RESULTS: We distilled an eight-member, Bacillus-dominated synthetic community 
(hereafter referred to as SynCom) from a rice-duckweed agroecosystem by 
targeting taxa consistently shared across soil, root and shoot niches. Under 
greenhouse conditions, the SynCom concurrently promoted rice growth and 
suppressed sheath blight caused by Rhizoctonia solani, reducing the final 
disease index by 70% without detectable phytotoxicity. Leave-one-member 
perturbations (-Dx), combined with untargeted LC-MS profiling and qRT-PCR of 
biosynthetic genes, revealed a division-of-labor architecture: individual 
strains specialized in auxin production, siderophore-linked iron mobilization, 
or lipopeptide/polyketide-based antagonism. These complementary yet partially 
redundant contributions mapped members, metabolite pools, plant outcomes and 
rendered community performance resilient to single-member loss. Across -Dx 
contrasts, the complete SynCom uniquely recovered the full suite of plant-growth 
metabolites (e.g., indole-3-acetic acid, acetoin/2,3-butanediol) together with 
antimicrobial chemistries (e.g., surfactin, bacillomycin, fengycin, difficidin). 
We formalize an assembly heuristic, ecological core, complementary functions, 
redundancy check, that links ecological origin to predictable, multi-trait 
performance.
CONCLUSIONS: A top-down, ecology-guided route can generate a multifunction 
SynCom with demonstrated greenhouse efficacy and mechanistic transparency. By 
coupling-member perturbations with multi-omics readouts, our study provides a 
transferable rule for building resilient plant-associated consortia and a 
tractable framework for future genetic and in-plant chemical confirmations.

Abstract Background Ecologically derived synthetic communities can provide robust plant benefits, yet generalizable rules for assembling multifunctional consortia remain limited. We hypothesized that a “top-down” community assembled from an ecological core would yield complementary functions and resilience superior to reductionist mixes. Results We distilled an eight-member, Bacillus-dominated synthetic community (hereafter referred to as SynCom) from a rice–duckweed agroecosystem by targeting taxa consistently shared across soil, root and shoot niches. Under greenhouse conditions, the SynCom concurrently promoted rice growth and suppressed sheath blight caused by Rhizoctonia solani, reducing the final disease index by 70% without detectable phytotoxicity. Leave-one-member perturbations (−Dx), combined with untargeted LC–MS profiling and qRT-PCR of biosynthetic genes, revealed a division-of-labor architecture: individual strains specialized in auxin production, siderophore-linked iron mobilization, or lipopeptide/polyketide-based antagonism. These complementary yet partially redundant contributions mapped members, metabolite pools, plant outcomes and rendered community performance resilient to single-member loss. Across −Dx contrasts, the complete SynCom uniquely recovered the full suite of plant-growth metabolites (e.g., indole-3-acetic acid, acetoin/2,3-butanediol) together with antimicrobial chemistries (e.g., surfactin, bacillomycin, fengycin, difficidin). We formalize an assembly heuristic, ecological core, complementary functions, redundancy check, that links ecological origin to predictable, multi-trait performance. Conclusions A top-down, ecology-guided route can generate a multifunction SynCom with demonstrated greenhouse efficacy and mechanistic transparency. By coupling-member perturbations with multi-omics readouts, our study provides a transferable rule for building resilient plant-associated consortia and a tractable framework for future genetic and in-plant chemical confirmations.

Introduction Rice–duckweed agroecosystems exemplify sustainable intensification in paddy cultivation, leveraging symbiotic plants to bolster crop performance. Duckweed species (Lemna, Spirodela, Wolffia, and Landoltia) have historically been co-cultivated with rice as living green manure, enriching paddies with bioavailable nitrogen and other essential nutrients [ 1 ]. Incorporating duckweed can substantially meet rice’s nitrogen requirement, significantly reducing dependence on chemical fertilizers while simultaneously improving soil organic matter and fertility [ 2 – 4 ]. In parallel, dense mats of duckweeds cover the water surface, acting as a natural mulch that suppresses weed germination [ 5 ]. For instance, introducing Lemna minor into flooded rice fields has been shown to reduce weed biomass by over 60–90% [ 4 ]. Such duckweed cover also functions as a physical barrier that diminishes ammonia volatilization and moderates water temperature [ 6 ], thereby conserving soil nutrients and water. Beyond agronomic inputs, duckweed coverage in paddy fields has been reported to reduce ammonia volatilization by acting as a physical barrier and by moderating the paddy-water microenvironment (e.g., temperature and pH), thereby improving nitrogen retention and potentially increasing nitrogen-use efficiency [ 3 ]. Field and pot studies further suggest that integrating duckweed with rice fertilization regimes can increase grain yield and economic return while reducing nitrogen loss, supporting its potential as an eco-friendly management practice under certain conditions [ 2 , 7 ]. Together, these findings underscore that coupling rice with aquatic plants confers multifaceted benefits-improved nitrogen economy, weed suppression, and water conservation, while aligning with eco-friendly farming practices. Nevertheless, the rice–duckweed composite system has long been regarded as an ecologically favourable configuration in traditional paddy agriculture. Historical and agronomic studies have shown that duckweed cover can modify the paddy water environment, influence nutrient cycling, and alter pest and disease pressure at the system level, thereby providing a rationale for exploring its associated microbiome as a potential source of beneficial strains [ 8 , 9 ]. Crucially, the rice–duckweed co-culture not only impacts abiotic factors but also shapes the paddy microbiome. Flooded rice fields represent an ecologically linked system of aquatic and terrestrial niches (soil, water, rice tissues, and duckweed) that harbor diverse microbial communities [ 10 , 11 ]. These microbiota profoundly influence plant growth, nutrition, and health, as plants recruit beneficial microbes that can fix nutrients, produce phytohormones, or antagonize pathogens [ 12 , 13 ]. In recent years, there is growing interest in harnessing plant-associated microbes as bioinoculants to improve crop productivity and stress resilience in sustainable agriculture [ 14 ]. A promising strategy is the use of synthetic microbial communities (SynComs)–defined, cultivated consortia of microbes that model or augment the natural microbiome’s functions [ 15 ]. By assembling a SynCom, researchers can dissect microbiome functions under controlled conditions and exploit synergistic interactions among community members [ 16 , 17 ]. Notably, SynCom approaches have been applied to improve plant traits such as growth promotion, nutrient uptake, and disease resistance [ 18 – 23 ]. However, most SynComs to date are reductionist in design: typically a small set of well-characterized strains combined in the lab (a “bottom-up” construction) to target specific functions [ 12 , 24 ]. Such artificially composed communities may oversimplify the native microbiome and risk omitting key interspecies interactions [ 25 ]. Indeed, translating the benefits of microbial inoculants from laboratory assays to the field has often proven inconsistent, partly because introduced strains struggle to establish in the presence of complex native communities and fluctuating field conditions [ 26 ]. An emerging consensus is that more ecologically informed SynComs — those derived from naturally co-occurring, co-adapted microbes — could offer greater functional robustness and stability in real-world applications [ 27 , 28 ]. One approach to ecological SynCom design is focusing on the core microbiome: the subset of microbial taxa consistently associated with a host across environments [ 29 , 30 ]. These core microbes are hypothesized to play essential roles in host performance and to form relatively stable communities. For example, a SynCom composed of core endophytic bacteria from peanut was able to suppress soil-borne root rot and enhance peanut yield under continuous monocropping [ 31 ]. Similarly, a defined community built from the core phloem microbiota of maize showed efficient biological nitrogen fixation within plants [ 30 ]. These successes illustrate that top-down constructed SynComs can retain beneficial emergent functions that simpler, bottom-up mixes might miss [ 32 , 33 ]. Nevertheless, few studies have applied this principle to integrated agroecosystems like rice–duckweed paddies. The rich microbial interplay in such systems, including potential exchange of bacteria between rice and duckweed hosts [ 34 , 35 ], offers an untapped reservoir of candidates for developing SynCom inoculants grounded in ecological compatibility. In parallel with SynCom advances, extensive work on individual plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) and biocontrol agents provides a knowledge base for dual-function inoculants. Bacillus species, in particular, have emerged as versatile beneficial microbes in crop systems. Bacillus spp. are Gram-positive spore-formers known for producing a wide array of secondary metabolites and enzymes that can stimulate plant growth and suppress pathogens [ 36 – 38 ]. For instance, strains of Bacillus amyloliquefaciens and related Bacilli secrete lipopeptide antibiotics that inhibit fungal pathogens, as well as phytohormones like indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) and siderophores that enhance plant nutrient uptake and development [ 39 , 40 ]. Many Bacillus spp. also trigger induced systemic resistance in plants, priming the host’s immune responses against a broad spectrum of diseases [ 41 , 42 ]. Owing to these traits, Bacillus-based bioinoculants have been tested against major rice pathogens with encouraging results. In the case of rice sheath blight, caused by Rhizoctonia solani, certain Bacillus subtilis isolates can significantly curb disease severity while improving crop yield parameters. For example, co-inoculation of two endophytic Bacillus strains reduced R. solani disease incidence in rice by 65% and increased the harvest index by 30% in a field study [ 43 ]. Sheath blight remains one of the most pernicious rice diseases globally; epidemics can cause up to 50% yield loss and effective genetic resistance in rice cultivars is lacking [ 44 , 45 ]. This necessitates alternative management strategies, and the success of Bacillus biocontrol agents in experimental trials highlights their potential as sustainable tools to combat R. solani while concurrently promoting rice growth [ 43 ]. The challenge moving forward is how to deploy such beneficial microbes in a reliable, durable manner in the field — a challenge that SynCom engineering may help address. In this study, we developed a synthetic Bacillus community (SynCom, which represents a functionally defined subset of a conventional synthetic community (SynCom)) from a rice–duckweed agroecosystem, integrating ecological microbiome insights with SynCom technology (Fig.  1 ). We isolated and screened bacteria from rice roots, shoots, duckweed fronds, and paddy soil, identifying Bacillus spp. as dominant beneficial taxa forming a core microbiome. The SynCom, composed of compatible core Bacillus isolates, was designed to suppress R. solani and enhance rice growth. We conducted in vitro assays to evaluate antagonism against R. solani and plant growth-promoting traits, alongside untargeted metabolomics to profile antimicrobial and signaling metabolites. Greenhouse experiments assessed SynCom’s impact on sheath blight and rice growth under pathogen challenge. This ecologically inspired SynCom demonstrates dual functionality, offering a blueprint for next-generation biofertilizers in sustainable rice agriculture. Samples from rhizosphere soil, bulk soil, and different rice tissues (roots, stems, leaves) were collected from rice–duckweed (AD) and rice-alone (DD) systems. (Figure created with BioRender.com) Methods Rice–duckweed co-cultivation and sample collection Two cultivation systems were established: a rice–duckweed coculture (denoted “AD”) and a conventional rice monoculture (“DD”). Rice seedlings (Oryza sativa L. cv. Nipponbare) were used in all experiments. In the AD system, rice plants were grown together with aquatic duckweed (introduced Lemna fronds covering the water surface), whereas in DD duckweed was absent. For each system, samples were collected from five compartments: rhizosphere soil, bulk (non-rhizosphere) soil, rice roots, stems, and leaves. Rhizosphere soil (ADR vs DDR) was collected by gently shaking soil adhering to roots, while bulk soil (ADS vs DDS) was sampled from the surrounding soil. Plant tissues (roots, stems, leaves) were surface-sterilized by sequential immersion in 70% ethanol for 30 s, followed by 2% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) for 2 min, and then rinsed five times with sterile distilled water. Sterility checks were performed by imprinting the final rinse onto LB agar plates. Samples were collected at 45 days after rice seedling transplantation, corresponding to the early tillering stage, which is prior to the natural infection window of Rhizoctonia solani. No R. solani inoculum was introduced into the AD/DD systems, and no sheath blight symptoms were observed in the soil or plants during this co-cultivation phase. The experiment was conducted in a controlled greenhouse. Pots were filled with paddy topsoil (0–20 cm) collected from Guangzhou, China (pH 6.4; EC 0.42 mS/cm), homogenized and sieved (2 mm), organic matter = 21.4 g/kg, total nitrogen = 1.24 g/kg, and available phosphorus = 23.8 mg/kg. Plant tissues were surface-sterilized as described above prior to downstream processing. In total, each of the ten sample types (ADR, DDR, ADS, DDS, AD (root), DD (root), ADST, DDST, ADL, DDL) was obtained in triplicate from independent pots. All samples were immediately frozen at −80 °C until DNA extraction and further processing. Notably, neither the rice-alone nor the rice–duckweed pots exhibited visible sheath-blight symptoms at the sampling stage, indicating that the observed community differences arose under non-infected baseline conditions. DNA extraction and metagenomic analysis Microbial DNA was extracted from ~0.5 g of each soil or ~0.2 g of surface-sterilized plant tissue using appropriate commercial kits (Qiagen DNeasy PowerSoil for soil, DNeasy Plant Kit for tissues). Shotgun metagenomic libraries were prepared and sequenced on an Illumina platform (NovaSeq) at a depth of ~32 Gb per soil sample and ~64 Gb per plant tissue sample. Raw reads were processed using a multi-step metagenomic pipeline. Quality control was performed using FastQC v0.11.9 [ 46 ], and adapters and low-quality bases were removed with Trimmomatic v0.39 [ 47 ]. For plant-tissue samples, host-derived sequences were removed prior to assembly by mapping all reads to the Oryza sativa L. cv. Nipponbare reference genome (IRGSP-1.0, EnsemblPlants release 56) using Bowtie2 v2.5.1 [ 48 ] in end-to-end–very-sensitive mode, retaining only unmapped reads for downstream analysis. Clean reads were assembled de novo using MEGAHIT v1.2.9 [ 49 ] with the preset meta-large assembly strategy (k-mer sizes 27–127). Taxonomic profiling of unassembled clean reads was performed with MetaPhlAn v4.0 [ 50 ] using default parameters and the mpa_vJan21 database. Relative abundance values shown in different figures and tables were derived using distinct filtering and normalization strategies tailored to their analytical purposes. Table S1 reports raw read-based relative abundances without prevalence filtering. In contrast, Table S5 summarizes genus-level taxa that passed a prevalence threshold (detected in ≥50% of biological replicates within a compartment). Fig. S1 depicts normalized relative abundance of Bacillus within filtered bacterial communities after removal of extremely low-abundance taxa (<0.01%), and therefore values are not directly comparable across these data presentations. Accordingly, Fig. S1 is intended as an illustrative summary of normalized trends rather than a quantitative comparison of absolute abundances across datasets. Functional gene prediction on assembled contigs was conducted with Prodigal v2.6.3 [ 51 ] in metagenomic mode (–meta). Predicted proteins were annotated by DIAMOND v2.1.8 [ 52 ] against KEGG, COG, and CAZy databases using sensitive mode. Biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) were identified using antiSMASH v6.1 [ 53 ] with default relaxed cluster-boundary detection. Diversity analyses were conducted in R v4.3.1. Alpha-diversity (Chao1, Shannon) was calculated with the vegan package, and beta-diversity was assessed using Bray–Curtis distances followed by Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA). Differential abundance testing between rice–duckweed and rice-alone systems was performed using DESeq2 v1.40.2 [ 54 ] on genus-level taxonomic read counts derived from shotgun metagenomic profiling (MetaPhlAn output). Raw read counts were used as input for DESeq2, followed by default size-factor normalization, dispersion estimation, Wald significance testing, and false discovery rate correction (Benjamini–Hochberg; adjusted P < 0.05 considered significant). Log2 fold-changes were shrunken using apeglm to reduce variance for low-abundance taxa. To complement DESeq2, we additionally applied LEfSe (Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size) implemented via the Galaxy interface using the following parameters: α = 0.05 for the Kruskal–Wallis test, α = 0.05 for Wilcoxon pairwise tests, and an LDA score threshold of 2.0. Taxa enriched by both DESeq2 and LEfSe were considered robust indicators of duckweed-associated microbial shifts [ 55 ]. A core microbiome was defined as taxa detected in all biological replicates of the rice–duckweed treatment (presence/absence threshold = 100%) but present in ≤1 out of 3 replicates in rice-alone controls. This combined DESeq2–LEfSe–prevalence framework was used to identify enriched compartments and guilds. Metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) of dominant Bacillus members were reconstructed using a multi-binner consensus approach. Initial binning was performed with MetaBAT2 v2.15 [ 56 ], MaxBin2 v2.2.7, and CONCOCT v1.1.0 using differential coverage profiles and tetranucleotide frequencies. A non-redundant, high-quality set of MAGs was then obtained by integrating the outputs of all three binners with DAS Tool v1.2.0 (default parameters), which selects optimal consensus bins based on scoring of completeness and contamination. MAG quality was further assessed with CheckM v1.2.2 [ 57 ]. Only MAGs with completeness ≥80% and contamination ≤5% were retained. Functional screening of MAGs targeted plant-beneficial pathways and biocontrol-associated biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs). Candidate genes were identified through a combination of: (i) KEGG Orthology assignments for auxin biosynthesis (ipdC, iaaM), ACC deaminase (acdS), and siderophore pathways (dhbACEBF, bacABCDE); (ii) annotation of NRPS and PKS clusters using antiSMASH v6.1 [ 53 ]; and (iii) cross-referencing antiSMASH-predicted clusters to experimentally validated Bacillus biocontrol metabolites (surfactin, bacillomycin D, fengycin, difficidin, bacilysin). Only NRPS/PKS clusters whose predicted products showed homology to experimentally validated antimicrobial compounds deposited in the Minimum Information about a Biosynthetic Gene cluster (MIBiG) database were classified as biocontrol-related [ 58 ]. Homology assessment was based on antiSMASH-assigned known cluster types and manual cross-referencing to curated MIBiG entries for Bacillus-associated lipopeptides and polyketides (e.g., surfactin, bacillomycin D, fengycin, difficidin, and bacilysin). To ensure safety, MAGs were screened for the absence of major virulence determinants using VFDB (updated 2023) [ 59 ]. The combination of ecological prevalence, functional potential (PGP + biocontrol BGCs), and lack of virulence factors informed the selection of the eight Bacillus strains incorporated into the synthetic community. Strain isolation and 16S rRNA identification A culture-dependent, parallelized isolation workflow was used to obtain representative bacteria from the rice–duckweed system. This approach involved the use of multiple culture media and a large number of plates processed in parallel across compartments to maximize coverage of culturable diversity. Fresh samples from each compartment (rhizosphere soil, root, stem, leaf, and duckweed fronds) were processed immediately after collection. Approximately 1 g of soil or root (or 1 mL rhizosphere suspension) and surface-sterilized segments of internal stem/leaf tissues were homogenized in sterile 0.85% saline. Serial ten-fold dilutions were spread onto multiple parallel media, Luria–Bertani (LB), Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA), and R2A, to maximize cultivable diversity. Plates were incubated at 28–30 °C for 2–3 days. Distinct colony morphotypes were imaged and triaged using a semi-automated plate-recording workflow, and unique morphotypes were picked and repeatedly streaked to purity. Across all compartments, 168 isolates were obtained. Genomic DNA from each isolate was extracted using a bacterial genomic DNA mini kit, and the full-length 16S rRNA gene was amplified with universal primers 27 F and 1492R. Amplicons were Sanger-sequenced and clustered to dereplicate clonal isolates, forming the initial “isolate library” used for downstream selection of core Bacillus members. Sanger sequencing of the nearly full-length 16S gene was performed, and sequences were compared against the NCBI GenBank database using BLAST. Multiple-sequence alignment was done using MUSCLE v3.8 [ 60 ], and phylogenetic tree construction was performed with MEGA X [ 61 ]. Isolates were assigned to genera and putative species based on the highest sequence similarity to type strains. Bacillus being the most frequently encountered genus and the only genus recovered from every sample type. From these, eight dominant Bacillus isolates were shortlisted for the synthetic community based on: (i) prevalence (recovered multiple times or from multiple compartments), (ii) membership in the in silico core microbiome (as indicated by metagenomic data), and (iii) phylogenetic distinctness (to cover a broad diversity within Bacillus). The eight selected strains were identified as Bacillus cereus (strain code D09), Bacillus pumilus (D10), Bacillus subtilis (D11), Bacillus velezensis (D12), Solibacillus sp. (D13), Gottfriedia solisilvae (formerly classified in Bacillus; D14), Bacillus altitudinis (D16), and Bacillus xiamenensis (D17). A phylogenetic tree of these isolates was constructed using 16S rRNA sequences: sequences were aligned and a neighbor-joining phylogenetic tree was generated with bootstrap support to confirm that the eight strains cluster into four clades within the B. subtilis, B. amyloliquefaciens (syn. B. velezensis), and related Bacillus species complexes. The eight strains selected represent a range of Bacillus lineages without redundancy. Screening of plant growth–promoting traits The eight Bacillus strains were screened for plant-beneficial traits under laboratory conditions. Nitrogen fixation ability was tested by the acetylene reduction assay [ 62 ]. Phosphate solubilization was assayed by inoculating strains on Pikovskaya’s agar containing insoluble tricalcium phosphate. Soluble phosphate released in liquid NBRIP medium was quantified spectrophotometrically by the molybdenum blue method [ 63 ]. Protease activity was determined by spotting cultures onto skim milk agar. Amylase activity was tested on starch agar. IAA (indole-3-acetic acid) production was measured by culturing strains in LB (with tryptophan), mixing culture supernatant with Salkowski’s reagent, and measuring absorbance at 530 nm. Siderophore production was assayed on Chrome Azurol S (CAS) agar [ 64 ]. Each assay was performed in triplicate. In vitro antagonism assays against R. solani Antagonistic activity of each isolate, and of the synthetic community (see below), against the rice sheath blight pathogen R. solani (Rhizoctonia solani AG1-IA strain Laboratory collection, originally isolated from sheath blight-infected rice plants in Guangdong Province, China) was assessed using dual-culture bioassays on agar plates. A 5-mm diameter plug of actively growing R. solani mycelium (from a 3-day-old PDA culture) was placed on one edge of a fresh PDA plate. The bacterial strain to be tested was grown overnight in LB at 28 °C with shaking, harvested at mid-log phase, and adjusted to OD₆₀₀ = 0.8 (≈1 × 10⁸ CFU mL⁻1) in sterile saline. For antagonism assays, the standardized bacterial suspension was streaked in a straight line on the opposite side of the plate (approximately 3 cm from the edge) or spotted at 10 µL per spot at multiple positions opposite the fungal plug. Plates were incubated at 28 °C for 3–5 days, and inhibition was assessed by measuring the radius of fungal growth toward the bacterial inoculum. Control plates were set up with the pathogen plug on one side and either a sterile PBS. Plates were incubated at 28 °C for 4–5 days and then evaluated for fungal growth. Fungal inhibition measurement: The radius or diameter of the R. solani colony was measured for treated versus control plates. Percent inhibition was calculated as: I n h i b i t i o n ( % ) = D control - D treated D control × 100 Dcontrol is the fungal diameter in the control plate and Dtreated is that in the presence of the bacterium. Each treatment was done in triplicate. Inhibition zones or qualitative interactions (e.g., formation of an inhibition line) were also noted. Representative dual-culture plates were photographed, and quantitative inhibition rates were plotted for all strains. All eight Bacillus isolates caused significant growth suppression of R. solani relative to controls, with varying efficacy. Growth kinetics and community compatibility To ensure the selected strains could co-exist and grow together, growth curve analyses and inter-strain compatibility tests were performed. For growth curve analysis, each Bacillus isolate was grown in liquid LB medium (shaking at 180 rpm, 28 °C) and optical density (OD₆₀₀) was measured at regular intervals. Prior to constructing the synthetic community (SynCom), a compatibility assay was performed to ensure no antagonistic interactions among the eight Bacillus strains. For this assay, each strain was individually spread (10^8 CFU/mL) over an LB agar plate. Seven sterile wells (5 mm diameter) were then bored symmetrically around the plate center, and each of the remaining seven strains (10^8 CFU/mL) was inoculated into one well. Plates were incubated at 28 °C for 2–3 days and then examined for clear zones of inhibition between strains. Synthetic community assembly and drop-out experiments To assemble the SynCom, each of the eight Bacillus isolates was first cultured independently in LB broth at 28 °C with shaking (180 rpm) until reaching mid-log phase (OD₆₀₀ = 0.8). Cells were harvested by centrifugation (4000 × g, 5 min), washed twice with sterile 0.85% saline, and resuspended to the same optical density (OD₆₀₀ = 0.8). Equal cell input ratios were achieved by mixing equal volumes (1:1:1:1:1:1:1:1) of the OD-standardized suspensions in sterile tubes. This defined equal-input mixture was used as the SynCom inoculum. Importantly, our experimental design assessed functional outputs of defined input communities; therefore, abundance dynamics during co-cultivation were not quantified. Each single-strain omission community (–Dx) was generated identically except that the corresponding strain was omitted from the mixture. Using the assembled SynCom and each of the eight dropout communities (−D09, −D10, −D11, −D12, −D13, −D14, −D16, −D17), in vitro dual-culture antagonism assays were conducted against the rice sheath blight pathogen R. solani. In each assay, a 5 mm mycelial plug of actively growing R. solani was placed at the center of a fresh PDA plate, and the bacterial treatment (SynCom or a drop-out community) was spotted on one side of the plate. Sterile water (distilled) was used as a negative control on separate plates. The plates were incubated at 28 °C for 4–5 days. After incubation, the diameter of the fungal colony was measured. Each treatment (SynCom, –D09, –D10, … –D17, and control) was tested in six independent replicates. To evaluate antagonism in planta at the tissue level, detached-organ infection assays were performed. For the detached leaf assay, healthy rice leaves were excised into ~5 cm segments and laid on moist filter paper in Petri dishes. Bacterial treatments were applied by gently pipetting 50 µL of an overnight bacterial culture (10^8 CFU/mL) onto the leaf segment and allowing it to absorb/dry for 1 h (sterile water served as a negative control, and a chemical fungicide treatment was included as a reference). A mycelial toothpick of R. solani was punctured onto the center of each leaf segment. Dishes were sealed and incubated at 28 °C with >90% relative humidity. After 3–5 days, the length of the necrotic lesion extending from the inoculum point was measured. Similarly, for the detached stem assay, segments of rice stem (approximately 4–5 cm long, taken from the lower culm) were surface-sterilized (70% ethanol, brief) and placed on moist sterile paper in containers. Bacterial treatments were applied to the stem surface (by pipetting culture around the inoculation site) and, after drying, R. solani inoculum (a toothpick with active mycelium) was affixed to the stem. After incubation (28 °C, high humidity), the length of the brown lesion on each stem segment was measured. Metabolomic profiling of community cultures (LC–MS) Untargeted metabolite profiling was performed to compare the chemical outputs of the complete SynCom versus each strain-omission community. Each community (SynCom and each –Dx) was cultured in liquid LB medium (shaking at 28 °C) for a defined period. Three independent culture replicates were grown for each community. After incubation, cultures were centrifuged (10,000 × g, 10 min) to remove cells, and the supernatants were subjected to organic extraction. An equal volume of acidified ethyl acetate (0.1% formic acid in ethyl acetate) was added to each supernatant, and the mixture was shaken vigorously to extract metabolites. The organic phase was collected and evaporated to dryness under vacuum. Dried extracts were re-dissolved in a small volume of methanol for analysis. LC–MS analysis was conducted using a high-performance LC system coupled to a high-resolution mass spectrometer (Q-TOF). A reversed-phase C18 column was used to separate metabolites, employing a gradient of water and acetonitrile (both with 0.1% formic acid). MS data were acquired in positive ion mode over an appropriate m/z range (100–1500 Da). Data processing: Raw LC–MS data were processed with XCMS v3.18.0 [ 65 ] for peak detection, retention time alignment, and feature quantification. Metabolic features (distinct m/z–retention time pairs) were putatively annotated by matching accurate masses and MS/MS fragmentation spectra against multiple public metabolite databases, including METLIN ( https://metlin.scripps.edu ), HMDB (Human Metabolome Database), MassBank, and GNPS. In addition, Bacillus-associated secondary metabolites (e.g., lipopeptides, polyketides, siderophores) were cross-referenced against published compound libraries and the curated Bacillus metabolite reference collection in antiSMASH/MIBiG. The data matrix (features × samples) was subjected to multivariate statistical analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) and/or orthogonal PLS-DA were used to visualize global metabolome differences between SynCom and –Dx. Differential metabolite analysis: For –Dx vs. SynCom, fold-changes in feature abundances were calculated, and statistical significance was determined. Features significantly less abundant in a –Dx (and/or uniquely present in SynCom) were inferred to be metabolites contributed by the omitted strain. Key metabolites of interest (antimicrobials, phytohormones) were further confirmed by comparing to authentic standards when available. Metabolites were categorized into functional classes, and pathway enrichment analysis was done by mapping identified compounds to metabolic pathways. The combined metabolomic profile of SynCom was thus dissected to assign specific metabolites (or classes of metabolites) to their producer strains within the community. Composite contribution scores reflected phenotypic dependency of metabolite production on each strain’s presence, and were not inferred from bacterial abundance or growth dominance within SynCom or –Dx cultures. For each strain, the functional loss caused by its removal was calculated as the relative decrease in the summed z-scores of key PGP and pathogen suppression traits. These values were normalized and aggregated into category-specific indices (PGP and biocontrol), as well as an overall composite index combining both traits. Contribution proportions were visualized as pie charts (growth promotion, disease suppression, total contribution). Gene expression analysis by qRT-PCR To evaluate the expression of genes related to key metabolites, quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed. RNA was extracted from bacterial cultures using a commercial kit (TIANGEN RNAprep Pure Cell/Bacteria Kit, DP430, Beijing, China), and 1–2 μg total RNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA. qRT-PCR reactions were set up with SYBR Green master mix and gene-specific primers for target genes involved in IAA or siderophore biosynthesis, as well as reference (housekeeping) genes. Amplifications were run on a real-time PCR instrument (ABI 7500). Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Each reaction was done in technical triplicate for three biological replicates per treatment. To visualize the integrative role of each SynCom strain in the consortium’s composite functionality, we constructed a bipartite network linking strains (nodes) to key functional traits. Associations were based on criteria combining qRT-PCR and metabolomic profiles: a trait that was connected to a strain led to a >60% decrease in the corresponding metabolite abundance or gene expression relative to SynCom. The final network included 8 strain nodes and 20 functional nodes (11 metabolites and 9 biosynthetic genes). Network visualization was performed using Cytoscape v3.9.1 [ 66 ] with a force-directed layout and modular shading by function class (PGP vs. biocontrol). Plant growth-promotion and disease suppression assays Rice seed germination and seedling growth bioassay Prior to extensive plant trials, a laboratory bioassay was conducted to evaluate the safety and growth-promotion effect of the SynCom on rice seed germination and early seedling development. Rice seeds (Oryza sativa L. cv. Nipponbare) were surface-sterilized by sequential washes in 70% ethanol (1 min) and 2% sodium hypochlorite (5 min), followed by thorough rinsing in sterile water. Sterile seeds were split into treatment groups (three replicates of 20 seeds each). Treatments were: SynCom inoculum (10^8 CFU/mL, in 0.85% saline), Water control (sterile 0.85% saline), and Fungicide control (50 mg/L validamycin solution). Seeds were soaked in the treatment solution for 4 h at room temperature with gentle agitation. Dishes were incubated in a growth chamber at 28 °C with a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. Germination scoring: After 5 days, the number of germinated seeds (visible radicle protrusion) was counted. Emerging seedlings were allowed to grow until 7 days post-imbibition, at which point their primary root length and shoot length were measured. Seedlings were also observed for any signs of discoloration, deformity, or disease. Greenhouse pot experiment for growth promotion and sheath blight control A 90-day greenhouse trial was conducted to test the efficacy of the SynCom in promoting rice growth and protecting against sheath blight disease under more realistic conditions. Plant material and growth conditions: Rice seeds were germinated in sterile vermiculite, and uniform healthy seedlings (4 weeks old) were transplanted into plastic pots (Length* Width* Height, 50 cm*40 cm*25 cm) containing 20 kg of autoclaved paddy soil. Each pot contained 8 rice plants. Pots were kept in a greenhouse with 28 °C day/25 °C night temperatures, 70% relative humidity, and natural light supplemented to 12 h photoperiod. Plants were allowed to establish for 45 days before treatments. Inoculation with SynCom: For the treatment group, each pot was inoculated with the synthetic community by drenching the soil around the base of the rice plant with 10 mL of SynCom suspension (~10^8 CFU/mL). The suspension was allowed to soak into the root zone. Control pots received an equivalent volume of sterile LB medium. No chemical fertilizers or other amendments were added to observe the effect of microbial treatment. Pathogen challenge: One week after SynCom application, plants were challenged with R. solani to induce sheath blight. Place a toothpick colonized by R. solani mycelium punctured at the base of each rice plant, near the ground on the stem. The fungus was allowed to infect under high humidity; pots were kept well-watered, maintaining flooded conditions about 5 cm above soil which favor sheath blight development. Experimental design: The trial had two groups (SynCom-treated vs. control), each with 24 plants. Within each group, half the plants were used for growth analysis and half for disease scoring. Disease assessment: Plants were monitored over time for sheath blight symptoms. Disease incidence was recorded at 15, 30, and 45 days post-inoculation. Lesion size and spread on each plant were also noted. At the final evaluation (45 days), a disease severity index was calculated for each plant based on the proportion of the culm circumference and plant height covered by lesions (using a standard rating scale for rice sheath blight). These ratings were converted into a percentage disease index. Growth and physiological measurements: After 45 days after SynCom inoculation, all plants were harvested to measure growth promotion parameters. Plant height and stem diameter were measured. Tillers per plant were counted. Roots were excavated and cleaned of soil. Clean root systems were spread and scanned using an EPSON flatbed scanner; the digital images were analyzed with WinRHIZO software to obtain total root length and total root surface area. Shoots and roots were oven-dried at 70 °C for 48 h and weighed to determine shoot dry weight and root dry weight. In addition, the top fully expanded leaves were taken at final harvest for pigment analysis: chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids were extracted from a fresh leaf punch in 80% acetone, and absorbances at 663, 645, and 470 nm were measured to calculate pigment concentrations (mg/g fresh tissue). For multi-group data, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied followed by post hoc Tukey’s HSD test for pairwise comparisons when ANOVA indicated a significant effect (P < 0.05). Repeated-measures data were analyzed with two-way ANOVA, with time and treatment as factors, and Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Results Microbial taxonomic diversity and compositional shifts under the rice–duckweed system We observed significant differences in microbial community diversity and composition between the rice–duckweed and the rice-alone across all sampled (Fig.  2 ). At the sampling stage, no agronomic traits (e.g., plant height, biomass, or disease symptoms) were quantified for the rice–duckweed versus rice-alone systems; therefore, the present analyses focus exclusively on microbiome differences rather than direct growth or disease outcomes attributable to duckweed addition. Alpha diversity was generally higher under the rice–duckweed system, particularly in root-associated compartments. Notably, the rhizosphere (ADR vs. DDR) and root (AD vs. DD) microbiomes in the duckweed treatment showed significantly greater species richness than in rice-alone (Fig.  2 a, b). In contrast, bulk soil (ADS vs. DDS) communities retained high diversity in both systems with no significant difference, and the stem (ADST vs. DDST) and leaf (ADL vs. DDL) endospheres had the lowest diversity overall (Fig.  2 a, b). These results indicate that co-cultivation with duckweed can elevate microbiome diversity in soil and root habitats. Beta diversity analysis further demonstrated distinct community shifts under the rice–duckweed (Fig.  2 a, b). Rhizosphere and root microbiomes from the duckweed co-culture diverged markedly from those in rice-alone, indicating a significant effect of duckweed presence on community structure. Notably, neither the rice-alone nor the rice–duckweed pots exhibited visible sheath-blight symptoms at the sampling stage, indicating that the observed community differences arose under non-infected baseline conditions. Rice–duckweed co-cultivation thus appears to promote a tiered microbial arrangement: belowground communities enriched in Bacillus and other rhizosphere-associated taxa, and aerial endosphere communities harboring Sphingomonas, Pseudomonas, and additional endophytes. Such stratification is consistent with differentiated ecological roles across compartments and may underlie the enhanced pathogen suppression observed in later assays. Fine-scale functional profiling of virulence-factor categories and compartment-resolved pathway mapping (Fig.  3 e, f and Table S2) revealed that duckweed co-cultivation assembled a functionally coherent belowground guild, but this guild was not dominated by any single genus. Culture-based mining of the rice–duckweed microbiome yields eight Bacillus isolates with complementary plant-beneficial traits Parallelized culturing across all soil and plant compartments (workflow, Fig.  4 a) recovered 168 bacterial isolates. Consistent with the metagenomic core analysis, Bacillus was the most frequently retrieved genus across all cultured compartments, reflecting its high prevalence and persistence in the taxonomic profiles (Fig.  2 e–h). To connect the culture-dependent and culture-independent datasets, we used a two-step procedure to identify candidate strains for SynCom construction. First, we restricted selection to isolates whose 16S rRNA sequences matched the Bacillus contigs present in the duckweed-associated core microbiome (Table S5). Second, we compared these isolates with the Bacillus MAGs recovered from the metagenomic data (Table S6): species-level assignments, predicted biosynthetic gene clusters (NRPS, PKS, siderophore, and RiPP pathways), and functional markers (ipdC/iaaM, acdS, dhb, bacA) were used to confirm that cultured representatives carried the same hallmark traits as their metagenomic counterparts. In practice, this meant that isolates were not selected solely because they belonged to Bacillus, but because they matched the dominant metagenomic Bacillus lineages in both taxonomy and functional potential. This integrated matching ensured that the assembled SynCom was composed of strains that were ecologically persistent in the rice–duckweed system and that reproduced the key BGCs and growth-promotion pathways detected in the metagenomic core. SynCom suppresses R. solani in a dose-dependent manner without compromising rice germination and early seedling growth SynCom fermentation broth inhibited Rhizoctonia solani in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig.  6 a). Based on metabolite profiles, SynCom clustered separately from all dropout communities, indicating that the presence of all member strains produces a unique and more complex metabolite (Fig.  7 a). The SynCom metabolome encompassed a broader diversity of compounds relative to any dropout, suggesting that each removed strain accounted for specific metabolic contributions absent in its absence. Notably, several metabolites were detected exclusively in the complete SynCom and were markedly reduced or undetectable in communities lacking the corresponding producer strain, underscoring the complementary metabolic functions provided by each community member. This overall comparison highlights that the complete SynCom integrates metabolic outputs from all strains into a distinctive profile that cannot be achieved by partial communities. Primary metabolites, such as amino acids and organic acids, were abundant, alongside numerous secondary metabolites, including phytohormones and antibiotics. KEGG pathway mapping of these metabolites showed enrichment in pathways relevant to plant-associated functions (Fig.  7 c). Many SynCom-derived compounds mapped to metabolic pathways involved in plant growth promotion and pathogen defense. The functional annotation, therefore, reflects a dual role of the SynCom metabolome, covering both growth-related metabolism and biocontrol-related metabolism. These results indicate that the ensemble of metabolites produced by SynCom spans multiple functional categories, aligning with its role in supporting plant health through diverse biochemical pathways. Pairwise comparisons of metabolite levels between the full SynCom and each strain-knockout community (–D_x) identified numerous differentially accumulated metabolites (Fig.  7 d). Volcano plot analyses revealed that dozens of compounds were significantly enriched in SynCom relative to communities missing a given strain (P < 0.05). Conversely, a smaller set of metabolites showed higher abundance in certain dropout communities than in SynCom, indicating compensatory metabolic shifts when a strain is absent. Each SynCom vs. –D_x comparison highlighted specific metabolites whose presence depended on the missing strain. For example, annotation-guided inspection identified a set of putative lipopeptide-class features, including surfactin-, fengycin-, iturin- and bacillomycin-like signals, whose abundances were reduced in –D14 compared with the full SynCom (Table S3). Because volcano plots in Fig.  7 d display unlabeled metabolite features, the identities of these lipopeptide-related signals are supported by MS-based annotation rather than visual inspection alone. Similarly, excluding the putative auxin-producing strain resulted in a sharp decrease of IAA levels relative to SynCom. In general, the full SynCom showed higher concentrations of key plant-associated metabolites than any dropout, confirming that each strain contributes uniquely to the metabolite pool and that the complete community maximizes the production of beneficial metabolites. To resolve the functional architecture of the SynCom metabolome, identified compounds were grouped into PGP and disease-suppression categories (Fig.  7 e, f). Within the PGP module, SynCom exhibited the highest relative abundances of auxin- and growth-associated metabolites such as IAA, gibberellin A43, zeatin, L-gluconic acid, citric acid and lactic acid. Single-strain deletions produced distinct but moderate shifts rather than complete losses. In –D10, multiple auxin-related metabolites showed the strongest declines (e.g., IAA = 0.5; gibberellin A43 = −1.5; abscisic acid = −1.1), whereas –D16 showed the greatest reductions in IAA and several organic acids (IAA = −1.3; citric acid = −0.3; lactic acid = −0.3). Quantitative analysis of four representative PGP metabolites (Table S4) supported these patterns: ILA, acetoin, 2,3-BDO and bacillibactin were modestly reduced across most dropouts (0.85–0.98), with the largest reductions in –D14 and –D16. No single strain deletion reproduced the full PGP signature of SynCom, indicating distributed but only partially redundant metabolic contributions among community members. A different structure emerged in the disease-suppression metabolome. SynCom contained high levels of lipopeptides (surfactin, fengycin A, bacillomycin D), dipeptide antibiotics (bacilysin) and diverse volatiles, but all dropouts introduced characteristic deficits. The largest and most widespread reductions occurred in –D11 and –D12 (e.g., surfactin = −0.8 to −1.5; bacillomycin D = −1.8; N-ethyl­aniline = −1.7; 2-naphthylamine = −1.6), while –D10 and –D14 selectively diminished phenylethanol, 1,3-butanediol and several aromatic amines (−1.2 to −1.5). These metabolite-specific reductions map each compound to a principal producer strain, generating a finely partitioned division of labour. Collectively, the PGP and antimicrobial heat-maps reveal that SynCom's full metabolic breadth requires the simultaneous activity of all members: some pathways are partially redundant (PGP organic acids and vitamins), whereas antimicrobial pathways are sharply strain-specific. Loss of any strain therefore removes a distinct functional module from the community metabolome, underscoring the non-substitutable contributions that enable SynCom to sustain both growth promotion and pathogen suppression. Quantitative partitioning of functional output revealed marked, task-specific asymmetry among SynCom members (Fig.  7 g). For plant-growth promotion, three strains, D16 (15.3%), D17 (15.3%), and D09 (14.4%), collectively accounted for more than 45% of the total growth-promotion index, whereas the low-abundance strains D12 and D13 each contributed <12%. A different hierarchy emerged for pathogen suppression: D09 provided the single-largest share (25.2%), followed by D12 (20.5%), D10 (14.5%), and D16 (11.4%), confirming that biocontrol potency is concentrated in a partially overlapping, but not identical, subset of strains. When the two functional dimensions were integrated into an overall contribution score, D09 (17.9%), D10 (14.1%), and D16 (14.0%) remained the dominant contributors, yet every strain retained a measurable share, indicating that no single member is functionally dispensable for the consortium’s composite performance. This quantitative dissection underscores a modular division of labour in which individual strains differentially weight growth-promoting versus disease-suppressive outputs, collectively generating the multifunctional phenotype of SynCom. Expression profiling of 23 PGP loci revealed a highly modular and strain-specific transcriptional architecture (Fig.  7 h). Across the full SynCom, auxin biosynthesis genes (ipdC and iaaM) remained at baseline levels, but both genes dropped sharply and uniquely in the –D16 community (0.10–0.22), confirming that D16 is the sole auxin-competent member of the consortium. In contrast, nitrogen-fixation genes (nifH, nifD, nifK) maintained full expression in all treatments except –D11, where their transcription collapsed to ≈0.10, indicating that D11 is exclusively responsible for nif-operon expression. For other PGP pathways, including those related to phosphate turnover (cps, gcd, ribA), lipopeptide precursors (srfAA, fenA, bacA), and siderophore or stress-response genes (dhbA, chiA, mlhA, sacB), expression levels remained largely stable (0.90–1.10) across most single-strain deletions. These flat profiles indicate broad functional redundancy, with no substantial loss of transcription when any single non-auxin/non-nitrogen-fixing strain was removed. Together, these patterns demonstrate a “division-of-labour” architecture in which only two strains, D16 (auxin biosynthesis) and D11 (nitrogen fixation), show irreplaceable transcriptional roles, whereas the remaining PGP traits are shared across multiple community members. A complementary heat-map of eight stress-response loci (Fig.  7 i) revealed that community-level resilience against oxidative, nitrosative, and thermal stress is governed by a set of broadly distributed genes rather than by single dominant strains. Across the full SynCom, detoxification-related genes, including nos (NO detoxification), nirK (nitrite/NO reduction), katA (catalase), sodA (superoxide dismutase), and ahpC (alkyl hydroperoxide reductase), were expressed at uniformly high levels (≈1.0). Their transcriptional profiles remained stable in all single-strain deletions except –D10 and –D11, where several loci showed moderate decreases (nos, nirK, katA, sodA, ahpC = 0.10–0.18), indicating that D10 and D11 contribute disproportionately to oxidative and nitrosative stress protection. Heat-shock and osmoprotection genes (groEL, treS, otsA) followed a similar pattern: expression stayed near SynCom levels in most dropouts but declined in –D11 (0.10–0.12) and –D12 (0.10–0.12), suggesting that stress-tolerance capacity is partially shared but not entirely redundant. Overall, the stress-response heat-map depicts a distributed and partially overlapping functional network, in which most SynCom members contribute to redox homeostasis and protein stability, while D10, D11, and D12 represent key nodes whose removal reduces the community’s cumulative stress-mitigation potential. These patterns complement the metabolic and phenotypic assays, indicating that SynCom’s protective effect on the host is not reliant on a single antimicrobial pathway but rather on a broad, multi-strain stress-buffering framework that enhances environmental robustness and suppresses pathogen establishment indirectly. Decoding the strain–gene–metabolite interaction network of SynCom The integrated network places the eight SynCom strains as source nodes feeding into two partially overlapping functional modules that converge on rice health (Fig.  7 j). Within the growth-promotion module, D16 rather than D10 emerges as the principal hub. D16 shows the strongest connections to the auxin‐associated genes (ipdC, iaaM) and to downstream metabolites including IAA, indole-3-lactic acid, acetoin, and 2,3-butanediol, all of which decline markedly when D16 is removed. D10 contributes to specific volatile-biosynthesis steps (e.g., alsS, bdhA) but does not serve as a major node in auxin production. By contrast, the disease-suppression module is centered on D14 and D17 through their lipopeptide synthetase clusters (srfAA–AD, bamA–E, fenA–E), together with D16’s polyketide pathway genes (dfnA-E, pksX). These genes feed into the antimicrobial metabolites surfactin, bacillomycin D, fengycin, and difficidin, and the resulting network is densely interconnected because these compounds share upstream precursors (branched-chain amino acids, malonyl-CoA, and fatty-acid starter units) and partially overlapping NRPS/PKS biosynthetic modules. This biochemical overlap produces correlated abundance patterns and creates functional synergy, as disruption of a single pathway often reduces flux into multiple downstream antimicrobial products. Several bridging nodes link the two modules, illustrating how nutrient acquisition and hormone production are integrated with defence outputs to support overall plant performance. Collectively, the network highlights a division-of-labour architecture: specialized strains drive distinct metabolite-gene circuits, but strategic overlaps and cross-feeding ensure resilience. This strain–gene–metabolite map therefore captures the mechanistic basis by which SynCom integrates complementary growth-promoting and disease-suppressive chemistries into a coherent, multifunctional consortium. Root architecture parameters were likewise enhanced, with total root length and root surface area rising by 146.56% and 171.94%, respectively. Discussion By assembling a SynCom sourced from a rice–duckweed co-cultivation system, we achieved the combination of enhanced rice growth and effective disease suppression. This ecology-guided strategy capitalizes on the unique microbial reservoir created by the long-standing symbiosis between paddy rice and duckweed, offering advantages that diverge fundamentally from conventional SynComs pieced together from laboratory strains. Importantly, the selection of SynCom members was guided by their prevalence and functional potential within a duckweed-associated microbial context, not by the assumption that duckweed itself is the optimal agronomic intervention. In this sense, duckweed functions as an ecological filter rather than a benchmark treatment, and the resulting SynCom represents a mechanistically defined community derived from that filtered microbiome rather than a proxy for duckweed addition. Under rice–duckweed co-cultivation, rhizosphere and endosphere bacterial richness increased markedly, including Bacillus, Pseudomonas, and other plant-beneficial genera [ 67 , 68 ]. These taxa form a shared “enrichment core” that underpins both growth promotion and pathogen defence [ 69 ]. While several ecological observations suggest that integrated rice–floating plant systems tend to exhibit reduced pathogen pressure compared with rice monoculture, the underlying causal mechanisms remain unclear and may vary across regions and species, we likewise recorded reduced pathogen invasion in the co-cultivation system. Hence, the rice–duckweed agroecosystem not only supplies nutrients and suppresses weeds, but also restructures the soil–root microbiome into a biological barrier that simultaneously stimulates plant vigour and curtails disease [ 70 ]. Consistent with this ecological context, Bacillus was significantly enriched across below- and above-ground compartments of the rice–duckweed system (Fig. S1), forming a natural core from which SynCom members were selected. Leveraging such naturally co-adapted communities provides a top-down route to SynCom construction; compared with bottom-up assemblages of individually selected strains, an ecologically derived SynCom is expected to exhibit greater functional robustness and environmental resilience [ 27 , 71 ]. The SynCom we assembled comprises eight core Bacillus strains isolated from the rice–duckweed system, and the consortium exhibits pronounced metabolic division of labour [ 72 ]. Untargeted metabolomics revealed that SynCom forms a distinct and highly complex metabolite cluster, clearly separated from any single-strain drop-out variant. In total, 427 metabolites were detected in SynCom, far exceeding the repertoire of every “single-strain-omission” community [ 73 ]. Many of these metabolites were present exclusively in the intact SynCom and were markedly reduced or lost altogether when the corresponding producer strain was removed, demonstrating that each member contributes unique metabolic functions that complement one another; only the full eight-strain consortium can generate the comprehensive metabolite portfolio. KEGG annotation and qualitative analyses further showed that SynCom metabolites map to both PGP and pathogen-antagonistic pathways. For example, IAA, siderophore, and antibiotic biosynthesis routes [ 36 , 74 ], consistent with the consortium’s dual phenotype. Comparative profiling indicated that removing any single strain eliminated a specific subset of metabolites and triggered compensatory shifts in the remainder, yet the overall abundance of plant-beneficial compounds was invariably lower than in SynCom. Hence, maximal PGP and disease-suppressive metabolites require the full eight-member assembly, underscoring the consortium’s finely tuned metabolic partitioning: each strain specializes in a discrete functional module, and all are indispensable to sustain the community’s integrated performance [ 75 ]. Importantly, the biosynthetic genes expressed by SynCom strains correspond closely to those identified in the metagenomic assemblies, indicating that the consortium recapitulates the major plant-beneficial functions naturally enriched in the rice–duckweed microbiome. Although our data clearly demonstrate strong enrichment of Bacillus in rice–duckweed systems (Fig. S1), the present study cannot fully separate whether these strains were originally introduced via duckweed thalli or were pre-existing soil inhabitants selectively favored by duckweed integration. Prior research has shown that duckweed carries a characteristic microbiome dominated by Firmicutes and Proteobacteria on its fronds, while simultaneously releasing simple carbohydrates and phenolics that enrich heterotrophic and plant-beneficial taxa in surrounding water or soil environments [ 34 ]. Both mechanisms could plausibly contribute. The consistent enrichment of Bacillus across multiple below- and above-ground compartments in our dataset suggests that duckweed acts primarily as a selective amplifier of Bacillus already present in the soil, rather than as an external inoculum source, but definitive discrimination will require microbial source-tracking or gnotobiotic reconstruction experiments. Integrated metabolomic and qRT-PCR analyses further clarified the mechanistic complementarity among SynCom members. Bacillus species are renowned for synthesizing a broad suite of secondary metabolites, including lipopeptide antibiotics, phytohormones, and siderophores [ 76 ], and qRT-PCR data confirmed a “division-of-tasks” pattern for the corresponding biosynthetic genes. In SynCom, IAA biosynthesis, phosphatase-mediated phosphate solubilization, and siderophore assembly were all abundant, removing the auxin specialist caused ipdC/iaaM expression to plunge by 75%, whereas removing other strains produced only minor reductions in phosphatase or siderophore genes, indicating partial redundancy for those traits [ 77 ]. Lipopeptide synthetase genes srfAA-AD for surfactin and bamA-E for bacillomycin were highly expressed in SynCom but dropped by 80–90% when either D14 or D17 was removed, identifying these strains as the principal cyclic-lipopeptide producers [ 78 ]. Conversely, the polyene antibiotic difficidin relied predominantly on D16, as reflected by the sharp loss of dfn/pksX transcripts in the –D16 community [ 79 ]. Each strain deletion therefore introduced a precise “gap” in the antimicrobial transcriptome, while expression of unrelated antibiotic genes remained largely unchanged, illustrating that contributions are complementary rather than redundant. Collectively, the metabolite and gene expression show that SynCom’s broad-spectrum suppression of R. solani emerges from the additive action of multiple strain-specific antibiotic pathways. Quantitative contribution analysis corroborated that every strain supplied ≥5% of the total functional output, even when present at lower relative abundance, and none could be deemed dispensable. SynCom thus operates as a modular network in which individual members specialize in distinct growth-promotion and pathogen-defence tasks, and strategic overlap between modules ensures a resilient, high-performance dual-function phenotype [ 80 ]. A feature of this work is that SynCom delivers pronounced improvements in both plant growth and disease resistance, an outcome rarely reported for synthetic communities, which have traditionally focused on either nutrient acquisition or a single stress tolerance trait [ 81 ]. Most “reductionist” SynComs are assembled from a few laboratory-screened strains; while effective under controlled conditions, they often fail to establish or persist in fluctuating field environments because they ignore the complex interactions that shape natural microbiomes [ 82 , 83 ]. By contrast, our ecology-guided, top-down approach extracted a core, co-adapted consortium directly from the rice–duckweed system, thereby capturing a broader and intrinsically synergistic functionality. In greenhouse pots, SynCom-treated plants grew taller and stouter, produced 41% more tillers, and developed root systems whose total length and surface area increased by roughly 1.5-fold; shoot height and culm diameter rose by 17% and 28%, respectively. Concomitantly, SynCom curtailed sheath-blight progression caused by R. solani: disease incidence remained at 29% vs. 92% in controls, and the final disease index fell by 70%. Thus, a single community inoculation achieved the dual goals of yield enhancement and loss mitigation. Previous attempts to deploy multi-strain inoculants have mostly targeted pathogen control alone. For example, the SynCom derived from grafted watermelon rhizospheres that protects ungrafted plants against Fusarium wilt [ 20 ] or the core-microbiome consortium assembled for biological control in maize [ 21 ]. Those studies provided limited insight into whole-plant growth benefits. In contrast, our SynCom integrates broad growth-promotion mechanisms with potent biocontrol activity and, through multi-omics, dissects the underlying metabolic division of labour and inter-strain interactions. This mechanistic depth extends current understanding of “the whole being greater than the sum of its parts” in plant microbiomes [ 75 ] and offers a new paradigm for applying synthetic biology to agriculture. Our data do not directly compare the performance of the SynCom against whole-duckweed addition, because the goals and mechanisms differ: duckweed modifies the system through physical cover, nutrient interception, shading, and broad shifts in soil–water chemistry [ 84 ], whereas SynCom is designed as a minimal, mechanistically defined inoculant that delivers targeted biochemical functions. Although duckweed-based rice co-culture systems have been associated with improved soil physicochemical properties and shifts in beneficial microbial taxa [ 85 ], direct evidence for reduced disease severity remains limited and may vary between sites and cultivars. Establishing whether SynCom matches or surpasses whole-duckweed treatment will require a dedicated side-by-side greenhouse comparison in future studies. Increasing evidence indicates that endophyte–plant interactions are strongly shaped by genotype-by-genotype (G × G) relationships. Beneficial strains such as Methylobacterium or Azospirillum have been shown to enhance growth or disease resistance in some plant accessions but not others [ 86 , 87 ], illustrating that mutualistic outcomes are not universal across host backgrounds. Because SynCom was derived from a single rice cultivar, its efficacy may reflect cultivar-specific compatibility. The strain-specific metabolic modules revealed in this study, auxin-associated functions (D16), lipopeptide pathways (D14/D17), and polyketide biosynthesis (D16), could interact differently with immune and metabolic traits in other cultivars. Future work should validate SynCom across diverse rice genotypes and identify core, cross-genotype microbial taxa to improve the translational robustness of ecology-guided synthetic communities. A limitation of the present study is that we used defined equal-input SynCom inocula without quantifying the abundance dynamics of individual strains during co-cultivation. While our –Dx perturbation approach allowed us to infer functional dependencies based on measurable phenotypes and metabolite profiles, future work incorporating strain-specific quantification (e.g., qPCR, ddPCR, or barcoding) will be needed to resolve how community composition shifts contribute to emergent functions. Additionally, this study is limited to greenhouse conditions and does not yet establish genetic necessity for the proposed metabolite-to-phenotype links. We therefore refrain from field-level claims. Immediate priorities include: (i) CRISPRi/knockout of candidate biosynthetic genes (e.g., srf/bam/fen/pks for lipopeptides; ipdC/iaaM for auxin) with complementation or metabolite add-back; (ii) in-plant targeted LC–MS/MS quantification and, where feasible, spatial imaging (e.g., MALDI-MSI) to verify on-site production; (iii) validation under non-sterile soil with native microbiomes and small-plot trials across sites/seasons. From an agronomic perspective, a SynCom-based formulation offers potential advantages over direct duckweed integration. Duckweed co-cultivation requires continuous water management, field preparation, and biomass control, and its performance varies with climatic conditions. In contrast, SynCom represents a standardized, shelf-stable inoculant with defined functions and predictable application rates, allowing farmers to obtain the microbiome-mediated benefits of the rice–duckweed system without needing to maintain duckweed biomass in the field. Rigorous field trials will be needed to determine whether SynCom can serve as a practical, lower-labor alternative in diverse agroecosystems. Supplementary Information
