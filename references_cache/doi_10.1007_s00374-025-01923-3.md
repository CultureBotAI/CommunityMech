Abstract
The possibility of establishing human colonies on the Moon or Mars depends on the ability to regenerate environmental
resources and produce food on-site. In this context, plant-based systems, such as Bioregenerative Life Support Systems,
offer a promising solution for regenerating environmental resources, primarily air and water, and producing fresh food,
partly recycling crew’s organic waste. This study investigates the role of selected consortium of plant growth-promoting
bacteria (PGPB) Azotobacter chroococcum 76A, Priestia megaterium EL5, Methylobacterium populi VP2, and Kosakonia
pseudosacchari TL13 in enhancing plant growth on Martian and Lunar regolith simulants, focusing on the effect on nutrient bioavailability in a system with zero external input aside from green compost derived from plant residues (pH 8.25 and
a low C/N ratio), using lettuce as a model plant. The compost amendment improved lettuce biomass, particularly on the
Lunar simulant (moving from 0.1 to 1.3 g plant−1), and nutrient bioavailability in all the amended substrates (+ 23% on
average). PGPB treatment enhanced leaf biomass by 35% and its mineral nutritional content (+ 26% on average), indicating
that the microbial consortium could exert positive impact on plants grown in nutrient-limited substrates. Detailed analysis
of the effect of microbial inoculation revealed an increase in functional genes linked with nitrogen fixation and phosphorus
solubilization in non-amended inoculated substrates. High throughput sequencing revealed an increment of bacterial diver -
sity in amended substrates, and a significant shift in microbial communities due to inoculation in non-amended substrates.
Moreover, the positive correlation between nutrient bioavailability in the rhizosphere and the presence of PGPB in Martian
simulant substrates suggests that PGPB inoculation can enhance nutrient uptake efficiency under Martian soil conditions.
Our study indicates that the application of microbial consortium along with green compost is a promising strategy to improve
regolith simulants fertility and thus, plant development, passing nutrient-deficient conditions and offering applications for
future long-term space missions.

Introduction
Future long-term space missions, such as those intended for
settlements on the Moon and Mars, need to be independent
of Earth supplies because it is not feasible to bring as a payload all needs to guarantee the crew outliving due to mass
and the associated extra costs (Llorente et al. 2018; Zabel
et al. 2020). These limits led to studying plant-growing systems for Bioregenerative Life Support Systems (BLSSs)
(Guo et al. 2017; Paradiso and De Pascale 2021) because the
plants have various ecological skills that can play a pivotal
role in these systems. Indeed, plants can recycle air (assimilation of CO2 and production of O2) through photosynthesis
activity, water (absorbing it from the roots and releasing it
through transpiration), and organic wastes (using them as
nutrients), as well as produce food (Wheeler 2004; Maggi
and Pallud 2010 ; Llorente et al. 2018 ). Not least, many
studies show how the cultivation of plants can recover the
well-being of people limited in confined spaces (including

mental and overall health status), like the crew members in

has long been proposed to integrate the In-Situ Resource

self-sustaining in a future long-term settlement, the ISRU
practices can align with the constraints we face, as it has
been proposed to use mostly native materials and recycle as
much waste as possible (Rygalov et al. 2001; Benaroya et al.
2013). This combination would facilitate the establishment
of sustainable agriculture (Silverstone et al. 2003; Nelson
et al. 2008; Fackrell et al. 2024).
The regolith is the mineral material that covers the surface
of the Moon or Mars (Kalapodis et al. 2020), and although
it is often referred to as soil, it lacks in organic matter, oxygen, and biotic activity compared to Earth soil. In fact, it is
a sandy material with a scant occurrence of fine particles
exerting colloidal properties; this implies that regolith is not
able to aggregate its own particles to form a porous system
with an adequate water holding capacity and exchangeable
surfaces to retain and release mineral nutrients. Additionally, the regolith is characterized by an alkaline pH (9 to 10)
and an anomalous availability of non-essential elements or
toxic chemical species, such as perchlorates in Mars regolith (Duri et al. 2022a). However, given the abundance of
regolith, the development of soil-based cultivation protocols
appears to be the most reasonable approach. Moreover, it
would be possible to recycle the organic waste produced by
crew (food, cultivation residues, feces, and urine) through
composting in the outpost itself. Amending the regolith with
an in-situ self-produced compost can improve its physicochemical characteristics making it more suitable for plant
growth. In recent years, various studies have evaluated how
to improve the physical and mineral fertility of regoliths
using organic amendments or fertilizers (Gilrain et al. 1999;
Wamelink et al. 2014, 2019; Duri et al. 2020; Caporale et al.
2022). Other studies assessed the solubilization of essential
elements for plant growth due to the application of microorganisms in extraterrestrial simulants (Xia et al. 2023).
Plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB) are beneficial
microorganisms with potential to enhance plant growth and
development, through various mechanisms (e.g. nitrogen
fixation, phosphorus solubilization, phytohormones synthesis, induced systemic resistance, and help against abiotic
stress), and have the potential to colonize the rhizosphere
(Ventorino et al. 2007; Reddy 2013; Rathore et al. 2013;
Romano et al. 2020). In addition, they can be lyophilized
(freeze-dried) or encapsulated and transported taking up
small space and weight (Rathore et al. 2013; Romano et al.
2020). The use of PGPB consortia, rather than single strains,
is gaining traction due to their inherent multi-functionality,
robustness, and adaptability (Woo and Pepe 2018; Qian et al.
2020; Vishwakarma et al. 2020; Kaushal et al. 2023; Woo
et al. 2023). Common PGPB genera include Rhizobium,
Azospirillum, Azotobacter, Bacillus, Enterobacter, and Pseudomonas (Kumar et al. 2016) , although unreported genera
were continuously discovered. Azotobacter, a free-living
aerobic rhizobacterium, showed multiple PGP activities
since are known to be able to fix N2, produce exopolysaccharide and phytohormone, and several strains to solubilize
P (Wani et al. 2013, 2016; Ventorino et al. 2019a; Romano
et al. 2020). The efficacy of Azotobacter chorococcum 76 A
on growth and yield enhancement has been demonstrated in
various conditions and stressed environments (Viscardi et al.
2016; Van Oosten et al. 2017; Cirillo et al. 2023). Bacillus
species are also attractive inoculants due to their resilient
spore formation, which enhances viability and persistence
in soil (Kumar et al. 2016; Etesami et al. 2023). Specifically,
Bacillus mucilaginosus and Priestia megaterium (formerly
classified as Bacillus megaterium) (Liu et al. 2023) have
shown strong phosphate solubilization capabilities, even in
extraterrestrial simulants, attributed to their ability to secrete
acidic substances and attach simulant particles (Xia et al.
2023). Promising PGPB activities have also been observed
in less common genera like Kosakonia and Methylobacterium. Kosakonia species, classified as endophytes in various
crops, exhibit PGP traits and yield enhancement capabilities
(Romano et al. 2020). The strain Kosakonia pseudosacchari
TL13 further demonstrates freeze-drying resistance, ease
of cultivation, and ability to use alternative carbon sources
(Romano et al. 2020 ). Methylobacterium species, known
phytosymbionts, contribute to plant development through
mechanisms like phytohormone biosynthesis, nitrogen fixation, and ethylene biosynthesis suppression, as exemplified
by the strain Methylobacterium populi VP2, isolated from a
polluted environment (Ventorino et al. 2014).
So far, there has been limited exploration of how the
combined use of organic amendments and microbial consortia might support plant resilience under severe nutritional
stress conditions in Lunar and Martian regolith simulants
(employed on the Earth to assemble space agriculture experiments). To encompass this knowledge gap, this study was
settled to investigate the effects of a microbial consortium
composed of selected PGPB on lettuce (Lactuca sativa
L. CV. “Grand Rapids”) grown in simulated Lunar and Martian regoliths, assuming that microbial inoculation would
improve plant growth and nutrient availability, with differ -
ent responses depending on the physicochemical properties
of each simulant. A greenhouse experiment was conducted
using commercial Lunar (LHS-1) and Martian (MMS-1)
regolith simulants, with quartz sand serving as an inert control substrate. Green compost, representing potential waste
from in-situ plant cultivation, was incorporated into the substrates. The study focused on three key areas: (1) the impact

of the microbial consortium on lettuce physiology, growth,
and mineral nutrient content; (2) nutrient bioavailability
and translocation within the substrate-plant system; and (3)
evaluation of simulants biological fertility and changes in
bacterial diversity and community composition across the
different substrates.
The data collected from this experiment could contribute
to the development of sustainable methods for using regolith
as a plant growth substrate in future off-Earth settlements.
Materials and methods
Experimental design
The experiment was carried out in a greenhouse in
autumn–winter season (November – December 2020) at the
Department of Agricultural Sciences of the University of
Naples Federico II in Portici, Italy (40° 49'N, 14°15'E, 72 m
a.s.l.). During the experiment, the means of air temperature
and relative humidity were 13 °C (min: 8 °C; max: 17 °C)
and 46% (min: 42%; max: 51%), with a 10/14 photoperiod
(light/dark) and mean light intensity at the canopy level of
277 µmol m−2 s−1. The study involved two different regolith
simulants: Mojave Mars Simulant (MMS-1; The Martian
Garden, Austin, TX, USA) and Lunar Highlands Simu -
lant (LHS-1; Exolith Lab, University of Central Florida,
Orlando, FL, USA), previously characterized in detail by
(Caporale et al. 2020, 2023). Quartz sand (Vaga, Sabbie e
Ghiaie Silicee, Costa de Nobili, Italy) was used as control
due to its similar particle size. Substrates were used in purity
(indicated as non-amended elsewhere in the text) and mixed
with a commercial green compost (GARDEA, Villafranca di
Verona, Italy) at the dose of 20% (v:v), simulating composting of potential green residues from in-situ plant cultivation.
The green compost, previously characterized by Caporale
et al. (2020), derived from the composting of pruning waste
and grass cuttings over three months, under controlled conditions. It shows an alkaline pH (8.25) and a low C/N ratio,
indicating a good nitrogen availability. It also contains a
significant aromatic component, which contributes to its
long-term stability. The simulant/compost rates (100:0% and
80:20%, v:v) were chosen based on our previous experiences
and findings in this field (Duri et al. 2020, 2022b; Caporale
et al. 2020, 2022, 2023). To improve substrate texture and
facilitate liquid and air circulation, inert quartz gravels (1
< Ø < 3 cm; ZOLUX S.A.S., Saintes, France) were added to
each pot (9 × 9 × 9 cm, approximately 0.630 L) in as much
comprising 5% of the pot volume.
Substrate mixtures were prepared 20 days before transplanting lettuce (Lactuca sativa L. cv. ‘Grand Rapids’).
Beginning on the day of pot preparation, substrates were
moistened to field capacity with reverse osmosis water and
subsequently dried. This process was repeated four times to
promote aggregate disruption and organic matter redistribution (Harrison-Kirk et al. 2013). The pots were distributed
in twin rows, with a resulting density of 14.8 plants m−2
(45 cm inter-raw and 30 cm intra-row spacing). The lettuce
seedlings were transplanted (one per pot) at the third true
leaf stage. For seedlings preparation, 1 g of lettuce seeds
was disinfected in a 5% sodium hypochlorite solution for 10
min. Several rinse cycles (5 times) in reverse osmosis water
were performed to remove hypochlorite residues. The disinfected seeds were placed onto absorbent paper in a germination tray, moist with reverse osmosis water (ensuring 100%
humidity), under dark conditions for 48 h. Subsequently,
germinated seeds with about 1 cm radicle were transferred
to styrofoam trays with inert vermiculite soaked in reverse
osmosis water and incubated at 25 °C in a nursery with 65%
relative humidity (RH), a light/dark regime of 16/8 h and a
light intensity of 200 ± 20 μmol·m–2·s–1 (PPFD) until they
reached the third true leaf stage. For the experiment, seedling equally developed were selected (the evaluation was
performed by visual inspection). At the transplanting, the
vermiculite was carefully removed, avoiding damage to the
roots.
Only reverse osmosis water was administered daily to
evaluate the potential nutritional input of compost and the
combined effect with PGPB inoculation against nutrient
deficiency, returning the evapotranspiration throughout the
experiment (48 days), using a drip irrigation system.
The experimental design was a randomized completeblock factorial design, involved in six substrate mixtures
(S), of which three without compost “non-amended” (LHS1
100%; MMS1 100%; Sand 100%) and three with 20% in
volume of compost “amendment” (LHS1 80%; MMS1
80%; Sand 80%), and two Plant Growth Promoting Bacteria
inoculum (P), an un-inoculated (PGPB -) and an inoculated
(PGPB +), with six plants per experimental unit, totaling
72 plants.
Microbial strains, inoculum preparation and lettuce
treatments
A non-commercialized consortium of selected PGPB,
belonging to the microbial collection of the Department of
Agricultural Sciences of University of Naples Federico II
(Portici) was inoculated to half of the pots for each treatment. The consortium was composed by Azotobacter ( A.)
chroococcum 76A, Priestia ( P.) megaterium EL5, Methy -
lobacterium ( M.) populi VP2, and Kosakonia ( K.) pseudosacchari TL13, previously isolated, characterized, and
described by our research group (Ventorino et al. 2014; Viscardi et al. 2016; Romano et al. 2020). These strains were
selected based on: i) their ability to promote plant growth,
as demonstrated in various studies, along with their ability
1148 Biology and Fertility of Soils (2025) 61:1145–1164
to utilize alternative carbon sources or thrive in polluted
environments (Ventorino et al. 2014; Viscardi et al. 2016;
Romano et al. 2020); ii) the absence of antagonism, assessed
using the dual culture method described by Hammami et al.
(2013) – as only strains that showed a complete absence
of inhibition zones were used for consortium; iii) the consortium’s ability to promote plant growth in quartz sand as
previously evaluated in growth chamber pot trials, follow -
ing standard procedures (DM 27/01/2014 2014) with some
modifications (data not shown). Microbial biomasses were
cultivated in a 14 L fermentor (New Brunswick BioFlo®/
CelliGen® 115, Eppendorf) with a working volume of 8 L.
The growth media used were as follows: Brain Heart Infusion (BHI, Oxoid, Italy) for K. pseudosacchari TL13, Yeast
Mannitol Broth (YMB, Merck, Germany) for A. chroococcum 76 A, Plate Count Broth (PCB: 5.0 g L−1 of Enzymatic
Digest of Casein, 2.5 g L−1 of Yeast Extract, and 1.0 g L−1
of Glucose) for P. megaterium EL5, and Nutrient Broth (NB,
Oxoid, Italy) for M. populi VP2. Each medium was inoculated with 2% (v/v) of a bacterial cell suspension (~ 108 CFU
mL⁻1). The fermentations were carried out under the following conditions, tailored to each strain: 30 °C for K. pseudosacchari and P. megaterium, 28 °C for A. chroococcum, and
M. populi; pH 7.00, agitation at 130 rpm, air sparging at 0.5
vvm. Additionally, 40 mL of a 3% Antifoam 204 (Sigma-
Aldrich) solution was added at the beginning of each process
to prevent excessive foaming. After centrifugation (45 min
at 3428 × g) all the strains were freeze-dried for long term
storage. For plant inoculation, a final concentration of 10⁶
CFU mL⁻1 for each strain was achieved in reverse osmosis
water, furthermore non-inoculated plants were irrigated with
the same volume of reverse osmosis water. Lettuce plants
were inoculated by using a root dip method, submerging
the planting trays in the microbial liquid suspension for 15
min to completely wet the roots; drained of excess liquid,
then the plant-plug was removed and transplanted. Further,
after 1 and 2 weeks from the transplanting, each plant was
repeatedly inoculated through the application of 100 ml
of inoculum at plants collar to cope transplant stress and
improve the effectiveness of treatment, whereas non-inoculated plants were irrigated with the same volume of reverse
osmosis water.
Sampling and biometric, physiological,
and colorimetric parameters
One day before harvest, chlorophyll fluorescence was measured on each plant using a portable fluorometer (Fv/Fm
Meter, Opti-Sciences Inc., Hudson, NH, USA) after 30 min
of dark adaption. On the same leaf, the SPAD index was
determined using a portable chlorophyll meter (SPAD-502,
Minolta Corp. Ltd., Osaka, Japan) (Caporale et al. 2022).
Leaf color was measured using the CIELab colorimetric
indices (L, a*, and b*) with a CR-400 portable colorimeter
(Minolta Co. Ltd, Osaka, Japan). The derived Chroma and
Hue angle indices were calculated using the formulas from
McLellan et al. (1995).
Growth index was calculated as [ /u1D70B x (Dm/2)2 × H] were
 Dm is the mean diameter, and H is the maximum plant
height, measured from the ground to the lettuce highest point
(Caporale et al. 2022).
At harvest (48 Days After Transplanting, DAT), plants
were cut at the base. Number of leaves (n plant−1) and leaf
area (cm2 plant−1), were measured on each plant. Leaf area
was measured using an electronic area meter (LI-COR 3100
C Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA). Before drying the leaves
at 50 °C in a forced-air oven (until constant weight) and
measuring the dry weight (g plant−1), the fresh weight was
noted to determine the dry matter percentage. Dried leaves
were ground using a Wiley mill for macro- and micro-nutrient determination.
Roots were carefully sampled from the pots, preserving
the linked rhizosphere soil. The remaining substrate in the
pot was considered Bulk soil and sampled separately. Roots
and bulk soil were stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis.
Assessment of multielement profile in lettuce foliar
biomass
The C, N, and S concentrations in lettuce foliar biomass were
measured in freeze-dried and pulverized sample (2 mg each)
by the Micro Elemental Analyser (UNICUBE®, Elementar,
Hesse, Germany), using sulphanilamide (Elementar, 99.5%)
as standard for instrument calibration and analytical quality
check. The total concentrations of Ca, P, K, Mg, Na, Fe,
Mn, Zn, Cu and B, was determined by ICP-OES (Thermo
Scientific iCAP 7400, Waltham, MA, USA), after digestion
of freeze-dried and pulverized samples (100 mg each) in a
microwave digestion system (Milestone ultraWAVE, Sorisole, BG, Italy), through a blend of HNO3 65% (2.5 ml), 3 M
HCl (0.5 ml) and HF 50% (0.5 ml). The standard reference
material NCS ZC85006 was used to monitor the quality of
analyses, with element recoveries around ± 10% of the certified values.
Assessment of mineral nutrient contents
and bioavailability in the substrates
The concentrations of organic C, total N and S, were
determined in substrate samples (20 mg each) by a Micro
Elemental Analyzer (UNICUBE®, Elementar, Hesse, Ger -
many). Instrument calibration and analytical quality check
were performed with sulphanilamide standard (Elementar,
99.5%). Possible inorganic C from carbonates was subtracted

to the total C content, to assess the organic C content (DM
13/09/1999).
The bioavailable fractions of the macro- and micronutrients (except P) were extracted from substrate samples (1
g each), at the end of the lettuce growing cycle, by 0.43
M acetic acid (solid/solution ratio: 1/40; reaction time: 16
h). The extracts were filtered by cellulose papers (Whatman
42) and analyzed by Inductively Coupled Plasma—Optical
Emission Spectrometry (ICP-OES, Thermo Scientific iCAP
7400, Waltham, MA, USA). The quality of the extractions
was monitored by the certified reference material BCR 700
(recovery at ± 12% of the certified values). On the other
hand, the bioavailable fraction of P was extracted from substrates by 0.5 M NaHCO3 (buffered at pH 8.5) and analyzed
with spectrophotometer (Perkin Elmer Lambda 365 UV/
VIS, Waltham, MA, USA) at 720 nm (Olsen method; DM
13/09/1999).
Microbial analysis
DNA extraction, quantification of whole bacterial
and fungal community, and functional groups by qPCR
Regolith and sand rhizosphere samples were collected at the
end of the trial as reported above. Total DNA was extracted
by the FastDNA Spin Kit for Soil (MP Biomedicals, Illkirch
Cedex, France) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.
Amplification of 16S, ITS, and nitrogenase (nifH), Alkaline Phosphatase (phoD), and phytase (BPP) encoding genes
were conducted in a Bio-Rad CFX Opus 96 System Ther -
mocycler (Bio-Rad, Milano, Italy) using the primers listed
in Table 1 (Luo et al. 2020). The PCR mixture (25 μL total
volume) included and 2 μL of genomic DNA diluted 1:15
in DNA-free milliQ water, 1 × I SYBR Green supermix,
(Bio-Rad), and 0.1 μM of each primer. All qPCR reactions
included an initial denaturing step at 95 °C for 3 min followed by 40 cycles of amplification as specified in Table 1
(Luo et al. 2020). Standard curves were generated to calculate the abundance of selected genes.
Technical triplicates of biological replicates along with
negative controls were examined. Standard curves were calculated using triplicates of tenfold serial dilutions of each
target gene, ranging from 104 to 1011 copies. The target
gene’s copy number per microliter was calculated using the
formula: (A B)/(C D); where A represents the DNA template’s concentration in ng µl−1, B represents the Avogadro
number (6.02 × 1023 copies mol−1), C represents the average
molecular weight of a DNA base pair (6.6 × 1011 ng mol−1),
and D represents the DNA size (bp).

Statistical analysis
A two-way ANOVA was conducted (using the software
SPSS 28), followed by the Tukey–Kramer HSD Test to analyze the mean effects at p ≤ 0.05 of biometric, physiological,
and colorimetric parameters, multielement profile in lettuce
foliar biomass, nutrient contents and bioavailability and for
marker functional genes.
The R statistical environment (R version 4.3.1) was
used for data analysis using RStudio (Kronthaler and Zöllner 2021). Bacterial community data were analyzed with
phyloseq (McMurdie and Holmes 2013) and vegan 2.5–6
(Oksanen et al. 2019) packages. The quality of sequencing was controlled with rarefaction analysis using the rare
curve function from vegan 2.5–6 (Oksanen et al. 2019).
Alpha diversity was assessed with Shannon diversity. The
Shannon–Weaver index (H) was calculated as follows: H =
-sum pi * ln pi, where pi is the proportional abundance of
species i. Additionally, from the Shannon–Weaver index,
the diversity was calculated as follows: D = exp(H) (Jost
2007; Bodenhausen et al. 2013). Beta diversity was analyzed
by permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PER -
MANOVA) using the Adonis function from Vegan. Principal
coordinate analysis (PCoA) on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities
was used to display differences between samples. To represent the bacterial community composition, unconstrained
ordination by Principal Component Analyses (PCA) based
on clr-transformed ZOTU tables was performed, followed by
distance-based redundancy analyses (db-RDA), constraining for significant factors identified in PERMANOVA and
conditioning for block. “Envfit” function of the “vegan”
package was used to identify the nutrient contents and bioavailability in rhizosphere soil and foliar biomass variables
significantly (p < 0.05) associated with the bacterial community constrained for inoculum factor.
Results
Growth, colorimetric, and chlorophyll fluorescence
analyses
Substrates (S), PGPB inoculum (P) and their interactions
(S x P), significantly affected most biometric parameters,
except for leaf dry weight in the S x P interaction. PGPB
inoculation generally increased leaf number, leaf area, and
growth index. However, non-inoculated plants had higher
dry matter. Specifically, the microbial consortium increased
leaf number by 29%, and leaf area by 34% (Table 2). Across
all substrates, the amendment enhanced plant growth
compared to the non-amended substrates. The highest
dry weight (2.1 g plant−1) was observed in plants grown
on 80% amended MMS-1 simulant (Table 2), followed by
amended sand and lunar simulant. No significant differences
were found among non-amended substrates. Similar trends
were observed for leaf area, and growth index. The S x P
interaction revealed that amended and inoculated substrates
(simulants and sand) yielded the highest values for leaf area,
and growth index. No significant differences were observed

Discussion
With a view to future manned missions to Mars or the
Moon, the present study investigated the potential of a
selected PGPB consortium to enhance lettuce growth and
stress resistance on extraterrestrial “soil” simulants, with
zero nutritional input besides nutrients released by green
compost and “soil” minerals. The aim of the study was
to refine ISRU protocols and reduce the dependence by
Earth-based supplies. Lettuce was chosen for its suitable
growth characteristics and high nutritional value, rich
in bioactive compounds that can minimize space flightinduced pathophysiological alterations on human body
(Bychkov et al. 2021).
The nutrient deficiency in regolith simulants, due to the
lack or scarcity of bioavailable key nutrients (mainly N
and P) and organic matter (Caporale et al. 2022), hindered
lettuce growth. Plants responded to nutrient deficiency
by reducing leaf area to minimize nutrient demand and
water loss, and delaying leaf senescence to maintain nutrient acquisition and photosynthetic capacity (Farooq et al.
2009; Thomas and Ougham 2014; Schippers et al. 2015;
Hu et al. 2020; Francis et al. 2023). Consequently, lettuces
grown on non-amended simulants significantly reduced
leaf number and area compared to those on amended
simulants. Amended substrates significantly enhanced
lettuce dry biomass with increments of 13, 9.5, and sev -
enfold for LHS-1, MMS-1 and sand respectively, compared to non-amended simulants. The amended Martian
simulant (MMS-1) showed the highest biomass production, this likely due to its superior physicochemical features
(Caporale et al. 2023). Nutritional deficiency altered pigment ratios in leaves (Shah et al. 2017), leading to lower
SPAD Index values than those typically reported for let -
tuce (Galieni et al. 2016; Di Mola et al. 2020). However,
the values were consistent with those reported by Galieni
et al. (2016) for lettuce grown under no-nitrogen fertilization conditions. Severe nutritional stress in non-amended
substrates was also indicated by low maximum quantum
efficiency of PSII photochemistry (Fv/Fm)(Ferrante and
Maggiore 2007; Cendrero-Mateo et al. 2016) values (under

The application of the microbial consortium resulted in
an overall 35% increase in leaf dry biomass. This can be
due to specific functional activities of the bacterial strains
composing the selected consortium. Both the presence of
nitrogen-fixing and phosphate-solubilizing bacteria such as
A. chroococcum 76 A and K. pseudosacchari TL13 (Viscardi et al. 2016; Romano et al. 2020), can increase the nutritional status of the plant, and through phytohormones (like
cytokinins and indole-3-acetic acid—IAA), produced by P.
megaterium EL5 and M. populi VP2 (Ventorino et al. 2014;
Kumar et al. 2016).
Even for the maximum quantum efficiency of PSII photochemistry, it is possible to observe a slight enhancement,
indeed, in plants treated with the microbial consortium we
observe optimal Fv/Fm values. This can be due to either
the production of the enzyme 1-aminocyclopropane-1-car -
boxylic acid (ACC) deaminase by PGPBs as suggested by
Rossi et al. (2021) on Brassica napus or the increase in IAA
concentration in plants driven by PGPBs modulating the
auxin signal pathway to protect plants from abiotic stresses
as stated by Barnawal et al. (2017) on wheat. Furthermore,
composting can enhance soil aggregation and raise the
microbial activity and biomass linked to symbiotic root
associations (Kebede et al. 2023).
On the other hand, we observed no statistical difference
in nutrient bioavailability (as assessed by 0.43 M acetic acid
extraction of soil after plant growth) due to the application of
PGPB. Generally, in the soil, the microorganisms are known
to transform elements into soluble compounds through
enzyme reactions and the secretion of acidic substances,
which lowers the pH and thus increases nutrient solubility;
moreover, microbial decomposition transforms high molecular weight substrates in low molecular weight compounds
which are taken up by microbes or roots. Microorganisms
also have the ability to bind to simulant particles forming a
stable bacteria-mineral complex (through the formation of
polysaccharides biofilms) as suggested by Xia et al. (2023)
which explored the possibility of solubilizing phosphorus
from lunar regolith with a view to future applications in
BLSSs. In contrast, the lettuce plants aided by PGPB inoculum exhibited a significantly higher content of nutrients in
their leaves, thus indicating a certain improvement of nutrient bioavailability in the PGPB-treated substrates. Probably,
this increase, of the sole promptly-bioavailable fraction of
nutrients, was not assessed through the extraction of substrates by 0.43 M acetic acid, because this complexing reagent generally addresses the entire potentially-bioavailable
pool of nutrients, not only the readily-bioavailable fractions
(Adamo et al. 2024). Else, another possible explanation
could be that the effect of PGPB inoculum on the bioavailability of nutrients is very unstable in the absence of the
plant. Nutrient bioavailability in soil is the result of multiple
processes that control nutrient release and movement in the
soil, among which the absorption of nutrients by the plant
root–microorganisms’ system is of huge importance. Therefore, soil chemical extractions carried out after plant growth
might be unable to quantify differences in nutrient bioavailability controlled by the interaction of soil and plant characteristics. Moreover, the q-PCR results indicate the absence
of microbial inhibition by heavy metals in the simulants and
reflexively the real regolith that they referred to. This result,

MMS-1 substrates inoculated with PGPB further emphasizes the beneficial role of compost and PGPB in promoting
microbial functioning related to phosphorus solubilization,
essential for many physiological and biochemical processes
(Chouyia et al. 2020). In particular, the increased 16S and
ITS rRNA gene copy numbers in amended samples and
non-amended MMS-1 substrate suggest that these conditions promote bacterial growth, likely due to enhanced nutrient availability due to green compost addition (Rezig et al.
2012) as well as better physicochemical properties of the
MMS-1 simulant (Caporale et al. 2023). Conversely, the
lowest bacterial abundance in uninoculated sand and LHS-1
confirm that these substrates, lacking key macronutrients
and organic matter (Caporale et al. 2022), are less supportive
for microbial colonization and growth. By applying PGPB
and organic amendments like green compost, soils can be
engineered to support plant growth (Rezig et al. 2012; Woo
and Pepe 2018). Although the seeds were superficially sterilized, it is known that seeds from plants grown under aseptic
conditions harbour lower microbial diversity (not absent)
compared to those grown under natural conditions (War
et al. 2023). This has certainly influenced the microbiome
of the uninoculated and untreated control plants with PGPB.
Otherwise, this study confirms that microbial inoculants
improve nutrient cycling, and fertilization. Organic amendments also enhance microbial activity, aiding in the break -
down of xenobiotic compounds (Ventorino et al. 2019b).
The enhanced nitrogen-fixing and phosphate-solubilizing
capabilities observed in this study may reduce the need for
fertilizers in space. In the perspective of In-Situ Resource
Utilization, green compost can be derived from waste produced by astronauts (Duri et al. 2020). Moreover, bacteria
such as K. pseudosacchari TL13 can be stored freeze-dried
form at concentrations of up to ~ 10 log CFU per gram
(Romano et al. 2020), for a long time in the space station
until application, therefore requiring limited space and light
in weight.
The Shannon index analysis revealed a higher bacterial
diversity in simulants amended with green compost compared to non-amended ones. According to previous studies
green compost increased the concentration of multiple soil
chemical characteristics providing a wide range nutrients
and substrates supporting various bacterial taxa and enhancing overall diversity (LeBlanc and Harrigian 2024). PGPB
inoculation in non-amended simulants led to a decrease
in alpha diversity, possibly indicating that the inoculants
favoured specific bacterial taxa, reducing overall diversity.
Bacterial inoculants can directly alter soil microbial community composition by suppressing other microorganisms, or
indirectly by impacting on root exudates such as phytohor -
mones which may select specific taxa (Mawarda et al. 2020).
The beta diversity analysis, as shown by PERMANOVA
results, further emphasizes distinct bacterial community
composition formed in amended simulants compared to
non-amended ones. The clear separation of communities between these treatments confirm that green compost
strongly influences the overall composition of bacterial communities (Rezig et al. 2012; LeBlanc and Harrigian 2024).
However, inoculation also affects beta diversity, as shown by
db-RDA ordinations, which revealed clustering of bacterial
communities based on the PGPB inoculation. This result
suggests that microbial inoculation can shape the composition, and potential functionality of substrates (Mawarda
et al. 2020). Furthermore, constrained ordinations, showed
a clear separation between MMS-1 simulant and the other
substrates (LHS-1 and sand), confirming that the different
physicochemical properties of MMS-1 (Caporale et al. 2023)
not only affects plant growth but also selects for a distinct
bacterial community.
Multidisciplinary approaches were needed to describe the
relationships between specific nutrients, the rhizosphere and
foliar biomass, and bacterial diversity. Redundancy analy -
sis showed that nutrients like Ca, Mn and Na were positively correlated with MMS-1 inoculated with PGPB, while
P showed a negative correlation. This suggests that PGPB
may enhance the availability of certain nutrients in the
rhizosphere, improving plant growth and resilience (Reddy
2013; Mawarda et al. 2020), though it may reduce others
like P, possibly due to microbial uptake or immobilization
processes (Chouyia et al. 2020). Furthermore, the signifi -
cant positive correlation between the nutrient bioavailable
fractions in rhizosphere soil and nutrient concentrations in
foliar biomass of MMS-1-based substrates suggests that
these plant growth media provide favourable conditions for
the uptake and accumulation of these nutrients in plant tissues (essential for food quality), which were enhanced by
the microbial inoculum. This may indicate that the physical
and chemical properties of the MMS-1 substrates allow for
better nutrient bioavailability or uptake efficiency compared
to other tested substrates. Even in LHS-1 and Sand, PGPB
inoculation induced better correlations and outcomes than
non-inoculated treatments. Additionally, the positive correlation across multiple elements points to a potential synergy
in nutrient uptake, where the presence of one nutrient could
facilitate the absorption of others.
The bacterial community analysis showed that the lettuce
rhizosphere, regardless of treatment, was mainly composed
of four phyla: Proteobacteria, Actinobacteriota, Bacteroidota, and Firmicutes. These phyla are well-suited to the conditions in both amended and non-amended simulants, with
or without PGPB. The phylum-level composition remained
stable in amended samples, suggesting that amendments
1161Biology and Fertility of Soils (2025) 61:1145–1164
may stabilize microbial community composition, aligning
with previous findings (Ventorino et al. 2019b). However,
in the non-amended theses, noticeable shift is observed
between inoculated and uninoculated treatments, indicating
a functional response of the bacterial community to the presence of PGPB, supporting previous results.
At a deeper taxonomic level, the increase in the order
Bacillales in all amended treatments suggests a positive
response to organic material, as they are known for their
ability to degrade complex organic compounds, and promote
plant growth through the production of phytohormones or
biocontrol agents (Kumar et al. 2016; Ventorino et al. 2019b;
Etesami et al. 2023). Conversely, the increased abundance
of the order Burkholderiales in non-amended uninoculated
treatments indicates that these bacteria may be suited to oligotrophic conditions (Nicolitch et al. 2019). The observed
increase in Micrococcales in PGPB-inoculated samples, particularly in non-amended substrates, highlights the potential
impact of PGPB inoculation on the selection of specific bacterial groups. Micrococcales are known for their vigorous
metabolism and ability to adapt to harsh conditions and to
degrade a wide range of organic compounds (Rojas-Gätjens
et al. 2022). However, meta-barcoding analysis offers limited
resolution to distinguish between environmental or inoculated strains due to conservation of the rRNA gene as well
as biases in reference databases (Lindner et al. 2022), not
allowing the effective tracking of microbial strains.
Conclusions
As space exploration continues to advance rapidly, the overall conclusions of this study demonstrate for the first time the
potential of PGPB inoculation and green compost amendment to enhance plant growth on extraterrestrial soils, boosting the mineral profile, main biometric and physiological
parameters in lettuce plants, supporting ISRU approaches
for future space missions. In addition, we can assert that the
consortium of A. chroococcum 76 A, P. megaterium EL5,
M. populi VP2, and K. pseudosacchari TL13 can improve
the chemical and biological fertility of the simulants under
nutritional deficiency-growth conditions. Our data validate
the positive impact of these microbial consortia on regolith simulants biological fertility, and future research could
focus on trials conducted in axenic conditions to minimize
potential environmental interference with plant-regolith
microbiomes and inoculum. Additionally, more advanced
sequencing techniques, such as shotgun sequencing, could
be employed to precisely monitor strain dynamics, functionality, and their interactions within the plant-regolith
ecosystem.
