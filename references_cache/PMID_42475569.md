Magnetotaxis has evolved independently numerous times in bacteria, whereby 
genetically controlled biomineralization of nano-crystalline magnets results in 
swimming along Earth's magnetic field lines. Compared to magnetotactic bacteria 
(MTB), evolutionary mechanisms of magnetotaxis as a trait in eukaryotes remain 
poorly understood. Here, we report a magnetotactic ciliate, Tropidoatractus 
magnetotacticus sp. nov., that acquires magnetotaxis via syntrophy. T. 
magnetotacticus exhibits magnetotaxis due to the magnetic moment of internal 
ferrimagnetic magnetite (Fe3O4) nanoparticles forming ellipsoidal 
"necklace-shaped" parallel chains. Electron microscopy revealed T. 
magnetotacticus hosts numerous internal rod-shaped bacteria containing these 
magnetosome chains. Consistent with this, a genomic population of MTB 
(Thermodesulfobacteriota) in magnetically sorted T. magnetotacticus cells was 
found that encoded and expressed a magnetosome gene cluster responsible for 
magnetosome Fe3O4 biomineralization closely related to that of the ectosymbiont 
"Candidatus Desulfarcum epimagneticum." T. magnetotacticus also housed a second 
genomic population affiliated with the endosymbiotic methanogen Methanoregula. 
Metatranscriptomes of sorted T. magnetotacticus cells show eukaryotic 
hydrogenosomal Fe-hydrogenase gene expression, and expression of genes encoding 
proteins in an electron transport chain indicative of H2-producing 
mitochondria-related organelles. Active gene expression of energy metabolism 
pathways indicates a tripartite syntrophic network whereby anaerobic 
fermentation products from T. magnetotacticus are consumed by two syntrophic 
partners: MTB producing the magnetosome chains and hydrogenotrophic methanogens. 
Our findings show how magnetotaxis can emerge as a trait in eukaryotes via 
syntrophic cooperation.

Significance Magnetotactic bacteria (MTB) swim along Earth’s magnetic field lines via genetically controlled biomineralization of nano-crystalline magnets, but magnetotaxis as a trait in eukaryotes remains poorly understood. Our findings show an anaerobic magnetotactic ciliate that acquires magnetotaxis via syntrophy with an internal partner MTB that biomineralizes internal ferrimagnetic magnetite (Fe3O4) nanoparticles in ellipsoidal “necklace-shaped” parallel chains. The ciliate host also has a second syntrophic partner, H2-oxidizing methanogenic archaeon, which likely helps promote anaerobic metabolism of the fermenting host by keeping internal H2-levels low. Our study shows that hydrogen-based syntrophy can result in magnetically guided niche adaptation of an anaerobic ciliate host, and represents a mechanism of cooperation between eukaryotic cells, MTB, and methanogens. Magnetotactic bacteria (MTB) are a polyphyletic group of bacteria capable of biomineralizing nanocrystals of the iron-containing minerals magnetite (Fe3O4) and/or greigite (Fe3S4), which allow for magnetotaxis and navigation using the Earth’s magnetic field ( 1 ). MTB form membrane-bound structures called magnetosomes ( 2 , 3 ), which are arranged in chains, resulting in a magnetic dipole moment that causes them to swim parallel to the Earth’s magnetic field. The biomineralization of magnetic crystals in MTB is under tight genetic control by the magnetosome gene cluster (MGC), which encodes Mam proteins responsible for the formation of magnetosomes ( 4 ). Magnetotaxis combined with aerotaxis guides the MTB in the sediment or stratified water column to help find the oxic–anoxic transition zone (OATZ), the optimal redox zone for MTB ( 2 ). MTB are mostly microaerophiles or anaerobes with a preferred habitat in suboxic or anoxic environments found at or below OATZs. The magnetosomes biomineralized by MTB play an important role in the iron cycle. Fossilized magnetosomes (“magnetofossils”) are used by geologists for paleomagnetic studies as well as dating rocks, and can provide insight into past climate ( 5 , 6 ). Compared with the well-known ecology and evolution of MTB, much less is known about magnetotactic eukaryotic microbes (protists). Some predatory microbial eukaryotes become transitorily magnetoreceptive after phagocytosing MTB prey ( 7 – 11 ). Magnetotactic protists isolated from a stratified salt pond have also been reported to contain magnetosome-like structures, although their origin remained unclear ( 7 ). A possible biomineralization-based magnetoreception was described in a protist reported to be a eukaryote flagellate, which had larger magnetosomes compared to co-occurring MTB from the same environment ( 12 ). However, no genetic evidence for eukaryotic magnetosome formation was reported ( 12 ). Available sequence data indicate that no genuine eukaryotic orthologues of Mam proteins have yet been described ( 13 ). Magnetotactic, anaerobic euglenid protists inhabit anoxic marine sediments and carry ectosymbiotic MTB ( 11 , 14 , 15 ), and more recently, a magnetotactic ciliate containing endosymbiotic MTB was discovered ( 16 ). These findings show that magnetoreception in microbial eukaryotes is a newly appreciated yet still poorly understood phenomenon among protists living in OATZs and anoxic aquatic environments. In anoxic environments, a thermodynamic drive selects for an abundance of protist-prokaryote partnerships based on anaerobic syntrophy ( 17 ). Relatively small amounts of energy available to cells in anoxic environments often force microorganisms into metabolic partnerships whereby metabolite sharing results in a more efficient energy metabolism for both partners, compared to if they acted alone ( 18 ). In anaerobic syntrophies, the mutual dependence of partner microbes can become so strong that neither partner can live without the other, forming a symbiosis, and a metabolism that neither partner can accomplish on its own ( 18 ). For example, anaerobic H2-producing ciliates often engage in syntrophic H2-exchange with H2-consuming methanogens and sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) under anoxia ( 17 ). These syntrophic relationships are based on the exchange of fermentation products (e.g., H2, acetate, ethanol, lactate) generated by the host ciliate, which are then utilized as electron donors by the prokaryote syntrophic partners ( 17 ). In many hydrogenosome-containing anaerobic ciliates, syntrophic H2-oxidizing methanogens keep H2 concentrations low to maintain the thermodynamic favorability of the host’s energy metabolism, which becomes self-limiting in the absence of H2 sinks ( 19 ). Here, using a combination of transcriptomic, phylogenetic, and microscopic techniques we describe how a three-way anaerobic syntrophy results in magnetotaxis by an anaerobic ciliate species, Tropidoatractus magnetotacticus sp. nov. Results and Discussion Discovery and Characterization of a Magnetotactic Ciliate. In sediment collected from a freshwater stream near Libreville, Gabon ( SI Appendix, Fig. S1 ) located in the southern geomagnetic hemisphere [inclination –27.9° ( 20 )], we identified unusually large magnetotactic microbes that swim antiparallel to an applied magnetic field, consistent with a “south-seeking” origin. Reversing the magnetic field direction inverted the swimming direction of the microbes, confirming active magnetotaxis ( Fig. 1 A–C and Movie S1 ). These magnetotactic organisms, with up to 48.9 µm length, were significantly larger than known MTB [lengths up to 9 µm ( 21 )], multicellular magnetotactic prokaryotes [MMPs, 3 to 12 µm in diameter ( 22 )], or previously reported magnetotactic protists [up to 23 µm ( 11 )]. The observed microbes were unicellular organisms with a macronucleus and numerous cilia, indicating they were ciliates, and with dark aggregates at the anterior ends of the cells ( Fig. 1 and Movie S2 ). In addition to the large magnetotactic ciliates, the sediment contained abundant coccoid MTB (1 to 4 µm in diameter) that also swam antiparallel to the applied magnetic field ( Fig. 1 A–C ). Fig. 1. Light microscopy revealed two size classes of ellipsoidal magnetotactic ciliates ( Movie S3 ). The majority (up to ~25 per 50 µL of sediment) of the observed cells were 14.7 ± 2.6 µm long and 6.7 ± 1.0 µm wide (n = 47). A relatively smaller number of cells (1 to 3 per sample) were substantially larger (38.6 ± 3.5 µm long, 12.9 ± 1.7 µm wide; n = 16) and exhibited a characteristic back-and-forth “ping-pong” movement known from MMP ( 23 , 24 ). Most cells in the sample swam antiparallel to the magnetic field (south-seeking) ( Movie S1 ); however, ~1 to 2% of the cells swam parallel (north-seeking). The sediment was stored in Munich [Germany; magnetic inclination + 63.1° ( 20 )] for 6 mo before the first detection of magnetotactic cells. It remains unclear whether the observed south-seeking orientation reflects the behavior in their native habitat of Gabon [inclination –27.9° ( 20 )]. We enriched the magnetotactic ciliates using the capillary racetrack method ( SI Appendix, Fig. S2 and Movie S4 ) ( 25 ). Scanning and scanning-transmission electron microscopy (SEM/STEM) revealed chains of electron-dense, elongated, nano-sized crystals within the cells ( Fig. 1 E–N ). Their size, “bullet-shaped” morphology, chain arrangement, and enrichment in iron and oxygen ( SI Appendix, Fig. S3 ) confirm that these are magnetite particles organized in magnetosomes. The magnetosomes form distinctive, atypical, “necklace-shaped” chains connected at their ends, generating the magnetic moment responsible for the cellular magnetotaxis ( Fig. 1 G, J, and M ). The intact, well-ordered chains and their characteristic ellipsoidal shape strongly indicate that the magnetosomes are produced by living rod-shaped bacteria residing inside the larger ciliate, rather than being ingested MTB. Indeed, rod-shaped bacteria within the ciliates were observed to contain the “necklace-shaped” magnetosome chains ( Fig. 1 G and I ). Not all internal bacteria contained magnetosomes, suggesting that the larger host ciliate contains a mixture of internal bacteria, some of which produce magnetosomes and some of which do not. The “necklace-shaped” arrangement could arise from the bacteria positioning magnetosomes along their membranes ( Fig. 1G ), with their rod-shaped geometry resulting in these elongated, ellipsoidal magnetosome chains. The magnetotactic ciliates were observed during repeated subsampling of the acquired sediment material over 2 y. We interpret the stability of the “necklace-shaped” magnetosome chains inside the ciliates over this relatively long time period as an indication that the biomineralization of the nanocrystalline magnetite chains was a continual process that occurred in a reproducing population of ciliates via the internal rod-shaped bacteria. Moreover, the ellipsoidal “necklace shape” of the magnetosome chains ( Fig. 1 K–N ) differs fundamentally from ingested magnetosomes reported in MTB-grazing protists that typically appear as discrete linear assemblies within digestive vacuoles ( 9 , 10 ). Considering these observations, the magnetosomes can be best explained by a controlled and continual biomineralization process via MTB living inside a larger host cell. The cytoplasm of magnetotactic ciliates was observed to be packed full of intracellular bacteria ( Fig. 1H and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–C and G ), similar to another magnetotactic ciliate that contains endosymbiotic MTB with similar bullet-shaped magnetosomes ( 16 ). In bacterivorous ciliates, digestive vacuoles are typically globular and contain loosely and irregularly arranged bacteria embedded within a digestive matrix. In contrast, MTBs formed an elongated, irregular, and comparatively loose pattern distributed throughout the cytoplasm. This organization does not match the morphology of a globular digestive vacuole and therefore argues against the MTB being enclosed within digestive vacuoles. The crystals were enriched in iron and oxygen ( Fig. 1 G and H ) but lacked sulfur ( SI Appendix, Fig. S3 ), consistent with magnetite but not greigite. Furthermore, the observed “bullet-shaped” crystal morphology has only been described for magnetite—not greigite—crystals biomineralized by MTB in the classes Deltaproteobacteria, Nitrospirae, and Omnitrophica ( 4 ). The magnetite crystal morphology ( Fig. 1 K–N ) closely resembles that in a magnetotactic protist isolated from freshwater sediments in Brazil, which contains magnetite crystals aligned in dense aggregations of parallel chains ( 12 ). However, average crystal sizes observed in the magnetotactic cells in our sample (up to 97.3 ± 29 nm in length, 39.4 ± 7.2 nm in width) ( Fig. 1 K–N ) were more than twofold smaller than those in the protist in Brazil described previously [276.6 ± 61.3 nm in length, 52.7 ± 5.1 nm in width ( 12 )]. Moreover, the bullet-shaped magnetosomes were organized into distinct, atypical chain structures comprising two to three parallel chains offset but connected at the ends forming an ellipsoid shape ( Fig. 1 J and K and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 ). Each cell contained multiple such ellipsoidal chain structures (5 ± 2.6 per cell, n = 10) ( Fig. 1 K–N ). The crystal morphology and magnetosome chain arrangement in the large magnetotactic cells we observed differ markedly from the single chains of cuboctahedral magnetite particles in “Candidatus Desulfarcum epimagneticum,” ecto-symbionts living on the external surface of an anaerobic euglenid protist ( 14 ). Much more similarity can be found when comparing to the bullet-shaped magnetite crystals, which are formed in bundles and biomineralized by MTB that live as endosymbionts in a freshwater ciliate host ( 16 ). In addition to magnetite particles, we identified large, dense, angular particles (2 to 3 µm in diameter) concentrated at the anterior ends of the cells ( Fig. 1F ). These particles consisted primarily of silicon and aluminum (feldspar), with negligible iron content ( SI Appendix, Fig. S3 ), and are therefore unlikely to be involved in magnetotaxis. Similar granular particles concentrated near the anterior cell pole were reported in a magnetotactic protist ( 12 ) and have been observed in ciliates of the Tropidoatractidae family ( 26 ). PCR and phylogenetic analyses of the 18S rRNA gene, ITS1-5.8S-ITS2 region, and the first two barcoding domains of the 28S rRNA gene classified the large magnetotactic cells from our sample as ciliates within the class Armophorea ( Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 ). The ciliate robustly nests within the family Tropidoatractidae, members of the paraphyletic and predominantly anaerobic and free-living order Metopida, including the anaerobic endosymbiotic order Clevelandellida ( Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 ), a group of obligate anaerobes that live in the hindgut of a variety of invertebrates and vertebrates. The phylogenetic placement of the magnetotactic ciliates within the class Armophorea (SAL supercluster) is evolutionarily distinct from the recently reported magnetotactic ciliates that are more closely affiliated with the Prostomatea/Plagiopylea group within the CONThreeP supercluster ( 16 ). Considering this, the ability of ciliates to acquire magnetotaxis through endosymbiotic MTB is not restricted to a single evolutionary lineage and may be more widespread than previously recognized. Typical of armophoreans, the 18S molecule lacks the entire helix 9es3b in the V2 region ( SI Appendix, Fig. S6 ). The ITS2 molecule contains four unequally long helices radiating from a common loop: helices I and III are the longest; helix II is the shortest and most conserved (composed of invariably four ribonucleotide pairs and four ribonucleotides in its terminal loop, with no pyrimidine–pyrimidine mismatch); and helix IV is the most variable ( SI Appendix, Fig. S6 ). Compared to other free-living metopids, the putative secondary structure of ITS2 of the magnetotactic ciliate exhibits an additional bulge in helix IV ( SI Appendix, Fig. S7 ). At the 18S rRNA level, the ciliate is 93.70% similar to Tropidoatractus acuminatus, and 92.93 to 93.51% to other congeners, suggesting it is a Tropidoatractus species. No cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (cox1) could be amplified with ciliate-specific primers for cox1 (F388dT and R1184dT), indicating that the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex IV (for O2 respiration) may be incomplete. Transcripts encoding potential hydrogenosomal specific Fe-hydrogenases, were confirmed via phylogenetic analyses and form a clade sister to hydrogenosomal Fe-hydrogenases from anaerobic metopid ciliate transcriptomes ( Fig. 2B ). The expression of these transcripts and the absence of cox1 suggests an anaerobic energy metabolism for the ciliate, and the potential for the “mitochondrion-related organelles” (MROs), hydrogenosomes or H2-producing mitochondria ( 19 ). Morphological features and the 18S rRNA gene phylogenetic analysis identify the magnetotactic cells as ciliates corresponding to the Tropidoatractus genus. The ciliates have four to five ventral rows of cilia, elongated posterior cilia, and perizonal stripe cilia ( Fig. 2C ; see Taxonomic account). They also exhibit a posterior contractile vacuole and anterior granular aggregates. All Tropidoatractus species show the ability to aggregate dark granules in the anterior cell region, and dark or refractive granules are observed across all deep-branching lineages of the class Armophorea and in outgroup taxa from the class Parablepharismea ( SI Appendix, Fig. S8 ). According to stochastic mapping, the most recent common ancestor of armophoreans also possessed such granule aggregates. These were, however, lost in several lineages, including the sister genus Palmarella, and never reappeared in more derived clades ( SI Appendix, Fig. S8 ). Lineages without this plesiomorphic feature diversify much faster than those retaining anterior granule aggregates ( SI Appendix, Fig. S9 ). Metagenomics and Metatranscriptomics from Magnetically Sorted Ciliates. Following magnetic cell sorting, an average of 6 (n = 2 sorts) and 31 (n = 2 sorts) magnetotactic ciliate cells were used for metagenomic and metatranscriptomic analysis, respectively. Two separate magnetic sorts were performed as biological replicates, for both metagenomes and metatranscriptomes. Metagenomes were sequenced to an average depth of 220 million reads (n = 4 metagenomes, SD = 9.6 million), and metatranscriptomes were sequenced to an average depth of 58.5 million reads (n = 4 metatranscriptomes, SD = 8.5 million). Coassembly of all metagenome and metatranscriptome data using SqueezeMeta ( 27 ), followed by manual refinement (Materials and Methods), resulted in 48,273 open reading frames (ORFs) that could be annotated using the custom aggregate MetaProt ( 28 ) database (Materials and Methods). A relatively high number of these ORFs had the highest level of similarity with the ciliate, SRB, and methanogen ORFs from previously published and annotated genomes and transcriptomes ( SI Appendix, Fig. S10 ). The average relative abundance of the ciliate_bin was 14.5% (n = 4, SD = 5.5) and 81.7% (n = 4, SD = 11.1) in the metagenomes and metatranscriptomes, respectively ( SI Appendix, Fig. S10 ). The ciliate_bin dominated the transcriptome data ( Fig. 3B ), possibly due to the higher amount of mRNA produced by protists compared to prokaryotes ( 29 ) and (or) mRNA amplification by poly-A selecting oligos that are present in the oligo mixture used for the transcriptome amplification (Materials and Methods). Further binning using Anvi’o ( 30 ) and BUSCO ( 31 ) indicated that the preliminary ciliate_bin contained four separate subbins ( SI Appendix, Table S1 ), with genome completion ranging from 5.9 to 23%. This binning pattern is more consistent with cell- or strain-level variation in the genome content of a single ciliate host species than with the presence of multiple ciliate species. Eukaryotic genomes commonly produce fragmented assemblies and multiple bins per species due to variable ploidy, repetitive regions, uneven coverage, and the presence of multiple nuclei ( 32 ). The two observed size classes of magnetotactic ciliate cells (14.7 ± 2.6 µm vs. 38.6 ± 3.5 µm in length) likewise do not necessarily indicate distinct species, given that size variation of this magnitude is not unusual among ciliates. Within Tropidoatractus, published data report length differences of 1.5–2.1× between the smallest and largest individuals ( 26 ). In T. magnetotacticus, the ratio is ~2.6×, only slightly exceeding that observed in congeners but still well below the variations reported from other ciliate groups, where cell length can vary by up to 5× in culture ( 33 ). Moreover, the new species is defined by very distinctive morphology within the genus (specifically, the inconspicuous preoral dome that does not extend beyond the left body margin) and only a single 18S and ITS–28S region sequence was recovered., We therefore grouped all four subbins of the ciliate_bin into a single bin for further analysis. However, it remains possible that some of the recovered ciliate bins originated from the micronucleus (MIC), which may contain internal eliminated sequences (IESs) and alternative gene architectures absent from the macronuclear genome ( 34 ). In ciliates, MIC sequences are generally not expressed except during developmental stages, whereas the macronuclear genome typically serves as the primary source of mRNA ( 35 ). Mapping of RNAseq reads against the four ciliate bins revealed that 17 to 25% of contigs in each bin were expressed, and all four bins had similar expression profiles characterized by an exponential distribution in terms of RNAseq coverage ( SI Appendix, Fig. S11 ). These results indicate that the four ciliate bins correspond to the same genomic system, and that the reason for the four separate ciliate bins is more likely due to fragmented assemblies from the macronuclear genome of a single species and less likely due to MIC sequences or multiple species. The combined ciliate_bin, which includes all four subbins, likely reflects cell- or strain-level variation within a single ciliate species ( Fig. 3A ). The ciliate_bin contained 9,218 ORFs that could be annotated against an existing ciliate transcriptome or genome. Of those, 17 ORFs had the highest similarity to hydrogenosomal Fe-hydrogenases, closely related to Fe-Fe hydrogenases encoded by other anaerobic ciliates, such as Metopus and Nyctotherus ( 19 ). Phylogenetic analysis verified that these ORFs were hydrogenosomal Fe-hydrogenases and clustered with a strong bootstrap support alongside the homologs encoded by other anaerobic armophoraen ciliates ( Fig. 2B ). The hydrogenosomal Fe-hydrogenases were expressed by the ciliate_bin with an average relative expression of 1.6% (n = 4, SD = 0.5) of the total transcriptome reads mapped. Methanoregula, as in our case, seems to be the ancestral methanogen associated with representatives of the order Armophorida and the members of the order Metopida, including Palmarella and Tropidoatractus ( 42 ). Syntrophic relationships between anaerobic ciliate hosts and prokaryotic partners offer a more efficient anaerobic energy metabolism to holobionts in anoxic environments ( 17 ). We propose a similar metabolite-based syntrophy between the magnetotactic ciliate host species and two partners: a hydrogenotrophic methanogen and a magnetite-biomineralizing MTB. This is supported by the presence of distinct gene expression profiles, namely differences in average relative abundances of COG and eukaryote clusters of orthologous genes (KOG) categories in the metagenomes and metatranscriptomes of the three partners ( Fig. 3C ). Gene expression in the ciliate_bin was dominated by genes encoding cytoskeleton proteins [Z], which accounted for 34.9% of all the KOG categories in the metatranscriptomes, implying their role in maintaining and adjusting cell shape and potentially in housing the internal syntrophic partners. Metabolic reconstruction indicates that the MTB syntrophic partner harbors genes for dissimilatory sulfate reduction ( Fig. 5 ). Phylogenetic analysis of the alpha subunit of dsrA, encoded by the Magneto_SRB_bin shows they are most closely related to Desulfarculus sp. ( SI Appendix, Fig. S13 ), a group of SRB previously detected in a subsurface groundwater system ( 44 ). Since no cox1 genes were recovered from the ciliate using cox1-specific PCRs, and no cox1 sequences were detected in the metagenomes or transcriptomes, the host appears to have an incomplete aerobic respiration pathway. Instead, it likely relies on anaerobic ATP production in hydrogenosomes or H2-producing mitochondria via the expressed hydrogenosomal Fe-hydrogenases ( Fig. 2B ). Similar energy metabolic strategies have been described in several groups of anaerobic protists ( 19 ). The internal syntrophic partners of T. magnetotacticus likely act as sinks for H2 (and potentially other fermentation products), thereby improving the efficiency of the host’s energy metabolism. In tripartite symbioses, the endosymbiotic Methanoregula cells efficiently consume host-produced H2 ( 39 ). We considered the possibility that the magnetosomes and the MGC are not produced by a living syntrophic partner but rather derive from ingested prey. The fact that the ciliates showed active magnetotaxis in changing magnetic fields ( Movies S1–S4 ) argues against passive alignment due to ingested MTB ( 10 , 16 ). In addition, the “necklace-shaped” magnetosome chains observed were well preserved and consistently observed inside the ciliates over more than 1 y. The ellipsoidal shape of the chain would be unexpected if they had been ingested magnetosomes in digestive vacuoles, since ingested magnetosomes typically show discrete linear chains ( 9 , 10 ). The ellipsoidal “necklace-shaped” magnetosome chains within the ciliate were consistent in shape, size, and crystal morphology, contrasting with, e.g., degraded magnetosomes found in the predatory protist Uronemella parafilificum HQ ( 45 ). We therefore interpret the “necklace-shaped” magnetosome chains to be derived from a living (syntrophic) MTB organism within the ciliate host, rather than ingestion of MTB prey. This interpretation is consistent with the recent discovery of endosymbiotic MTB that live in a ciliate host, which also biomineralize bullet-shaped magnetosomes ( 16 ). Because we observed intact “necklace-shaped” magnetosome chains in subsamplings of the magnetotactic ciliates over an entire year, the syntrophic partnership appears to have been stable over time and preserved across many cellular reproductions and generations of the ciliate population. Moreover, rod-shaped bacteria were observed within the ciliates that contained the “necklace-shaped” magnetosome chains. Similar to endosymbiotic MTB living within ciliates ( 16 ), the rod-shaped bacteria were not organized into digestive vacuoles, indicating they were not ingested prey cells. This further indicates that the magnetosomes derive from MTB that live inside the ciliate. Nevertheless, it remains possible that the MTB are repeatedly acquired horizontally from a free-living pool. In this case, the MTB would not be used as a food source, but also would not be transmitted vertically. More research is needed to determine the exact mechanism by which ciliates acquire the internal MTB. Moreover, the phylogenetic analysis of mamAB from the Magneto_SRB_bin shows that the MTB biomineralizing the “necklace-shaped” magnetosome chains are affiliated with the Thermodesulfobacteriota ( Fig. 4A ). The mamAB from the Magneto_SRB_bin branches in between Desulfovibrio magneticus RS-1 and Fundidesulfovibrio magnetotacticus and the ectosymbiont MTB “Candidatus Desulfarcum epimagneticum” ( 14 ) ( Fig. 4A ). Similarly, the MGCs of MTB endosymbionts of freshwater ciliates are also closely related to D. magneticus RS-1 and F. magnetotacticus ( 16 ). The MGC expression indicates ongoing biomineralization ( Fig. 4B ), which would not be expected from ingested prey MTB. The synteny shared with the MGC of the ectosymbiont “Candidatus Desulfarcum epimagneticum” ( Fig. 4B ) further supports the interpretation that the internal MTB correspond to endosymbionts. However, demonstrating a true symbiosis requires showing that the partner organisms depend on each other for survival, evidence that could not be obtained due to the limited number of available cells. All attempts at fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) to independently verify the identity of the internal MTB also failed because of the low cell numbers. After the culture died following 2 y of maintenance, no additional material was available for further FISH analyses. Despite the lack of FISH confirmation, electron microscopy clearly shows rod-shaped bacteria containing magnetosomes inside the ciliates ( Fig. 1 ). When observed in Munich (Germany; inclination + 63.1°), the magnetotactic ciliates were south-seeking, but whether they exhibit the same behavior in their native habitat in Gabon (inclination –27.9°) remains unknown. In MTB, swimming polarity reverses within weeks when exposed to a reversed magnetic field ( 46 ), and we identified the magnetotactic ciliates after the sediment had been stored in Munich for 6 mo. Anaerobic, magnetotactic euglenid protists with ecto-symbiotic MTB have been found to be south-seekers in the northern and north-seekers in the southern geomagnetic hemisphere ( 14 ). The antiparallel swimming polarity likely enables these protists to approach their preferred habitat from the anaerobic sediment layers toward the OATZ ( 14 ). Thus, collective magnetotaxis in anaerobic protists, achieved by syntrophy with internal MTB, may facilitate adaptation to anoxic niches. Evolution of the Host in the Context of Earth’s History. Based on the time-calibrated 18S rRNA gene phylogeny, the lineage leading to T. magnetotacticus sp. nov. is estimated to have emerged at 172 ± 75 Ma ( Fig. 6 ). Although the node representing the most recent common ancestor of Tropidoatractus species cannot be interpreted as the origin of T. magnetotacticus itself, nor of its symbiosis with MTB, the error range places the emergence of the lineage leading to T. magnetotacticus sometime during the Mesozoic or the late Permian ( Fig. 6 ). The Mesozoic began with the Permian–Triassic boundary mass extinction event (251.9 Ma) and was characterized by repeated episodes of widespread anoxia ( 47 ). Tropidoatractus radiated in the Triassic period ~ 212 Ma ( SI Appendix, Fig. S15 ), about 40 Ma after the global Permian–Triassic extinction event (251.9 Ma). The mean estimated age of the lineage leading to T. magnetotacticus (172 Ma) is close to the Pliensbachian–Toarcian boundary (~183 Ma), a period marked by an extinction event associated with global ocean anoxia ( 48 ) ( Fig. 6 ). Freshwater environments (the present habitat of T. magnetotacticus sp. nov.) were also characterized by extensive anoxia during the Pliensbachian–Toarcian extinction event ( 48 ). Stochastic mapping indicates that the most recent common ancestor of the class Armophorea likely originated in a freshwater environment ca. 750 Ma ( SI Appendix, Fig. S16 ). The widespread anoxia of aquatic habitats due to excess nutrients during the Pliensbachian–Toarcian ( 47 ), where anaerobic magnetotactic ciliates would have lived, may have created additional ecological niches for anaerobes and facilitated subsequent invasion into anoxic brackish and marine sediments ( SI Appendix, Fig. S16 ). Macroevolutionary analyses show that speciation and net diversification rates accelerated after the Toarcian extinction event, but only within the endozoic clade ( SI Appendix, Figs. S9 and S17 ). Although the Toarcian was characterized by elevated extinction rates driven by global anoxia, new speciation events did occur ( 49 ). Conclusion The discovery of a tripartite syntrophy involving a ciliate host, magnetosome-forming bacteria, and methanogens expands our understanding of how magnetotaxis can emerge as a trait in eukaryotic evolution. We show that metabolic syntrophy enables magnetically guided niche adaptation in the ciliate host and represents a previously unrecognized form of microbial cooperation. Although magnetite biomineralization has been proposed for various microbes and even some higher organisms, definitive, particularly genetic, evidence for this process in eukaryotes remains elusive. The presence of syntrophic, magnetite-producing bacteria within a eukaryotic host raises questions about the evolution of magnetoreception and the ecological roles of magnetically responsive cooperation in sedimentary and aquatic environments. Magnetotaxis in eukaryotes could emerge as a consequence of niche adaptation. In MTB, magnetotaxis is believed to provide a vertical component for their orientation, guiding cells toward their optimal redox zone, which in stratified sediments is the OATZ ( 2 ). Assuming the south-seeking swimming of the magnetotactic ciliates observed in our study is reflective of their behavior in the natural environment in the southern magnetic hemisphere, acquisition of magnetotaxis via syntrophy might guide the anaerobic eukaryote host downward into anoxic sediment. Similarly, recently reported north-seeking magnetotactic ciliates containing endosymbiont MTB were discovered in anoxic sediment layers in the northern hemisphere, supporting niche adaptation of eukaryotes via magnetotaxis ( 16 ). Generally, niche adaptation via endosymbiosis is well documented in other ciliates, including host phototaxis enabled through the acquisition of endosymbiont algae that provide the symbionts with optimal conditions for photosynthesis ( 50 ). By analogy, the acquisition of magnetotaxis via endosymbiosis may represent a similar adaptive strategy in anaerobic eukaryotes, helping them navigate to their preferred redox zone deeper in sediments. For full details, see SI Appendix, Materials and Methods . Bioinformatic Analysis. Metagenomes and metatranscriptomes were coassembled using SqueezeMeta ( 27 ) and annotated according to a prior pipeline ( 28 ) (see Supplemental Methods). The annotation database was enriched with other ciliate transcriptomes from publicly available sources, including the Marine Microbial Eukaryote Transcriptome Sequencing Project (MMETSP) ( 76 ), Scuticociliatia, Armophorea, Litostomatea, and Spirostomum ( 42 , 43 , 77 ). Annotated ORFs were sorted into three preliminary bins: ciliate_bin, sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB_bin), and methanogen_bin and sorted as a function of GC percentage vs. coverage. Additional binning and manual refinement were then performed using MetaBat2 ( 36 ). Genome contamination and completeness of the aforementioned bins were also verified with CheckM ( 78 ) and BUSCO ( 31 ) for quality control. Raw reads were mapped to contigs using the BWA MEM Tool ( 79 ) and indexed using SAMtools ( 80 ) and processed using Anvi’o ( 30 ). ORFs from the SRB and methanogen bins were annotated against COG using BLASTp, while ciliate ORFs were annotated against KOG using BLASTp. Metatranscriptome reads were mapped against ciliate bins using Minimap2 ( 81 ) and SAMtools ( 80 ), and the expression profiles were visualized using R. For full details, see SI Appendix, Materials and Methods . Function Gene Phylogenies. A concatenated alignment of the mamA and mamB genes was performed in Seaview, using related mamA and mamB sequences obtained from BLASTp searches against NCBI-nr for closest hits. Phylogenetic trees were constructed with 100 bootstrap replicates and visualized using FigTree v.1.4.4 ( 64 ). The MGC encoded on the single contig (contig 35480) was characterized using Prokka ( 82 ), and the annotations of the encoded genes were verified by manual BLASTp searches against NCBI-nr. Transcriptome reads were collectively mapped against the MGC encoding contig in CLC Genomics Workbench. For full details, see SI Appendix, Materials and Methods . Modeling Diversification Dynamics. Speciation, extinction, and net diversification rates through time were estimated from the 18S time-calibrated tree using the BAMM (Bayesian Analysis of Macroevolutionary Mixtures) program ( 83 ). The best shift configuration, the 95% credible set of shift configurations, and the rate-through-time plots were obtained with the BAMMtools R package ( 84 ). Convergence to a stationary distribution (effective sample size > 200) was checked with the R package CODA ( 85 ) after discarding 10% of samples as burn-in. Four independent runs were conducted to confirm the consistency of the estimates. Finally, a lineage-through-time (LTT) plot was constructed from the 18S time-calibrated tree using laser v.2.3 ( 86 ) in R. For full details, see SI Appendix, Materials and Methods . Trait Mapping. Diversification dependent on lifestyle and anterior granule aggregate was analyzed using the R library diversitree v.0.10-0 ( 87 ). Ancestral lifestyle and the presence/absence of the anterior granule aggregate were reconstructed for nodes of the 18S time-calibrated tree using stochastic mapping and the SIMMAP function ( 88 ), as implemented in the R library phytools v.1.2-0 ( 89 ). Posterior probabilities for ancestral lifestyle and the presence/absence of the anterior granule aggregate at individual nodes were calculated by averaging the state frequencies across all map trees.
