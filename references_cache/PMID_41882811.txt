Phosphorus availability drives rhizosphere metabolite-microbial community interactions to modulate cucumber susceptibility to Fusarium wilt.

BACKGROUND: Agricultural intensification has led to excessive phosphorus fertilizer application on croplands, yet the causal linkage between soil phosphorus enrichment and soil-borne disease susceptibility mediated by rhizosphere microbial community remains unclear. This study aimed to decipher how phosphorus availability modulates cucumber susceptibility to Fusarium wilt by restructuring rhizosphere metabolite-microbial community interactions. RESULTS: We demonstrate that a moderately low phosphorus availability enhances cucumber resistance to Fusarium wilt. Amplicon sequencing analyses reveal that low phosphorus conditions promote microbial community stochastic assembly, enriching beneficial genera (bacterial genera Bacillus, Devosia, Sphingopyxis, Cupriavidus, and fungal genera Aspergillus and Amesia) with dual functions of phosphate solubilization and disease resistance, and enhancing cross-kingdom network robustness. In contrast, high phosphorus availability induced deterministic assembly and increased disease incidence by 62% compared to low phosphorus treatment. Metabolomics identified low phosphorus distinguished rhizosphere metabolites (succinic acid, azelaic acid, threonic acid, and methionine) that recruit beneficial taxa, whereas high phosphorus distinguished rhizosphere metabolites promoted pathogen growth. To validate the functional role of microbes-metabolites interaction, we constructed a synthetic microbial community composed of the signature taxa from the low phosphorus microbiota, which, when combined with low phosphorus metabolites, reduced pathogen abundance by 85% and conferred disease suppression under high phosphorus conditions. CONCLUSIONS: Our study establishes a phosphorus-driven mechanism whereby plant-metabolite-microbial community interactions orchestrate rhizosphere immunity. These findings provide new insights and potential strategies for sustainable disease management in agricultural systems with legacy phosphorus accumulation. Video Abstract.

Introduction The intensification of agricultural production has driven substantial inputs of inorganic phosphorus fertilizers into croplands. Globally, less than 20% of applied phosphorus is acquired by plants, with the majority accumulating in soils as legacy phosphorus [ 1 , 2 ]. While the role of phosphorus leaching in aquatic eutrophication is well established [ 3 , 4 ], the consequences of soil legacy phosphorus for plant health—particularly its implications for soil-borne disease dynamics—remain poorly understood. Mounting evidence indicates that phosphorus enrichment disrupts soil microbial community homeostasis [ 5 , 6 ]. However, whether and how elevated soil phosphorus availability increases plant susceptibility to pathogens remains a critical knowledge gap, with profound implications for agriculture. Cucumber ( Cucumis sativus ) is a widely cultivated economic crop. Cucumber Fusarium wilt, a soil-borne disease caused by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cucumerinum , has become a major challenge to global cucumber production [ 7 ], leading to significant yield and economic losses. Soil microbial communities underpin numerous critical ecosystem processes [ 8 ], with the rhizosphere, as a microbial hotspot enveloping plant roots, constituting a highly dynamic interface. Here, intricate cross-kingdom interactions orchestrate physiological and biochemical processes vital for plant growth and immunity, including enhanced nutrient solubilization, phytohormone production, and the induction of systemic resistance. Crucially, the functional capacity of these microbial communities is inherently context-dependent. Agricultural intensification, such as monoculture practices, often diminish rhizosphere microbial diversity and functional resilience, thereby potentially fostering the dominance of soil-borne pathogens [ 9 ]. The structural and functional integrity of the rhizosphere microbiome is increasingly recognized as fundamental to plant health [ 10 – 13 ]. However, how shifts in phosphorus availability driven by intensive fertilizer application shape the rhizosphere microbial community, and consequently influence plant-pathogen interactions, remains unclear. Specifically, elucidating the mechanisms through which phosphorus-driven changes modulate plant-rhizosphere microbial community interactions is critical. Addressing this gap requires a comprehensive understanding of alterations in the rhizosphere metabolome, microbial community assembly processes, and cross-kingdom network interactions. Ultimately, deciphering how these processes modulate plant-pathogen dynamics is essential for developing sustainable agricultural practices.

Phosphorus, as an essential plant nutrient, exerts profound regulatory control over plant immunity, metabolic reprogramming, and root exudation dynamics [ 6 , 14 ]. The phosphate starvation response (PSR), activated by phosphate deficiency, directly suppresses immune reactions and triggers metabolic reprogramming to alter resource allocation patterns [ 15 ]. Low phosphorus availability stimulates the synthesis of organic acids and the accumulation of anthocyanins and flavonoids in plants [ 16 , 17 ]. This metabolic shift directly drives substantial changes in the composition of root exudates. Conversely, phosphorus sufficiency accumulation upregulates genes involved in primary metabolism, including lipid metabolism and carbohydrate metabolic processes [ 18 ]. Research indicates that phosphorus overaccumulation can suppress pathogen-triggered immune signaling networks [ 19 ]. These phosphorus availability-dependent modulations of immunity and metabolism constitute fundamental determinants of plant-pathogen interactions. However, in the context of soil-borne disease, the net outcome of phosphorus availability on plant health is likely a product of the interplay between this direct physiological effect and the indirect, microbially mediated pathways shaped by the rhizosphere environment [ 20 – 22 ]. Nevertheless, despite the established role of phosphorus in direct plant immunity [ 21 ], the mechanisms by which phosphorus availability orchestrates the rhizosphere metabolome to recruit a protective microbial community, and how this process ultimately governs disease outcomes, are still poorly understood. The specific mechanisms linking phosphorus-driven root exudation, microbial community assembly, and the emergence of disease-suppressive functions in the rhizosphere constitute a critical knowledge gap in plant–microbe interactions.

This study aims to decipher the interrelationships among phosphorus availability, rhizosphere metabolite profiles, and microbial community assembly during the pathogenesis of cucumber Fusarium wilt. We first assessed Fusarium wilt severity under a gradient of phosphorus availability. Using an integrated approach combining amplicon sequencing, metabolomics, and cross-kingdom network analysis, we aimed to (i) characterize phosphorus-dependent shifts in microbial community assembly and stability; (ii) identify key bacterial and fungal taxa enriched under low phosphorus conditions and define the characteristic metabolite profiles that distinguish the rhizospheres of low- and high-phosphorus plants; and (iii) uncover the mechanisms by which these key taxa and metabolites mediate disease suppression under moderately low phosphorus conditions. Finally, we validated the synergistic effect of the low phosphorus-enriched key taxa and metabolites on pathogen suppression using a synthetic microbial community (SynCom), thereby elucidating how phosphorus availability modulates plant susceptibility to Fusarium wilt.

Results Phosphorus supply level affects cucumber disease occurrence and rhizosphere microbial community To investigate the impact of phosphorus nutrition on cucumber Fusarium wilt, infection assays with Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cucumerinum (FOC) were conducted using cucumbers ( Cucumis sativus cultivar: Changchun Mici) grown under three distinct phosphorus regimes. Results demonstrated significantly heightened susceptibility in high phosphorus (HP) plants, exhibiting an 84.5% disease incidence, 40% higher than cucumbers under normal phosphorus (NP) conditions. Conversely, low phosphorus (LP) grown cucumbers showed a 22.2% incidence rate, representing a 22.3% reduction compared to normal phosphorus treatment (Fig. 1 A, B; Supplementary Fig. 1). To determine whether the rhizosphere microbial community is involved in the relationship between phosphorus levels and cucumber Fusarium wilt occurrence, we inoculated axenically grown cucumbers under differential phosphorus regimes with the pathogen FOC. There was no significant difference in disease incidence between LP and NP conditions (69.3% and 70.6%; p > 0.05). In contrast, HP plants exhibited significantly higher susceptibility, with 79.7% incidence ( p < 0.05) (Fig. 1 C). This indicated that the rhizosphere microbial community contributed to Fusarium wilt suppression under low phosphorus conditions.

LP: low phosphorus treatment; NP: normal phosphorus treatment; HP: high phosphorus treatment We then analyzed the rhizosphere bacterial community of cucumber under three phosphorus supply levels. Richness-based alpha diversity significantly decreased under both LP and HP versus NP conditions. Ordination analysis via PCA showed clear treatment-based segregation (88.18% cumulative variance), with ANOSIM validating a strong phosphorus effect on β-diversity ( R = 0.905, p = 0.001, Fig. 1 D). Proteobacteria maintained predominance across all rhizosphere samples. Compared to the significant enrichment of Bacteroidetes (11.57%) in NP rhizospheres, both HP and LP treatments exhibited substantially reduced relative abundances of this phylum. Compared with the LP group, the HP group showed significantly higher relative abundances of Actinobacteria (7.92%), but a significantly lower relative abundance of Bacteroidetes (6.95%) and Firmicutes (2.64%) (Supplementary Fig. 2). The circular heatmap of rhizosphere bacterial genera revealed a significant enrichment of Bacillus (18.86%) with progressively decreasing phosphorus levels; conversely, the relative abundance of Massilia (0.41%) markedly increased with rising phosphorus levels (Fig. 1 E).

For rhizosphere fungal communities, LP treatment induced a significant reduction in richness-based alpha diversity. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the first two principal components collectively explained 88.08% of total variation (PC1: 50.87%; PC2: 37.21%). Phosphorus regimes significantly altered fungal community structure (ANOSIM R = 0.995, p = 0.001) (Fig. 1 F). Ascomycota and Basidiomycota predominated across all treatments. Compared to the HP and NP treatment, the relative abundances of Ascomycota (16.03%), Basidiomycota (4.00%), and Glomeromycota (2.32%) were significantly higher under LP conditions (Supplementary Fig. 3). Analysis of genus-level relative abundances revealed divergent responses among dominant rhizosphere fungi to progressive phosphorus reduction: Aspergillus (7.04%) abundance increased significantly, while Rhizopus (<0.01%), Fusarium (0.08%), and Gonostomum (0.29%) exhibited marked declines under phosphorus limitation (Fig. 1 G). To identify dominant microorganisms under differential phosphorus regimes, differential abundance analysis was performed using the TMM method [ 23 ]. Microbial community analysis revealed significant enrichment of specific taxa under contrasting conditions: bacterial genera such as Bacillus and Devosia in LP conditions, and among rhizosphere fungi, Gonostomum , Ascobolus , Fusarium , and Rhizopus in the HP group compared to Aspergillus and Amesia in the LP group (Fig. 1 H, I). Using random forest analysis to identify microorganisms significantly correlated with different phosphorus nutrient levels, we identified the top 40 most discriminative microbial features. Both the fungal and bacterial communities achieved perfect discrimination (100% accuracy) based on these features. The complete list of the top 40 discriminatory microbial features, along with their importance rankings, taxonomic information, and differential abundance between groups, are detailed in Supplementary Tables 3–4 and Supplementary Fig. 4–5.

Identifying rhizosphere microbial assembly and network drivers under different phosphorus levels β-Nearest taxon index (βNTI) analysis of rhizosphere microbial communities across phosphorus gradients revealed deterministic assembly under HP conditions (βNTI > 2) for both bacteria and fungi. In contrast, stochastic processes dominated community assembly in LP and NP treatments, with |βNTI|< 2 indicating neutral processes (Fig. 2 A, B). Cross-kingdom network analysis revealed increased edge numbers in cucumber rhizosphere fungal-bacterial association networks under both HP and LP treatments. Crucially, LP networks exhibited significantly more negatively correlated edges between fungi and bacteria, whereas HP networks were dominated by positive correlations (Supplementary Table 5). Vulnerability assessment further demonstrated that rhizosphere networks under HP were structurally more vulnerable than those under NP and LP conditions, with LP cross-kingdom networks displaying the highest stability (Fig. 2 C). Modularity analysis of cross-kingdom microbial networks revealed increasing modular complexity with decreasing phosphorus availability in the cucumber rhizosphere. Keystone taxa within major LP modules included the bacterial genera Bacillus , Devosia , Sphingopyxis , and Cupriavidus , alongside the fungal genus Aspergillus . In contrast, HP networks exhibited higher topological centrality of Massilia , Rhizopus , and Fusarium (Fig. 2 D). Through integrated evaluation of differential abundance, contribution magnitude (defined by machine learning as the most discriminatory taxa), and importance in network modules, we designated bacterial genera Bacillus , Devosia , Sphingopyxis , and Cupriavidus , along with fungal genera Aspergillus and Amesia , as signature microbial taxa for the LP group. These taxa may play roles in cucumber Fusarium wilt resistance under phosphorus limitation. Conversely, bacterial genera Massilia and Cellvibrio , and fungal genus Rhizopus were identified as signature microbial taxa for the HP group (Supplementary Table 6). These organisms may contribute to enhanced Fusarium disease severity in phosphorus-replete conditions.

LP: low phosphorus treatment; NP: normal phosphorus treatment; HP: high phosphorus treatment Exploring the key metabolites involved in the cucumber disease occurrence under different phosphorus levels To gain a deeper understanding of the relationship between phosphorus nutrition, plants, and rhizosphere microorganisms, rhizosphere metabolites were analyzed using Gas Chromatography-Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (GC-TOF-MS). A total of 336 compounds were identified within the rhizosphere soil metabolome across all samples. Among these 336 compounds, the profile included 2 benzenoids, 1 homogeneous non-metal compound, 1 lignan, neolignan or related compound, 21 lipids and lipid-like molecules, 4 nucleosides, nucleotides, and analogues, 33 organic acids and derivatives, 1 organic nitrogen compound, 34 organic oxygen compounds, 6 organoheterocyclic compounds, 36 miscellaneous compounds, and 196 unidentified compounds. Specifically, in the HP rhizosphere, the relative abundance of organic acids and derivatives was significantly decreased, while those of organic oxygen compounds and lipids and lipid-like molecules were significantly increased (Fig. 3 A). PLS-DA analysis revealed distinct metabolic profiles among the three experimental groups (P1mM, P2mM, and P500uM). The score plot (Fig. 3 B) showed that the first principal component (PC1) explained 48.4% of the total variance in metabolomic data, while the second component (PC2) explained 8.8%, with a cumulative explained variance of 57.1% for the first two components. Statistical significance of group separation was confirmed by ANOVA for both PC1 ( p = 0.0003) and PC2 ( p = 0.0369). Loading plot analysis identified the top 20 metabolites contributing most to principal component 1 (PC1) (Fig. 3 C). Additionally, the top 40 metabolites identified by Random Forest analysis for discriminating among the three groups (Supplementary Fig. 6) are potential key group-distinguishing metabolites involved in plant–microbe interactions. For example, compounds such as galactonic acid, 9-fluorenone, 2-deoxy-D-glucose, and azelaic acid were identified based on their high contribution to group discrimination. The Random Forest model achieved an accuracy of 72.2% (Supplementary Fig. 6). Nonparametric testing revealed 33 metabolites exhibiting significant differences ( p < 0.05) between the HP and LP groups (Supplementary Table 7). The top 20 metabolites exhibiting the greatest differences among the three groups are presented in Fig. 3 D. Integrating results from the loading plot, Random Forest analysis, and nonparametric tests, we defined succinic acid, azelaic acid, threonic acid, and methionine as LP-distinguished rhizosphere metabolites (LPDRM), and myo-inositol, dihydroxyacetone, sorbitol, and 3-aminoisobutyric acid as HP-distinguished rhizosphere metabolites (HPDRM) for further investigation.

Integrated analysis of rhizosphere metabolites and microbial community Integrated analysis of rhizosphere metabolites and microbiota (fungi and bacteria) was performed to uncover potential mechanisms linking the phosphorus-regulated rhizosphere environment to differential Fusarium wilt susceptibility. Redundancy Analysis (RDA) revealed that RDA axis 1 accounted for 31% of the variation in the microbial community composition. The negative direction of RDA1 was significantly associated with LPDRM compounds, indicating their positive regulatory role in shaping the LP group microbial community. RDA axis 2 independently explained 23% of the microbial community variation. The positive direction of RDA2 was strongly driven by HPDRM compounds, suggesting a positive influence of these metabolites on the HP group microbial community (Fig. 4 A). The heatmap illustrates the correlation patterns between signature microbial taxa and distinguished metabolites. Cluster analysis (using Euclidean distance and Ward’s linkage algorithm) revealed distinct co-occurrence modules grouping specific microbial taxa with metabolites. Notably, signature microbial taxa characteristic of the LP group exhibited strong positive correlations with LPDRM, while showing negative correlations with HPDRM. Conversely, signature microbial taxa associated with the HP group displayed strong positive correlations with their corresponding HPDRM compounds (Fig. 4 B). This pattern suggests potential functional linkages between these specific microbial groups and metabolite suites within their respective phosphorus regimes. The metabolite-fungal-bacterial co-occurrence network was constructed using the SparCC correlation algorithm (| ρ |> 0.7, FDR-corrected q < 0.05), revealing higher-order interactions among differentially enriched fungi, bacteria, and host-derived metabolites (Fig. 4 C). Topological analysis demonstrated significant modular structure across all treatments, with the LP treatment exhibiting a significantly higher number of functional modules than the HP group. Within the LP network, organic acid compounds served as central hubs. The interactions within the NP network were predominantly microbe-microbe associations, while the HP network showed reduced fungal-bacterial-metabolite connectivity. This network visualization illustrates the potential capacity of cross-kingdom microbial community-metabolite interactions to influence host disease resistance phenotypes.

LP: low phosphorus treatment; NP: normal phosphorus treatment; HP: high phosphorus treatment Functional verification of specific microbial taxa and distinguished metabolites To gain deeper mechanistic insights into how specific microbial taxa and distinguished metabolites contribute to plant disease resistance, we isolated a total of 288 microbial isolates. Among these, 62 isolates (37 bacterial and 25 fungal strains) representing six key genera were identified under LP conditions. Following a comprehensive pairwise antagonism assay, mutually compatible consortia (i.e., strains exhibiting no inhibitory activity against each other) were selected for multi-dimensional (19 bacterial and 9 fungal strains) in vitro functional characterization. Growth curve assays under the two groups of distinguished rhizosphere metabolite (HPDRM and LPDRM) assessed the strains’ ability to utilize these metabolites (Fig. 5 A). None of the nine tested fungal strains or nineteen bacterial strains demonstrated the ability to utilize HPDRM (Fig. 5 A). Several fungal strains grew well in medium containing LPDRM as the sole carbon and nitrogen source, producing significantly higher mycelial biomass compared to the 1/5 LB medium control. The pathogen (FOC) utilized HPDRM effectively, showing significantly higher mycelial production in HPDRM compared to the control. This may explain the persistently high disease incidence of Fusarium wilt observed even in axenic high phosphorus conditions. The vast majority of bacteria exhibited significantly higher maximum biomass in LPDRM than in the 1/5 LB medium control groups. We subsequently tested the chemotactic responses of bacterial strains to the two DRM groups. The results showed that ten tested strains exhibited positive chemotaxis toward LPDRM (Fig. 5 B, Supplementary Table 8). However, while some strains demonstrated the ability to utilize LPDRM, they showed no chemotactic response to it. The antagonistic effect of characteristic microbiota against the pathogen is considered a potential factor contributing to the plant disease-resistant phenotype. Consequently, we assessed the impact of individual strains on the pathogen FOC using a dual-culture plate assay. Only five bacterial strains demonstrated strong antagonistic activity against the pathogen (pathogen inhibition rate > 0.4), whereas none of the tested fungal strains exhibited antagonism. Notably, the vast majority of fungal strains displayed a competitive advantage over pathogen growth (Fig. 5 C).

Exploring low phosphorus-mediated rhizosphere cross-kingdom interactions and disease suppression To the comprehensive evaluation of in vitro interactions and colonization capabilities among the strains, 13 bacterial and 6 fungal strains were ultimately selected to form the synthetic community for testing their internal interactive dynamics. The evolutionary relationships and taxonomy of these strains are presented in Fig. 6 A and B, respectively. The bacterial component of the SynCom comprised six Bacillus strains, three Cupriavidus strains, two Sphingopyxis strains, and two Devosia strains. The fungal component consisted of five Aspergillus strains and one Amesia strain. Given the critical roles biofilms play in rhizosphere microbial colonization and mobility, we further investigated the bacterial-fungal interactions within the SynCom. The results (Fig. 6 C) revealed complex interaction patterns: (i) Mutual promotion (reflected by concurrent enhancement of bacterial biofilm formation and fungal colony diameter) was observed between specific fungal-bacterial pairs, including: Amesia sp. F7 & Devosia sp. B2; Amesia sp. F7 & Bacillus sp. B10; Aspergillus sp. F5 and Bacillus sp. B18. (ii) Unidirectional facilitation (where one partner enhanced the growth of the other without a reciprocal benefit) characterized other interactions, including Aspergillus sp. F4 & Bacillus sp. B10; Aspergillus sp. F3 & Cupriavidus sp. B12; Aspergillus sp. F5 & Cupriavidus sp. B12). These findings demonstrate the intricate network of fungal-bacterial interactions within the low phosphorus rhizosphere microbial ecosystem.

Pathogen FOC abundance in soil varied significantly across treatments after two weeks (Fig. 6 D). The results demonstrate that, compared to the control, both the co-application of bacterial metabolites with fungal strains and the combination of fungal metabolites with bacterial strains significantly reduced the FOC population, with no significant difference between these two treatments (Fig. 6 D). Discussion This study investigates the critical role of phosphorus availability, rhizosphere metabolite dynamics, and microbial community assembly in shaping susceptibility to Fusarium wilt ( Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cucumerinum ) in cucumber. Our findings demonstrate that high phosphorus availability increases cucumber susceptibility to Fusarium wilt, whereas low phosphorus availability enhances host resistance. This outcome appears to contrast with some previous studies: Castrillo [ 24 ] et al. reported that plants under phosphorus limitation may prioritize nutrient acquisition and uptake at the expense of appropriate defense responses. Similarly, Cao [ 6 ] et al. indicated that alleviating phosphorus stress can mitigate bacterial wilt ( Ralstonia solanacearum ) severity in tomato. However, other research aligns with our results, showing that excessive phosphorus increases susceptibility to rice blast ( Magnaporthe oryzae ) [ 5 ]. Collectively, these studies underscore the complexity of phosphorus-host–pathogen interactions, where factors such as pathogen type, plant genotype, and soil/rhizosphere microbial conditions critically influence the outcome.

Pathogen inoculation assays under axenic conditions across a phosphorus gradient revealed that the rhizosphere microbial community plays a pivotal role in mediating phosphorus-dependent plant susceptibility. The sustained high disease incidence in plants under axenic high phosphorus conditions may arise from dual mechanisms: intrinsic physiological alterations in hosts and pathogen hijacking of rhizosphere metabolites. Specifically, excessive phosphorus accumulation impairs defense-related gene expression in plants [ 5 ], while concurrently, the enrichment of rhizosphere metabolites induced by high phosphorus conditions provided pathogens with accessible carbon and nitrogen sources, directly facilitating their proliferation (Fig. 5 A). The high phosphorus soil microbial community, assembled primarily through deterministic processes, was associated with increased pathogen proliferation and plant infection. This observation is consistent with our previous reports on microbial community assembly under high nutrient conditions [ 25 ]. The prevalence of negative correlations in low phosphorus rhizosphere networks (Fig. 2 C; Supplementary Table 5) suggests intensified inter-microbial competition under phosphorus limitation. This competitive landscape likely favors bacterial taxa with high phosphorus-use efficiency (PUE), as indicated by the selective enrichment of Bacillus , Devosia , and other PUE specialists [ 26 ] in LP conditions (Fig. 1 H), alongside their superior growth on LP-specific metabolites (Fig. 5 A). Conversely, specialist species occupying narrow ecological niches exhibited greater survival under low phosphorus compared to high phosphorus environments [ 27 , 28 ]. This explains the significantly higher bacterial α-diversity observed under phosphorus limitation. For fungi, filamentous fungi exhibited a competitive advantage under resource scarcity. This intense competition and exclusion resulted in the displacement of fungal species with slower growth rates or inferior phosphorus acquisition capabilities, consequently reducing fungal community α-diversity under low phosphorus [ 29 ].

Metabolomic analysis revealed a profound impact of phosphorus availability on the cucumber rhizosphere chemical environment. HP treatment significantly decreased the relative abundance of organic acids and their derivatives, while concurrently increasing organic oxidates and lipids (Fig. 3 A). This suggests that phosphorus excess may suppress the root capacity to secrete specific defense-related metabolites. Critically, cross metabolite supplementation of different phosphorus levels directly established the causal role of these phosphorus-responsive metabolites in Fusarium wilt development (Fig. 3 E). Regardless of ambient phosphorus levels, adding metabolites distinguished from HP conditions significantly exacerbated disease incidence, reaching levels comparable to HP controls. Conversely, supplementation with metabolites distinguished from low phosphorus conditions effectively suppressed disease progression (Fig. 3 F). This strongly indicates that phosphorus availability indirectly modulates host resistance by reshaping the rhizosphere metabolome. This metabolite-mediated resistance shift suggests a hypothesis to resolve the paradox where an HP environment promotes plant growth yet concurrently creates a rhizosphere microhabitat conducive to soil-borne disease outbreaks. Conversely, under LP stress, plants establish an indirect defense barrier via metabolic reprogramming.

Both low phosphorus and high phosphorus treatments increased the connectivity within the fungal-bacterial interaction network. However, the nature of these associations appeared to differ: in the LP network, negative correlations were more frequently observed, while positive correlations were most common in the HP network (Supplementary Table 5). This shift in association patterns suggested enhanced microbial competition under phosphorus stress. Scarcity of available phosphorus in the LP environment likely intensified antagonistic interactions or resource competition among microorganisms for limited nutrients. Concurrently, modular structure analysis of the microbe-metabolite co-occurrence network revealed a significant increase in the number of functional modules under LP conditions. This indicated the formation of more refined cooperative patterns between the microbial community and rhizosphere metabolites. Within this structure, organic acids functioned as central hubs, connecting phosphate-solubilizing bacteria and fungi. This highly modular architecture not only optimized resource competition strategies but also enhanced system stability through organic acid-mediated chemical defense [ 30 ]. Conversely, HP conditions increased positive correlations among microorganisms. This aligned with weakened connectivity within the microbe-metabolite network and reduced modular complexity. In this environment, the enrichment of rhizosphere metabolites created an ecological basis characterized by resource redundancy. This redundancy diminished functional cooperation among microbes, contributing to reduced network stability. Notably, the NP network exhibited balanced microbial cooperation, reflecting the equilibrium of the rhizosphere ecology under non-extreme conditions.

Our investigation into the interactions within the low phosphorus derived SynCom revealed a complex web of volatile organic compounds-mediated cross-kingdom collaborations, including both mutual facilitation and unidirectional enhancement between specific bacterial-fungal pairs [ 31 – 33 ]. This intricate communication provides a mechanistic explanation for the stability and efficacy of the stochastically assembled low phosphorus microbial community. Furthermore, the functional characterization of these strains confirmed that their beneficial role is multifaceted: they not only directly antagonized the pathogen but also exhibited significant phosphate-solubilizing capacity, which translated into increased shoot Pi content in the host plant (Supplementary Fig. 9). Most importantly, the intact, living SynCom demonstrated superior antimicrobial efficacy compared to any individual metabolite combination. This dual functionality of enhancing plant nutrition while suppressing disease underscores a sophisticated adaptive strategy within the low-phosphorus microbial community. The fact that optimal pathogen suppression was achieved by combining the SynCom with low-phosphorus distinguished metabolites (LPDRMs) indicates that their ecological coupling establishes a defense barrier superior to that of any single component, demonstrating the integrated mechanism behind low phosphorus induced resistance.

This study demonstrates that phosphorus availability modulates the susceptibility of cucumber to Fusarium wilt by fundamentally restructuring the rhizosphere microenvironment. Specifically, under high phosphorus conditions, resource redundancy generated jointly by rhizosphere metabolite structure and elevated phosphorus levels increased positive correlations among rhizosphere microbiota. This directly contributed to reduced stability within the cross-kingdom microbial network. Furthermore, pathogens exploit phosphorus-induced rhizosphere metabolites as nutritional resources, facilitating the proliferation of Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cucumerinum . Conversely, low phosphorus availability induced a reconstructed rhizosphere metabolome dominated by organic acids, which in turn fostered a stochastically assembled rhizosphere microbial community. Low phosphorus conditions promoted a stable, modular microbe-metabolite network architecture characterized by prevalent negative microbial correlations, enhanced functional coordination, and the central role of organic acids as hubs connecting phosphate-solubilizing microbial community and bolstering system resilience. Crucially, both the application of low phosphorus metabolites alone and their integration with the low phosphorus-derived SynCom effectively suppressed Fusarium wilt even under high phosphorus conditions. This underscores the emergence of defensive properties arising from synergistic microbe-metabolite interactions. Collectively, these findings establish phosphorus availability as a regulator of rhizosphere disease suppressive capacity. It operates through an integrated continuum where plant physiological responses, metabolite-mediated cross-kingdom interactions, and microbial community assembly processes collectively determine pathogen susceptibility.

Conclusion This study elucidates a phosphorus-dependent regulatory mechanism governing rhizosphere microbial community assembly and soil-borne disease susceptibility. It was demonstrated that low phosphorus availability promotes the formation of a stochastically assembled, stable microbe-metabolite network centered around organic acids, which enhances resistance against Fusarium wilt. In contrast, high phosphorus availability facilitates deterministic assembly and enriches metabolites that promote pathogen proliferation, thereby increasing plant disease susceptibility (Fig. 7 ). Our study shifts the perspective on phosphorus management from focusing solely on plant nutrition to comprehensively considering soil ecological health and disease dynamics. This implies that agricultural systems with long-term phosphorus accumulation face higher risks of crop disease. Therefore, rational phosphorus fertilization strategies should account for the dual effects of phosphorus on soil microbial community ecology and plant nutrition. In the context of sustainable agriculture, future research should further explore the phosphorus-driven mechanisms underlying different crop types and diseases, as well as how to optimize phosphorus management to enhance soil health and crop disease resistance.

The central circle emphasizes the pivotal role of phosphorus in mediating these interactions among metabolites, fungi, bacteria, and pathogens, ultimately determining the level of plant disease resistance Materials and methods Plants and growth conditions The soil used in this experiment was collected from the Baima town (31° 58′ N, 119° 17′ E) of Nanjing, Jiangsu Province, China, which has a subtropical monsoon climate, with an annual mean total precipitation of 1200 mm and an average annual temperature of 15.4 °C. The soil chemical properties are shown in Supplementary Table 1. The soil was collected from a maize field, and transported to the laboratory in airtight coolers and stored at 4 °C until use. Before the start of the experiment, all soils were dried at room temperature, pooled, and thoroughly passed through a 2-mm sieve to remove roots and other plant tissues, and the same batch of soil was used for all experiments.

Cucumber of C. sativus (cultivar “Changchunmici”) were sown in seedling trays (50 wells per tray) that were placed in a greenhouse under 23/20 °C day/night with a photoperiod of 16/8 day/night, a relative humidity of 50%, and a light intensity of 200 μmol −2 s −1 . Each cell (5 cm × 5 cm × 15 cm) contained 100 g of growth substrate formulated as 10% (v/v) soil and 90% (v/v) vermiculite. Trays were randomly re-positioned in the greenhouse weekly. Plants were watered three times per week from the bottom until the substrate was moist.

Preparation of pathogen Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cucumerinum (FOC) spores The Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cucumerinum (FOC) strain used in this study was preserved in glycerol at −80 °C in our laboratory. To obtain spores, the frozen FOC stock was first cultured on solid Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) medium at 28 °C for 48 h. Subsequently, it was transferred to fresh Potato Dextrose Broth (PDB) medium and incubated at 28 °C for 1 week. The resulting culture was then centrifuged at 8000 × g for 2 min. The pellet was resuspended in sterile distilled water and adjusted to a final concentration of 5 × 10⁶ spores·mL⁻ 1 .

Greenhouse experiment: effects of phosphorus supply on cucumber Fusarium wilt During planting, cucumber seeds were surface-sterilized with 75% ethanol for 1 min, followed by immersion in 1% sodium hypochlorite for 15 min [ 34 ]. The sterilized seeds were then incubated on moist filter paper in the dark at room temperature for 2 days. Uniformly germinated seedlings were selected and transplanted into the aforementioned seedling trays for cultivation. Throughout the growth period, all potted cucumber plants were supplied with Johnson nutrient medium [ 21 ] once per week (KNO 3 (0.6 g L −1 ), Ca(NO 3 ) 2 ·4H 2 O (0.9 g L −1 ), MgSO 4 ·7H 2 O (0.2 g L −1 ), KCl (3.8 mg L −1 ), H 3 BO 3 (1.5 mg L −1 ), MnSO 4 ·H 2 O (0.8 mg L −1 ), ZnSO 4 ·7H 2 O (0.6 mg L −1 ), CuSO 4 ·5H 2 O (0.1 mg L −1 ), H 2 MoO 4 (16.1 μg L −1 ), FeSO 4 ·7H 2 O (1.1 mg L −1 ), Myo-Inositol (0.1 g L −1 ), MES (0.5 g L −1 ), pH 5.6–5.7). Phosphorus (Pi), supplied as KH₂PO₄, was added to the solution at concentrations varying according to the experimental treatment. Three phosphorus treatments were established: low phosphorus (LP), normal phosphorus (NP), and high phosphorus (HP), corresponding to final Pi concentrations of 500 µM, 1 mM, and 2 mM, respectively, added individually to their designated media solutions. Plants were watered three times per week from the bottom until the substrate was moist. The available phosphorus content for each treatment group substrate at the end of the experiment is presented in Supplementary Table 2. Each treatment comprised three seedling trays, serving as three biological replicates, with 50 seedlings per replicate. At 30 days after cucumber planting, rhizosphere soil sampling was performed according to established methods [ 35 ]. The rhizosphere samples were stored at 4 °C for microbial strain isolation and at −80 °C for subsequent microbial community and metabolites analysis. Concurrently, plant shoot tissues were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent determination of inorganic phosphate concentration. Within each biological replicate (seedling tray), rhizosphere soil from three randomly selected cucumber plants was pooled to form one composite sample. Two such composite samples were collected per tray. Consequently, for each phosphorus treatment (with three independent trays as biological replicates), a total of six composite rhizosphere samples were obtained (3 trays × 2 samples per tray). The method of Ames [ 36 ] was used to determine the phosphate concentration in the shoots of plants. Subsequently, each tray was inoculated with 500 mL of FOC spore suspension (5 × 10⁶ spores·mL⁻ 1 ). Disease incidence was recorded after 18 days of pathogen inoculation.

Greenhouse experiment: effects of phosphorus supply on cucumber Fusarium wilt under sterile conditions To evaluate whether rhizosphere microorganisms influence the occurrence of cucumber Fusarium wilt under different phosphorus supply, plants were cultivated under sterile conditions. Surface-sterilized cucumber seeds were germinated on 1/2 MS solid medium. Sterile seedlings were then transplanted into transparent bags (320 mm × 450 mm × 200 mm; Sigma-Aldrich) containing 100 g of vermiculite. Prior to transplantation, both bags and vermiculite underwent four sequential cycles of autoclaving (121 °C, 20 min) followed by drying (80 °C, 24 h). Three phosphorus treatments were maintained: low phosphorus (LP), normal phosphorus (NP), and high phosphorus (HP). Each treatment included six biological replicates, with three cucumber plants per replicate. Sterilized nutrient solution (identical to the aforementioned formulation) was added weekly to each bag for 30 days. Vermiculite from every individual bag was subjected to dilution plating on nutrient agar (NA) prior to pathogen inoculation to confirm sterility. Subsequently, each validated sterile bag was inoculated with a FOC spore suspension (1 × 10⁶ spores/mL, as previously described). Disease incidence was recorded after 18 days of pathogen inoculation.

Rhizosphere microbial communities profiling A sample of 0.5 g rhizosphere soil collected was used for total genomic DNA extraction with the PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit (Qiagen, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions [ 37 ]. The DNA concentration and quality were assessed on 1% agarose gels and with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For bacterial community analysis, the V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primers 515F: GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA and 806R: GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT 58 which yielded an amplicon length of 292 bp. For the fungal community analysis, PCR targeting the ITS2 region was carried out with the primers ITS3-F: GCATCGATGAAGAACGCAGC and ITS4-R: TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC) [ 38 ].

Amplicon library preparation and high throughput sequencing were performed as described previously [ 39 ]. Briefly, the 50 μL reaction mixtures contained 25 μL 2 × Premix Taq (Takara Biotechnology, Dalian, China), 1 μL each primer (10 μM), 3 μL DNA (20 ng/μL), and 20 μL of sterilized ultrapure water. PCR amplification was performed using a Bio-Rad S1000 (Bio-Rad Laboratory) with the following cycles: 95 °C for 5 min, then 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 52 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Products were separated on a 1% agarose gel and those with clear bands between 290 and 310 bp were combined for sequencing. PCR products were mixed in ratios of equal density according to GeneTools analysis software (version 4.03.05.0, SynGene), and the mixtures were purified with an E. Z. N. A Gel Extraction Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA). Sequencing libraries were generated using the NEBNext® UltraTM DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Bio-labs, Ipswich, MA, USA) following the manufacturer recommendations. Index codes were added and library quality was assessed with a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Scientific) and an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Finally, the library was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq6000 platform, yielding an average of approximately 200,000 and 150,000 high-quality sequences per sample for bacterial and fungal communities, respectively. High-quality reads processed with USEARCH (V. 10.1) [ 40 ].

Bioinformatics Bioinformatic processing of the raw paired-end reads was performed using Usearch (V. 10.1) and vsearch (V. 0.6.3) pipelines [ 41 ]. First, paired-end reads were merged using “vsearch--fastq_mergepairs” with a minimum overlap length of 20 bp (“--fastq_minovlen 20”). For 16S rRNA gene sequences (V4 region), primer sequences were trimmed using the command “--fastq_stripleft 29 --fastq_stripright 18”. For ITS2 sequences, the corresponding primer trimming parameters were “--fastq_stripleft 20 --fastq_stripright 20”. Stringent quality filtering was then applied using “vsearch --fastx_filter” with a maximum expected error rate of 0.01 (“--fastq_maxee_rate 0.01”), followed by removal of reads outside the expected length range. High-quality sequences were dereplicated using “vsearch--derep_fulllength” with a minimum abundance threshold of 10 (“--minuniquesize 10”). OTUs were defined by clustering the dereplicated sequences at a 97% similarity threshold using the “-cluster_otus” command in USEARCH, which concurrently performs de novo chimera removal. The resulting OTU table was constructed by mapping all quality-filtered reads back to the OTU representative sequences using “vsearch--usearch_global” with a 97% identity cutoff. Taxonomic assignment for each OTU was performed using “vsearch--sintax” against the RDP 16S rRNA gene database (v16) for bacteria and the UNITE ITS database (v9.0) for fungi, applying a confidence threshold of 0.8 (“--sintax_cutoff 0.8”). Finally, OTUs classified as mitochondria, chloroplasts (for 16S data), or those unassigned at the kingdom level were removed from the dataset. All downstream statistical analyses in this study are based on this final OTU table.

Rhizosphere metabolite collection and metabolic analysis Rhizosphere samples from cucumber plants were obtained as described above and immediately stored at −80 °C for subsequent metabolite analysis. Rhizosphere metabolites were extracted and analyzed following a previously described method with modifications [ 25 ]. Briefly, each soil sample was split into two equal parts (0.2 g each) in two 2−mL Eppendorf (EP) tubes, and 24 μL of adonitol (1 mg/mL) was added to each tube as an internal standard. One part of the rhizosphere soil was homogenized in 0.5 mL methanol solution ( V methanol : V H2O = 3:1) using a ball mill at 45 Hz for 4 min and then ultrasonically treated 5 times for a period of 5 min each. The soil was then centrifuged at 10,000 g at 4 °C for 15 min, and the supernatant (~ 0.4 mL) was transferred to a fresh EP tube. A second extraction was performed with 0.5 mL ethyl acetate using the same method as above, and the resultant extracts were combined (~ 0.8 mL in total). Then, 0.5 mL of ethyl acetate and 0.5 mL of methanol solution were applied to each soil sample at the end of the extraction procedure. A second portion of the soil sample was extracted by another 0.5 mL ethyl acetate followed by 0.5 mL methanol solution ( V methanol: V H 2 O = 3:1) using the same method, resulting in an additional extract (~ 0.8 mL). After four extractions, each sample yielded 1.6 mL solution. Prior to GC-TOF-MS analysis, the dried metabolites were derivatized as follows: methoximation with methoxyamine hydrochloride in pyridine (20 mg/mL, 80 μL, 30 °C for 90 min), followed by silylation with N,O-bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide (BSTFA) containing 1% trimethylchlorosilane (TMCS) (80 μL, 70 °C for 60 min). The extractions were analyzed by BIOTREE technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using a gas chromatograph (Agilent 7890) coupled with time-of-flight mass spectrometry (GC-TOF-MS) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Raw data processing and analyses were performed as previously reported [ 42 ]. The detailed workflow is as follows: the acquired raw data files were first processed with Chroma TOF 4.3X software (LECO Corporation) for baseline calibration, peak detection, and deconvolution to resolve co-eluting compounds. The resulting peak lists, containing retention times and deconvoluted mass spectra, were then aligned across all samples. Metabolite identification was performed by matching the mass spectra and calibrated retention indices against the LECO-Fiehn Rtx5 database. A metabolite was considered positively identified only if it met the dual criteria of a similarity score > 700 (maximum 1000) and a retention index deviation < 20 units from the database value. The peak area of each identified metabolite was quantified via integration. Subsequently, the quantified peak areas were normalized to the peak area of the internal standard (adonitol) within the same sample to account for technical variations. Finally, the normalized data matrix was cleaned by replacing any missing values with half of the minimum positive value for the corresponding metabolite, yielding the final dataset for statistical analysis. Metabolite identification required concurrent validation through mass spectrum matching and retention index correspondence, with GC-TOF-MS analysis and raw peak analysis performed as reported by Li et al. [ 43 ]and Niu et al. [ 44 ].

Greenhouse experiment: impact of distinctive rhizosphere metabolites on Fusarium wilt in cucumber To investigate the functional roles of distinguished rhizosphere metabolites (DRMs) in cucumber Fusarium wilt, we selected two DRM profiles: Low phosphorus DRMs (LPDRMs: succinic acid, azelaic acid, threonic acid, methionine) and high-phosphorus DRMs (HPDRMs: myo-inositol, dihydroxyacetone, sorbitol, 3-aminoisobutyric acid). Equimolar aqueous solutions containing each metabolite group were prepared at 1 μM, 100 μM, and 1 mM concentrations for dose optimization. Seven experimental treatments were established: LP_HPDRM (low phosphorus substrate + HPDRMs), NP_HPDRM (normal phosphorus substrate + HPDRMs), HP_LPDRM (high phosphorus substrate + LPDRMs), NP_LPDRM (normal phosphorus substrate + LPDRMs), alongside control groups LP_CK (low phosphorus + autoclaved water), NP_CK (normal phosphorus + autoclaved water), and HP_CK (high phosphorus + autoclaved water) (Fig. S7). Cucumber cultivation followed previously described sterilization protocols and growth conditions. Beginning at germination, 10 mL of filter-sterilized metabolite solution (10% w/w) was applied to each cell twice weekly for 30 days, with all trays randomized. Subsequently, each tray was inoculated with 500 mL of FOC spore suspension (5 × 10⁶ spores·mL⁻ 1 ). Disease incidence was recorded after 18 days of pathogen inoculation. Disease progression was evaluated at the optimal metabolite concentration (100 μM) to assess treatment effects.

Isolation of distinctive microbes Distinctive microbes were isolated from cucumber rhizosphere samples cultivated under different phosphorus conditions. Specifically, 1 g of rhizosphere soil collected from the low phosphorus treatment in the aforementioned greenhouse experiment was placed into an autoclaved 15 mL centrifuge tube. Ten milliliters of autoclaved deionized water were added. The soil suspension was vortexed at 180 rpm for 30 min at room temperature. This suspension was then subjected to serial dilution, and 100 μL aliquots of the 10 –5 dilution were spread-plated onto various culture media. For bacterial, the isolation procedure employed the dilution plate-spreading method on Luria–Bertani (LB), Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB), and Nutrient Agar (NA) plates. Individual colonies were selected and purified through repeated streaking and subculturing. Genomic DNA was extracted using a commercial bacterial bacteria DNA isolation Kit (B518255, Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), amplified via PCR with universal primers 27F/1492R [ 45 ]. For fungal isolation, rhizosphere suspensions were dilution-plated on PDA and Oatmeal Agar (OA) media. Distinct colonies were selected and purified through 2–3 successive subcultures. Genomic DNA was extracted using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method, followed by PCR amplification of the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region with primers ITS1/ITS4 [ 46 ]. All PCR products were sequenced by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. Sequence processing and analysis were performed as follows: Raw sequencing chromatograms were assembled into consensus sequences using CodonCode Aligner [ 47 ]. Primer sequences were trimmed from the ends of the assembled contigs. The quality of the consensus sequences was visually inspected, and low-quality base calls at the ends were trimmed. Potential chimeric sequences were checked using the DECIPHER [ 48 ] online tool. The resulting high-quality, full-length 16S rRNA or ITS sequences were used for subsequent analysis. Taxonomic identification was achieved by comparing obtained sequences against the NCBI BLAST database. Purified isolates were phylogenetically characterized through sequence alignment and BLAST similarity analysis using thresholds of ≥ 98% sequence identity and ≥ 95% query coverage, thereby ensuring all recovered strains corresponded to signature genera identified in prior sequencing analytics.

Comprehensive in vitro characterization of selected microbes Following the initial isolation of 288 microbial isolates, a subset of 28 strains (19 bacterial and 9 fungal isolates) was selected for in-depth functional characterization and SynCom construction based on their representation of signature genera identified under low-phosphorus conditions. The taxonomic identity of each of these 28 strains was definitively confirmed by sequencing and analyzing their full-length 16S rRNA (bacteria) or ITS (fungi) gene sequences (Supplementary Table 9).

Growth kinetics assessment : Growth capacity assessment under differential media was conducted using three treatments: (1) control: 1/2 LB (bacteria) or 1/2 PDB (fungi); (2) carbon/nitrogen-deficient mineral salts medium [ 49 ] + LPDRM; (3) carbon/nitrogen-deficient mineral salts medium + HPDRM. For bacterial strains, growth in 12-well microplates (180 rpm, 28 °C) was monitored by measuring OD₆₀₀ at 1-h intervals for 24 h, with the maximum recorded OD₆₀₀ value directly representing growth capacity per treatment. For fungal strains, cultures in aerated test tubes (180 rpm, 28 °C) were assessed by daily measurement of mycelial biomass over 7 days, where the peak biomass accumulation defined growth potential.

Chemotactic response : The chemotaxis assay was performed using a modified method based on Robert et al. [ 50 ]. Four experimental treatments were established: LPDRM (100 μM); HPDRM (100 μM); CK (autoclaved distilled water control); 1/5 LB medium. Each treatment group consisted of four biological replicates processed simultaneously. Metabolite solutions were prepared in autoclaved distilled water, sterilized through 0.22 μm membrane filters, and 1 mL aliquots were loaded into sterile disposable syringes. All bacterial strains were uniformly cultured in aerated LB medium. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 6000 × g for 2 min and resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to an OD 600 = 0.5. Then, 200 μL aliquots of each bacterial suspension were transferred to sterile pipette tips. Syringes containing metabolites were connected to tips containing bacterial suspensions. After 6 h of horizontal incubation at 25 °C, bacterial suspensions and connecting tips were removed. The residual liquid in syringes was collected into sterile 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes, serially diluted, and plated for colony counting.

In vitro assessment of antagonistic capacity of microbial isolates against FOC pathogen Antagonistic activity of strains against FOC was evaluated using a modified protocol based on Bordoloi et al. [ 51 ]. In experimental groups, 1 μL of FOC spore suspension (1 × 10⁶ spores/mL) was inoculated at the center of a potato dextrose agar (PDA) plate while simultaneously 1 μL of a single test strain suspension—either bacterial suspension standardized to OD 600 = 0.5 or fungal spore suspension (1 × 10⁶ spores/mL)—was inoculated at the midpoint between the plate periphery and the central FOC inoculation site. Five replicate plates were established per test strain-FOC pair. Control groups received only 1 μL of FOC spore suspension at the plate center without any test strain (with five replicates). Following 72 h incubation at 28 °C, measurements were taken of: the radial growth diameter of FOC colonies in control plates, the radial growth diameter of FOC colonies in experimental plates, and the colony diameter of the test strain in experimental plates. The inhibition rate was calculated as (Control FOC diameter − Experimental FOC diameter)/Control FOC diameter, and the competitive advantage was determined as Test strain diameter/(Experimental FOC diameter + Test strain diameter).

Assessment of microbes colonization fitness in soil To assess the field colonization capacity of screened microbial strains and the influence of designated metabolite groups on colonization efficacy, three treatments were established: (1) CK group (autoclaved distilled water), (2) LPDRM group, and (3) HPDRM group. All strains were individually cultured—bacteria in LB liquid medium and fungi in PDB medium. To prepare the inoculation complexes, high-concentration stocks of each individual strain were first generated: bacterial stocks at OD 600 = 1.0 and fungal stocks at 1 × 10 4 spores/mL. For each treatment (CK, LPDRM, or HPDRM), a composite inoculum was formulated as follows: 5 mL from each of the 13 bacterial stocks and 1 mL from each of the 6 fungal stocks were pooled into a sterile container. The pooled suspension was centrifuged (6000 × g , 3 min), the supernatant was discarded, and the pelleted cells/spores were resuspended in 10 mL of the corresponding treatment solution (CK: sterile distilled water; LPDRM or HPDRM: a 100 μM aqueous solution of the respective metabolite group). This procedure ensured that in the final 10 mL complex, each bacterial strain was present at an OD 600 of 0.5, each fungal strain at 1 × 10 3 spores/mL, and each metabolite in the LPDRM or HPDRM groups was present at a concentration of 100 µM. Cucumber plants were grown under sterile conditions consistent with the previously described protocol. On day 14 post-planting, the bacterial-fungal-metabolite complexes were inoculated into the rhizosphere. Rhizosphere soil samples were collected at three timepoints (days 3, 5, and 7 post-inoculation) using our established methodology. A total of 54 rhizosphere soil samples (3 treatments × 3 timepoints × 6 biological replicates) were subjected to DNA extraction following identical procedures to those previously detailed. Sequencing of full-length 16S rRNA and ITS amplicons and the assembly of reads were performed on the PacBio platform at Novogene Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). To determine the absolute abundance of individual strains within the samples, total bacterial and fungal communities in all rhizosphere soil samples were subjected to absolute quantification following the methodology established by Siles et al. [ 52 ].

Characterization of fungal-bacterial interactions within SynCom Prior to SynCom assembly, all bacterial consortia members were revived in half-strength LB medium, while fungal strains were activated in half-strength potato dextrose broth (1/2 PDB). Each strain was standardized to uniform cellular density: bacterial suspensions to OD₆₀₀ = 0.5 and fungal suspensions to 1 × 10 3 spores/μL. Equal volumes of these standardized suspensions were then combined to construct the synthetic communities.

Bidirectional effects of fungal-bacterial volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in SynCom : The experiment was conducted using partitioned Petri dishes with a central divider. Fungal spore suspension (1 × 10 5 spores/μL, 1 μL) and bacterial suspension (OD 600 = 0.5, 1 μL) were inoculated into opposite compartments of the bipartite plate. Control plates were prepared by inoculating either fungi or bacteria alone into one compartment. Following 48 h incubation at 28 °C, bacterial biofilm formation was quantified according to Jia et al. [ 53 ], while fungal colony diameters were measured simultaneously. Data normalization was performed using the corresponding single-strain control values for each microbial strain.

Inhibition of FOC by SynCom : The basal inorganic salt liquid medium was employed to collect metabolites from individual SynCom strains. Following cultivation periods (24 h for bacteria, 72 h for fungi), cultures were centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 5 min to collect supernatants containing strain-derived metabolites. Supernatants from all bacterial strains were pooled to form the Bacterial Metabolite mixture (BM), while those from fungal strains constituted the Fungal Metabolite mixture (FM). Bacterial suspensions were cultured in 1/2 LB liquid medium (24 h, 28 °C) and fungal suspensions in 1/2 PDB medium (72 h, 28 °C). Prior to mixing for SynCom preparation, the concentrations of all bacterial and fungal suspensions were standardized to a uniform level according to the aforementioned protocol. Five experimental treatments were established: CK (sterile water added to test soil), BM + fungi (bacterial metabolites + fungal suspension), FM + bacteria (fungal metabolites + bacterial suspension), SynCom (full microbial suspension), and SynCom + LPDRM (full microbial suspension + low phosphorus characteristic metabolites). For each treatment, 50 g of soil was placed in Petri dishes with four biological replicates. Twice weekly, 5 mL of respective treatment solutions were applied. After 2 weeks, 5 mL of FOC spore suspension (10 3 CFU/mL) was introduced to each dish. Seven days post-inoculation, soil samples were serially diluted and plated on K2 [ 54 ] medium for quantification of pathogenic FOC colonies.

Greenhouse experiment: suppression of cucumber Fusarium wilt by SynCom and LPDRM Under the aforementioned experimental conditions and cucumber cultivation protocol, SynCom and LPDRM were introduced simultaneously into high-phosphorus-treated cucumber plants. Disease suppression efficacy was assessed by measuring the incidence of Fusarium wilt. Disease assessment was conducted at 18 days post-inoculation with FOC. A plant was considered diseased when clear vascular browning and wilting symptoms were observed. Disease incidence was calculated for each replicate as (Number of diseased plants/Total number of plants) × 100%.

Phosphate solubilization assay of SynCom-strains Phosphate solubilization was evaluated on NBRIP agar containing calcium phytate or Ca3(PO4)2, according to Hu et al. [ 55 ]. Briefly, strains were cultured in NBRIP broth (28 °C, 170 rpm, 5 days), and supernatants were collected by centrifugation (10,000 × g , 5 min). Samples were diluted, pH-indicated with methyl orange, reacted with molybdenum-antimony reagent, and absorbance at 700 nm was measured.

Statistical analysis All statistical analyses were performed using the R 4.4 software environment [ 41 ], based on the OTU table generated above. Prior to alpha and beta diversity analyses, the OTU table was rarefied to correct for uneven sequencing depth. Samples with fewer than 35,000 or 20,000 (for bacteria or fungi) reads were removed, and a normalized number of 35,000 or 20,000 (for bacteria or fungi) reads was randomly extracted from each remaining sample using the “vegan” package [ 56 ]. Alpha diversity indices (Richness index) were calculated on this rarefied OTU table (was also used). The results were visualized using the “EasyStat” package (v 0.1.0) [ 42 ]. For beta diversity analysis, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on these matrices using the vegan (v2.6-4) package to ordinate samples and visualize major patterns of variation in microbial community composition across treatments. The significance of community structural differences among treatments was tested using Analysis of Similarities (ANOSIM) with 999 permutations in the vegan package. Data were centered and scaled ( Z -score normalized) to ensure equal weighting of all features prior to analysis. Resulting ordinations were visualized through biplots generated with the R package ggplot2 (v3.4.2). The “randomForest” package (v 4.7.1) [ 57 ] was used to develop random forest models. Differences in the relative abundance of OTUs among phosphorus treatments were detected using likelihood ratio tests in the “EdgeR” [ 58 ]package. Low-abundance OTUs were filtered prior to analysis using the filterByExpr function, retaining only those with counts per million (CPM) > 1 in at least 4 samples. Data were normalized using the Trimmed Mean of M -values (TMM) method within “EdgeR” to account for library size differences. A negative binomial generalized linear model (GLM) was then fitted to the filtered and normalized data. The model design specified the phosphorus treatment level (low, normal, high) as the main factor. Model dispersion (variance) was estimated in three steps: a common dispersion across all OTUs, a trended dispersion dependent on OTU abundance, and final gene-wise (OTU-wise) dispersions stabilized by empirical Bayes shrinkage. To test for significant overall differences in OTU abundance across all phosphorus treatments simultaneously, a likelihood ratio test (LRT) was applied to the fitted model. This test compares the full model (including the phosphorus factor) against a reduced null model (excluding it). The resulting p -values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). OTUs with an FDR-adjusted p -value < 0.05 were considered statistically differentially abundant across the phosphorus gradient. For OTUs identified as significant by the global LRT, post hoc pairwise comparisons between specific treatment levels were conducted using “glmLRT” function on relevant model coefficients or contrasts to determine the direction of change. Co-occurrence network analysis was performed using the package “ggClusterNet2” [ 59 ], following the recommended workflow with minor modifications. Pairwise Spearman’s rank correlations were calculated. A robust correlation was retained for network construction if the correlation coefficient | ρ |> 0.6 and the associated p -value was < 0.001 after Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction. To validate the non-random nature and reliability of the constructed network, its topological properties were compared against those of 500 randomized networks generated by random permutation of the original data. The constructed ecological network displayed significantly higher modularity and non-random topological features compared to its randomized counterparts, confirming its structural reliability.

Other statistical analyses not covered by the specific tests above (e.g., comparisons of alpha diversity indices, disease incidence, and plant biomass) were performed using the R package “EasyStat” (v 0.1.0) [ 42 ]. Data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For normally distributed data, differences between two groups were assessed using independent samples t -test, while differences among three or more groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test for post hoc pairwise comparisons. For non-normally distributed data, differences between two groups were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, while differences among three or more groups were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s test with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. Significance was set at p < 0.05 for all tests.

The processed metabolomics dataset was subjected to the following statistical analyses. The raw peak area of metabolites was processed using MetaboAnalyst (v 4.0.0) [ 60 ], with missing values filled using its recommended method. The normalized metabolic dataset was processed with R software. The ComplexHeatmap package (v 2.2.0) [ 61 ] was used to visualize metabolite profile across different treatments. To identify differentially abundant metabolites between the two groups, the glm() was applied. Raw p -values obtained from the GLM were adjusted for FDR using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Metabolites with FDR-adjusted p -values (FDR < 0.05) were considered statistically significant. Metabolite enrichment analysis was performed using the R package “MetaboAnalyst” (v 4.0.0) [ 62 ]. All other plots were created with the “ggplot2” package (v 3.4.2) [ 63 ] in R. Bray-Curtis similarity matrices were calculated using the R package “vegan” (v.2.5–7.5) [ 64 ]. Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) was performed using the “mixOmics” package (Version: 6.18.1) [ 65 ] in R. Metabolomic data were log₂-transformed and Pareto-scaled prior to analysis to minimize technical variance and enhance biological signals. Model performance was evaluated through fivefold cross-validation with 10 repetitions, providing predictive ability ( Q 2 ) and explained variance metrics ( R 2 X and R 2 Y ). The statistical significance of group separation in PLS-DA components was assessed using one-way ANOVA for each component. PLS-DA score plots were generated using the “ggplot2” package (Version: 3.3.5) in R. In this study, loading Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used to identify the variables most contributing to the metabolic differences. The data were first normalized, and PCA was performed to extract principal component scores and loadings, followed by selecting the top 20 metabolites contributing to the first principal component, which were visualized using “ggplot2” to better understand their role in the observed differences across treatments. To identify metabolites that were distinct between phosphorus treatments, a two-step procedure integrating machine learning and differential abundance testing was employed. Differential abundance analysis of metabolites between the experimental groups was conducted using the “limma” package [ 66 ] in R. The analysis was followed by a Benjamini-Hochberg correction to control the FDR. A p -value threshold of 0.05 was used to determine the significance of the differences. The top 20 metabolites exhibiting the most significant changes (based on p -value and fold change) were selected for downstream visualization. To illustrate pairwise group differences for these 20 metabolites, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD post hoc test was performed on their transformed abundance data. Based on the post hoc test results, significance letters ( p < 0.05) were assigned to the mean of each treatment group using the multcompLetters function. Finally, for heatmap generation, the raw intensity values of these 20 target metabolites were log-transformed (log2( x + 1)) solely to generate the color gradient. The heatmap was plotted using the pheatmap package, with its color scale directly mapping the transformed values, ranging from blue (low intensity) to red (high intensity). First, a random forest model was conducted using the “Random Forest” package as previously described in Wen et al. [ 25 ]. We used the importance () function to rank individual metabolites based on their contribution to the accuracy of the models. We performed cross-tabulations in R that evaluated the accuracy of the model. A Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for detection of different metabolites by using the “EasyStat” package (v.0.1.0). The p -values were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure for multiple comparisons, and metabolites with adjusted p -values < 0.05 were selected. Finally, distinctive metabolites in the cucumber rhizosphere under distinct phosphorus conditions were identified by integrating random forest modeling with differential abundance analysis, and these metabolites were subsequently selected for further experimental validation. Microbiome-metabolite co-occurrence network analysis was performed using the “corBionetwork” function from the R package “ggClusterNet2” [ 59 ] following the established workflow. The analysis integrated both bacterial (16S rRNA gene) and fungal (ITS) sequencing data. Pairwise Spearman’s rank correlations were calculated between microbial OTUs (from both bacteria and fungi) and metabolites. A robust correlation was retained for network construction if the absolute correlation coefficient (| ρ |) was > 0.8 and the associated p -value was < 0.05. To validate the non-random nature and structural reliability of the constructed bipartite network, its topological properties were compared against those of 100 randomized networks generated by permutation of the original data. The constructed ecological network exhibited significantly higher modularity and distinct non-random topological features compared to the randomized networks, confirming its structural integrity. Network visualization was generated using the Fruchterman-Reingold layout algorithm.
