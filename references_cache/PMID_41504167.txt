Cross-Kingdom Synthetic Microbiota Suppresses Wheat Fusarium Crown Rot by Remodeling the Rhizosphere Microbiome and Metabolome.

Zhou Q, Gao X, Wu Q, Zeng W, Cao W, Zhou T, Cui X, Chen J, Gao D, Zhao H.

ABSTRACT

Fusarium crown rot (FCR) of wheat lacks sustainable control. We assembled a cross-kingdom synthetic microbial community (SMC) of Trichoderma harzianum T19 and five Bacillus strains and tested it in nonsterile soil challenged with Fusarium pseudograminearum. The SMC treatment significantly suppressed FCR, reducing the disease severity index by approximately 70%. Wheat growth and yield were simultaneously enhanced. In the rhizosphere, SMC improved soil health by elevating soil organic matter and nitrogen levels by over 50%, while mitigating pathogen-induced nutrient imbalances and boosting nutrient cycling. Full-length ITS and 16S profiling revealed that SMC suppressed pathogenic Fusarium in favor of beneficial fungi (Mortierella, Trichoderma, Chaetomium) and plant growth-promoting bacteria (Pseudomonas, Paenibacillus). Co-occurrence network analysis showed that SMC treatment restructured the rhizosphere microbial network with higher connectivity, stability, and a prevalence of positive cooperative interactions under F. pseudograminearum stress. Defense-related metabolites, such as epi-jasmonic acid, allantoin, Nβ-acetyltryptamine, and dihydrodaidzein, accumulated to higher levels with SMC. These findings demonstrate that the cross-kingdom SMC modulates soil nutrients, microbial community structure, and rhizosphere metabolites to synergistically promote wheat growth and enhance resistance to FCR.

Molecular Identification and Strain Deposition

The core microbial strains used in this study were isolated from the rhizosphere soils of healthy wheat plants collected from experimental fields at Xinjiang Normal University (Urumqi, China). Genomic DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit (Tiangen, China), and the bacterial 16S rRNA gene and fungal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region were amplified with universal primers 27F/1492R and ITS1F/LR3, respectively. PCR products were purified and sequenced by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Sequence similarity searches were performed using NCBI BLAST, and phylogenetic assignment was based on ≥ 99% identity to type strains in the GenBank database. The six functional strains were identified as follows: B. subtilis BS-Z15 (CCTCC M 20251892), B. spizizenii PM9 (CCTCC M 20251894), B. rugosus PM16 (CCTCC M 20251895), T. harzianum T19 (CCTCC M 20251896), B. rugosus S32 (CCTCC M 20251897), and B. halotolerans S8 (CCTCC M 20251898). All strains are preserved in the culture collection of the Xinjiang Key Laboratory of Special Species Conservation and Regulatory Biology, School of Life Sciences, Xinjiang Normal University, and have also been deposited in the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center. The pathogenic fungus used in this study was F. pseudograminearum. The working strain (Fp-XJ-07) was isolated from the crown tissues of wheat plants showing typical Fusarium crown rot symptoms collected from infected fields in Xinjiang, China. The isolate was identified as F. pseudograminearum based on morphological characteristics and ITS sequence analysis.

SynCom-Based Control of Fusarium Crown Rot in Wheat

Conidia of T. harzianum T19 were produced in carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) liquid medium at 28 °C for 5 d, then filtered through four layers of sterile gauze. Spore density was determined with a hemocytometer and adjusted to 1 × 106 conidia mL–1 with sterile water. The five core bacterial strains were grown in nutrient broth (NB) at 37 °C for 8 h; each culture was adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1 and resuspended in sterile water. Equal volumes of the five bacterial suspensions and the T19 spore suspension were then combined to constitute the SynCom SMC.

Pot experiments were conducted to evaluate disease control against Fusarium crown rot and growth promotion. Wheat was grown under four treatments: sterile water control (CK), inoculation with F. pseudograminearum (Fp), inoculation with the SynCom (SMC), and coinoculation with Fp and the SynCom (SMC+Fp). Each treatment comprised six independent biological replicates, and each biological replicate included ten technical replicates. Pots were arranged in a randomized complete block design. Each pot contained 1,100 g of air-dried, nonsterile soil. The pot experiment was conducted in the plant tissue culture room at Xinjiang Normal University (25 °C; 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod). Fp was applied at 1 × 106 CFU mL–1, and the SMC was inoculated 1 week later. Forty-5 days after Fp inoculation, Fusarium crown rot severity was assessed for each plant using a 0–9 disease severity index (DSI) following Yang et al. For each treatment, three rhizosphere soil samples were randomly collected for high-throughput amplicon sequencing of the 16S rRNA gene and the ITS region, and for soil metabolomic profiling.

Assembly of the Cross-Kingdom SynCom

In compatibility assays, coculture of T. harzianum T19 with the other Bacillus strains produced no visible antagonism and no inhibition zones (Figure S3A). Pairwise tests among the Bacillus strains likewise showed no antagonistic effects (Figure S3B). Moreover, the two auxiliary strains with strong synergistic antagonistic and growth-promoting potential identified in preliminary screening (S8 and S32) displayed no antagonism in compatibility assays, further indicating a relatively narrow niche breadth and weak competitiveness, and thus suitability as auxiliary members of the SMC. Based on prior screening, we assembled an SMC comprising antagonists (T19 and BS-Z15), growth-promoting strains (PM9 and PM16), and auxiliary strains (S8 and S32) (Figure G). In pots containing nonsterile soil, we established four treatments to evaluate the effects of the SynCom on wheat growth and disease resistance. The treatments were a CK, Fp, SMC and SMC+Fp.

Growth Kinetics and Compatibility of the Cross-Kingdom SynCom

Cell-free supernatants from the five Bacillus strains (BS-Z15, PM9, PM16, S8, S32) did not impair T. harzianum T19. After 72 h, colony morphology of T19 was indistinguishable from the control. Microscopy showed normal hyphal architecture under bright field; CFW staining produced strong chitin signals comparable to the control, while PI staining remained negative, indicating intact membranes and viable hyphae (Figure S4). Consistently, hyphal-extension trajectories under each supernatant nearly overlapped the control across 0–60 h, without growth lag or slope reduction, and all cultures reached the plate rim by the end of the assay (Figure S5). These data demonstrate that the Bacillus supernatants are nonantagonistic to T19. As shown in Figures S6–S7, across BS-Z15, PM9, PM16, S8, and S32, adding 10% (v/v) T. harzianum T19 supernatant or heterologous Bacillus supernatants yielded OD600 trajectories indistinguishable from CK (no consistent lag extension or lower stationary phase; only minor late-time fluctuations). These results support assembling an SMC integrating T. harzianum T19 with Bacillus strains.

Effects of the SMC on Wheat Growth and Resistance to Fusarium Crown Rot

Under nonsterile soil conditions, wheat growth was significantly promoted 45 days after SMC application. As shown in Figure A–C, compared with CK, the SMC increased plant height, root length, and stem diameter by 18.94%, 22.36%, and 76.83%, respectively. Under Fp infection, plant height, root length, and stem diameter decreased by 16.31%, 10.46%, and 33.98% relative to CK. Relative to Fp, the SMC+Fp treatment increased plant height, root length, and stem diameter by 28.63%, 24.53%, and 92.75%, respectively. In healthy plants, SMC significantly enhanced biomass; compared with CK, fresh weight and dry weight rose by 100.60% and 101.44%, respectively. Under Fp infection, biomass declined markedly, with fresh and dry weight reduced by 43.49% and 44.09% versus CK. Compared with Fp alone, SMC+Fp increased fresh and dry weight by 181.21% and 184.64%, indicating that the SMC promotes biomass accumulation and alleviates Fp-induced growth inhibition (Figure D–E). In addition, relative to CK, Fp reduced chlorophyll content by 54.27%, whereas SMC increased it by 26.57%. Relative to Fp, SMC+Fp increased chlorophyll content by 150.87%, indicating that the SMC helped maintain photosynthetic capacity and physiological activity (Figure F).

As shown in Figure G–H, Fp-infected plants were dwarfed, chlorotic, wilted, and showed premature senescence, with smaller spikes and sparse kernels. By contrast, SMC-treated plants were vigorous, with robust growth and well-filled spikes. Although SMC+Fp plants experienced pathogen stress, overall performance approached that of CK, indicating that the SMC effectively antagonized Fp, modulated plant physiology, and maintained growth. DSI analysis showed that Fp infection resulted in a DSI of 86.45% (Figure I). With SMC treatment, the DSI decreased to 25.93%, a 70.01% reduction relative to Fp, demonstrating marked control efficacy. As shown in Figure J, Fp significantly reduced TKW by 7.80%, whereas SMC+Fp increased TKW by 10.39% relative to Fp, indicating that the SMC sustained grain development under pathogen pressure. Moreover, SMC alone increased TKW by 14.20% compared with CK, indicating a yield benefit.

Regulation of Rhizosphere Fungal Community Structure and Function by the SynCom

At the genus level (Figure S9), the Fp treatment was significantly enriched in pathogenic taxa, including Fusarium, Monosporascus, and Psathyrella, indicating that pathogen stress favored these fungi. By contrast, SMC and SMC+Fp increased the relative abundances of growth-promoting and antagonistic genera, including Chaetomium, Trichoderma, and Mortierella.

SynCom-Mediated Modulation of Rhizosphere Bacterial Community Structure and Functions

The SMC+Fp treatment was enriched in taxa associated with phosphate-solubilizing, or nitrogen-fixing traits, such as Paenibacillus, Devosia sp., Paraburkholderia ultramafica, and Rhizobium etli.

Measurement of Wheat Physiological Traits and Rhizosphere Soil Properties and Enzyme Activities

At 45 days after SynCom inoculation, plant physiological traits were measured, including plant height, shoot and root fresh weight, shoot and root dry weight, chlorophyll content, root length, and stem diameter. After heading, thousand kernel weight (TKW) and grains per spike were determined. Each treatment included three replicates, and within each replicate 20 spikes were sampled to measure grains per spike and TKW. Leaf catalase (CAT), superoxide dismutase (SOD), superoxide anion (OFR), peroxidase (POD), and malondialdehyde (MDA) were quantified using commercial assay kits (Suzhou Geruisi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Fresh leaf tissue (0.1 g) was homogenized on ice and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Absorbance of the supernatant was recorded at 510 nm (CAT), 450 nm (SOD), 540 nm (OFR), and 470 nm (POD) using a spectrophotometer (Feyond-A300, China). MDA was determined from the difference between absorbance at 532 and 600 nm.

At 45 days after SynCom inoculation, rhizosphere soil was collected from potted plants. Activities of soil acid phosphatase, urease, sucrase, and catalase were quantified using commercial assay kits (Suzhou Geruisi Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Standard methods were used to determine soil pH, electrical conductivity (EC), soil organic matter (SOM), total nitrogen (TN), total phosphorus (TP), total potassium (TK), available nitrogen (AN), available phosphorus (AP), and available potassium (AK).

Full-Length 16S rRNA/ITS Amplicon Sequencing

Total DNA was extracted from soil using a commercial soil DNA extraction kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. For bacterial community profiling, the full-length 16S rRNA gene (approximately 1,500 bp; V1-V9) was specifically amplified with primers 27F and 1492R. For fungal community profiling, the full-length ITS sequence (approximately 1,300 bp) was specifically amplified with primers ITS1F and LR3. PCR reactions were performed with a high-fidelity DNA polymerase. Thermal cycling conditions were initial denaturation at 98 °C for 5 min, followed by 25-30 cycles of 98 °C for 30 s, 56 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. Amplicons were assessed on 2% agarose gels, and target bands were excised and purified. Amplicons were purified and used to construct third-generation sequencing libraries with the SMRTbell Express Template Prep Kit 3.0 (Pacific Biosciences). Libraries were quantified with the Quant-iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit, quality-checked, and sequenced on the PacBio Revio platform using Circular Consensus Sequencing (CCS). Microbiome data were processed in QIIME 2 (version 2024.5). Raw reads were demultiplexed with the demux plugin, and quality filtering, denoising, read joining, and chimera removal were performed with DADA2 to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) and the corresponding abundance table.

Soil Metabolomics Analysis

Metabolites were profiled on a Vanquish Flex UHPLC system coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo) using methanol-extracted samples that were ultrasonicated, centrifuged, and filtered prior to injection. Chromatographic separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column with gradient elution using 0.1% formic acid in water and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile. The mass spectrometer operated in both positive and negative ion modes with a full-scan range of m/z 70-1000, and data-dependent acquisition was used for MS/MS fragmentation. Metabolomic data were processed in MS-DIAL and annotated against multiple reference libraries, including the PerSonalbio Next-Generation Metabolomics Database, mzCloud, LIPID MAPS, HMDB0000003, MoNA, and NIST_2020_MSMS, as well as AI-predicted MS/MS spectral libraries. Peaks with a detection frequency below 50% in quality control samples were removed, and the resulting metabolite relative abundance matrix was used for statistical analyses.
