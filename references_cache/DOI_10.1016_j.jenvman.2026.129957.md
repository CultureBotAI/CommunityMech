Cupples * Department of Civil and Environmental Engineering, Michigan State University, East Lansing, MI, USA ARTICLE INFO Keywords: Rhodococcus Mycobacterium Propane monooxygenase Trichloroethene cis-dichloroethene 1,1-Dichloroethene ABSTRACT Aerobic cometabolism offers a viable strategy for the remediation of chlorinated solvent plumes at oxic sites where anaerobic approaches are limited. Here, propane-enriched mixed cultures (derived from agricultural soils and an impacted site sediment) which previously degraded 1,4-dioxane, were evaluated for their capacity to also degrade trichloroethene (TCE), cis -1,2-dichloroethene (cDCE), and 1,1-dichloroethene (1,1-DCE) over successive transfers. Sustained biodegradation of TCE and cDCE was observed across multiple enrichments and cultures enriched on one compound generally degraded the other. In contrast, 1,1-DCE biodegradation was restricted to a subset of cultures and removal times increased over transfers. Further, 1,1-DCE removal was absent at elevated concentrations, both trends consistent with inhibitory or toxic effects. Whole genome sequencing analyses revealed pronounced substrate-dependent selection of microbial communities, with cDCE-degrading cultures being dominated by Mycobacterium and Mycolicibacterium , whereas TCE-degrading cultures were dominated by Rhodococcus . Rhodococcus metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) in the TCE degrading cultures classified as R. opacus or R. wratislaviensis. 1,1-DCE degrading cultures were dominated by Pseudonocardia , although the associated MAGs contained a truncated propane monooxygenase alpha subunit, suggesting other enzymes were responsible for 1,1-DCE transformation. Functional gene analysis identified both group 5 ( prmABCD ) and putative group 6 propane monooxygenases (although their expression was not examined). Together, these results demonstrate that substrate-specific pressures govern propanotrophic community structure and function, and highlight distinct roles of key actinobacterial genera in chlorinated ethene cometabolism. These findings support the development of propane-based bioaugmentation strategies for the treatment of mixed chlorinated solvent contamination under aerobic conditions. 1. Introduction The chlorinated solvents, tetrachloroethene (PCE), trichloroethene (TCE) and 1,1,1-trichloroethane (TCA) have been among the most widely used cleaning and degreasing solvents in the United States ( Doherty, 2000 ). Indiscriminate disposal of these chemicals has caused significant soil and water contamination and the remediation of such sites has been difficult and costly ( McCarty, 2010 ). One approach to remediate chlorinated solvent contaminated sites involves bioremediation. PCE was first reported to be biologically reduced to TCE under anaerobic conditions ( Bouwer et al., 1981 ). Similarly, TCE was later also reported to be biologically reduced to form cis -1,2-dichloroethene (cDCE) and vinyl chloride (VC) ( Parsons and Lage, 1985 ; Vogel and McCarty, 1985 ) and TCA can be transformed into 1,1-dichloroethene (1, 1-DCE) ( Vogel and McCarty, 1987 ). In many cases, these chemicals occur with 1,4-dioxane, which was used as a stabilizer in chlorinated solvent formulations ( Adamson et al. 2014 , 2015 ; Anderson et al., 2012 ). Bioremediation efforts for the chlorinated ethenes have involved biostimulation or bioaugmentation with microbial cultures containing Dehalococcoides mccartyi ( Steffan and Vainberg, 2013 ). However, bioremediation with D. mccartyi is unlikely to be used at large, oxic sites because of the cost associated with creating anaerobic conditions. Although anaerobic 1,4-dioxane biodegradation has previously been reported ( Shen et al., 2008 ), this contaminant is typically recalcitrant under anaerobic conditions ( Ramalingam and Cupples, 2020 ). Another option, co-metabolic bioremediation, was first identified as a remediation method forty years ago ( Hopkins and McCarty, 1995 ; Nelson et al., 1988 ; Pfiffner et al., 1997 ; Wilson and Wilson, 1985 ) and has gained recent interest due to the potential to treat large, dilute, mixed-contaminant, aerobic plumes ( Hatzinger, 2022 ). Co-metabolic oxidation involves fortuitous oxidation of chemicals by monooxygenases or dioxygenases ( Alexander, 1994 ; Horvath, 1972 ; Wackett, 1996 ). Such enzymes are induced by growth substrates, such as methane and propane, and because of their lower specificity, can oxidize other compounds beside the growth substrates ( Alexander, 1994 ; Wackett, 1996 ). Oxygenases introduce one or two oxygen atoms from molecular oxygen to the molecule, leading to products which can then be further mineralized ( Karigar and Rao, 2011 ). Although much attention has focused on the ability of methanotrophs to degrade the chlorinated solvents, these microbes are unable to degrade 1,4-dioxane (both in pure and mixed cultures)( Hatzinger et al., 2017 ). In contrast, propanotrophs offer much potential to degrade multiple contaminants, including the chlorinated ethenes and 1,4-dioxane ( Skinner et al., 2024 ). Propanotrophs are microorganisms that can use propane as a sole source of carbon and energy ( Steffan et al., 1997 ; Wackett et al., 1989 ). These microorganisms are of particular interest because they have been associated with the biodegradation of numerous organic contaminants ( Cappelletti et al., 2018 ; Hand et al., 2015 ; Sharp et al. 2007 , 2010 ; Steffan et al., 1997 ; Wackett et al., 1989 ; Wang and Chu, 2017 ). For example, an early study documented TCE biodegradation by five Mycobacterium strains growing on propane ( Wackett et al., 1989 ). Propane induced isolates, M. vaccae JOB5 and Rhodococcus jostii RHA1, degraded 1,4-dioxane ( Hand et al., 2015 ). Propanotrophs, along with propane biosparging, have also been successfully used for in situ site remediation ( Bell et al., 2022 ; Divine et al., 2024 ; Horst et al., 2019 ; Lippincott et al., 2015 ). A key functional enzyme for propane oxidation is propane monooxygenase. This is coded by four subunits: propane monooxygenase large subunit ( prmA ), propane monooxygenase reductase subunit ( prmB ), propane monooxygenase small subunit ( prmC ) and propane monooxygenase coupling protein ( prmD ) ( Kotani et al., 2003 ; Sharp et al., 2007 ). These enzymes have been reported within both the group 5 ( Kotani et al., 2003 ) and group 6 ( Deng et al., 2018a ; Kotani et al., 2006 ; Masuda et al., 2012 ) soluble di-iron monooxygenases (SDIMO) ( Yang et al., 2024 ). Previous research has directly linked both group 5 and 6 propane monooxygenases to contaminant biodegradation ( Chen et al., 2023 ; Deng et al., 2018a ; Lippincott et al., 2015 ; Sharp et al., 2007 ). Group 5 propane monooxygenase were also detected in 1, 4-dioxane degrading mixed cultures in our previous work ( Eshghdoostkhatami et al., 2025 ). Toxicity from 1,1-DCE transformation was also thought to be responsible for a stall in the cometabolic transformation of cDCE, 1,4-dioxane and 1,1,1-TCA in groundwater microcosms ( Rolston et al., 2022 ) and for the limited decline in cDCE, 1,1-DCA and 1, 4-dioxane at a field site ( Danko et al., 2023 ). The propanotrophic enrichment cultures were derived from agricultural soils and impacted site sediments in a previous study focused on 1,4-dioxane biodegradation ( Eshghdoostkhatami et al., 2025 ). Here, the cultures were evaluated for chlorinated ethene biodegradation across successive transfers to assess both transformation potential and stability. To link the observed activity with microbial function, whole genome sequencing was used to reconstruct community composition and recover metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), with a focus on identifying propane monooxygenase operons and associated metabolic features. The specific objectives of the current study were to 1) determine the sustainability of chlorinated ethene biodegradation in propanotrophic mixed communities, 2) determine which propanotrophs survive well when exposed to individual chlorinated ethenes, 3) retrieve MAGs for dominant propanotrophs and obtain molecular data for propane monooxygenase operons and surrounding genes, and 4) compare the occurrence of group 5 versus putative group 6 propane monooxygenases. 2. Microcosm preparation and incubations The initial microcosms for this study, from ( Eshghdoostkhatami et al., 2025 ),consisted of 160 mL serum bottles containing 10 g (wet weight) of soil or sediment and 30 mL of liquid media, with the remaining volume serving as the gas headspace. Bottles were sealed with 20 mm butyl rubber stoppers (Fisher Scientific, product no. 50143854) and aluminum crimp seals (MilliporeSigma, Merck KGaA). All microcosms were incubated at room temperature on an orbital shaker operated at 120 rpm. The liquid medium was prepared using a buffer stock solution and a trace metal stock solution. The buffer stock consisted of K 2 HPO 4 (32.4 g/L), KH 2 PO 4 (10 g/L), and NH 4 Cl (1 g/L), while the trace metal stock contained MgSO 4 ⋅ 7H 2 O (2 g/L), FeSO 4 ⋅ 7H 2 O (0.12 g/L), MnSO 4 ⋅ H 2 O (0.03 g/L), ZnSO 4 ⋅ 7H 2 O (0.03 g/L), and CoCl 2 ⋅ 6H 2 O (0.01 g/L), as previously described ( Faghihinezhad et al., 2025 ). Growth media was prepared by adding 100 mL of each stock solution to 800 mL of deionized water. The solution pH was then adjusted to 7.4 and placed in the autoclave for sterilization. After sterilization, the media were sonicated for 15 – 20 min in an ultrasonic bath to dissolve any precipitated salts. For each inocula type (Soil T2, SoilT3, SoilT4 and Site1) three to four replicate microcosms were prepared along with corresponding abiotic controls. The chlorinated ethenes, TCE (4 to 5 mg/L), cDCE (4 to 5 mg/ L) and 1,1-DCE (0.5 to 1 mg/L) were added to the active microcosms and abiotic controls. To protect against nutrient depletion and the accumulation of metabolic byproducts, serial transfers were performed for each microcosm, with up to five successive transfers being conducted over the incubation period (if biodegradation was observed). Except in three cases (at the start of the experiment) the majority of serial transfers included 10 mL culture into 20 mL fresh media. The dilution ratio was changed (from 1:9 to 1:2) to favor the sustained biodegradation of each chemical and protect against losing any less abundant, yet active microorganisms. Following the degradation of cDCE and 1,1-DCE over multiple transfers, the cultures were then tested for their ability to degrade TCE. An additional set of experiments involved testing the cDCE and TCE degrading cultures for 1,1-DCE degradation (all approx. 4- 5 mg/L). In another set of experiments, after the degradation of TCE over multiple transfers, the cultures were tested for their ability to degrade cDCE. As 1,1-DCE was only degraded by a subset of the cultures (at low concentrations), only two sets of microcosms and controls were then tested for TCE biodegradation potential. 2.3. Genomic sequencing and analysis Twenty DNA extracts were submitted for library generation and Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS) to the RTSF Genomics Core at MSU. This included eight DNA extracts that were amended repeatedly with TCE (replicates for Site 1, Soils T2, T3 and T4), eight that were amended repeatedly with cDCE (replicates for Site 1, Soils T2, T3 and T4), and four that were repeatedly amended with 1,1-DCE (replicates for Soils T2 and T3). DNA was extracted using the DNeasy PowerLyzer PowerSoil Kit (Mo Bio, USA) according to the manual protocol, following the last amendment of TCE, cDCE, and 1,1-DCE. The concentration of DNA in each extract was quantified using the Quant-iT ™ dsDNA High- Sensitivity Assay Kit. Libraries were prepared using the Quantabio sparQ DNA Library Preparation Kit with IDT xGen Unique Dual Index adapters following manufacturer's recommendations. Completed libraries were QC'd and quantified using a combination of Biotium AccuGreen High Sensitivity dsDNA and Agilent 4200 TapeStation HS DNA1000 assays. All libraries were normalized to a constant molar concentration prior to pooling. Libraries were pooled in equimolar amounts and the pool quantified using the Invitrogen Collibri Quantification qPCR kit. The pool was sequenced using an Element AVITI Cloudbreak Freestyle High Output 300 cycle kit. Sequencing was performed in a 2x150bp paired end format. Base calling was done by AVITIOS v3.4.1 and the output was demultiplexed and converted to FastQ format using Element Biosciences bases2fastq v2.2.0. The shotgun sequencing files were uploaded using Globus to KBase ( Arkin et al., 2018 ). The data sets were subject to filtering and quality control using FastQC (version 0.12.1) ( Andrews, 2010 ) and Trimmomatic (version 0.36) (sliding window size equal to 4, sliding window minimum quality equal to 15) ( Bolger et al., 2014 ). The reads were subject to classification using Classify Taxonomy of Metagenomic Reads with Kaiju (version 1.9.0.) ( Menzel et al., 2016 ). Following this, Megahit was used to assemble the sequences (version 1.2.9) ( Li et al., 2015 ) with a minimum contig length of 2000 bp. The, the assemblies were annotated with RASTtk (version 1.073) ( Aziz et al., 2008 ) and Prokka (version 1.14.16) ( Seemann, 2014 ) and the annotated assemblies were downloaded for collection of the targeted sequence subunits and gene positions on the appropriate contigs. A subset of the nucleic acid sequences ( prmA ) were subject to blastn ( Altschul et al., 1990 ) on the NCBI website for the creation of phylogenetic trees (as described below). Also, after quality control, the assembled genomes were classified using Classify Taxonomy of Metagenomic Reads with Kaiju (version 1.9.0) ( Menzel et al., 2016 ). Additionally, the contigs (from Megahit) were binned using CONCOCT (version 1.1) ( Alneberg et al., 2014 ), MaxBin2 (version 2.2.4) ( Wu et al., 2016 ) and MetaBAT2 (version 1.7) ( Kang et al., 2015 ). TCE followed by cDCE biodegradation trends Propanotrophic enrichment cultures, derived from soils T2, T3, and T4 as well as from sediment from an impacted site (Site 1), were evaluated for TCE biodegradation in the presence of propane ( Figs. 1 – 4 ). As propane was typically rapidly depleted, it was amended multiple times. Discussion This study investigated the capacity of propane-enriched microbial communities to cometabolically transform three chlorinated ethenes (TCE, cDCE, 1,1-DCE) under aerobic conditions. The enrichment cultures demonstrated consistent biodegradation of TCE and cDCE over multiple transfers. TCE degrading cultures were capable of cDCE degradation and vice versa. In contrast, only a subset of the cultures degraded 1,1-DCE. It is important to note the chlorinated ethene degradation pathways were not investigated in this study. Future research should examine the transformation products generated to ensure more toxic products are not produced. Interestingly, metagenomic analysis indicated the cDCE degrading cultures were dominated by Mycobacterium and Mycolicibacterium , whereas the majority of the TCE degrading cultures were dominated by Rhodococcus . The occurrence of group 5 and putative group 6 propane monooxygenase operons in the cultures suggests their importance in mixed contaminant biodegradation (however their expression was not examined). The current study also provided novel information concerning the flanking genes of propane monooxygenase operons from MAGs classifying as R . wratislaviensis, R . opacus, Mycobacterium, Methylibium and P . broussonetiae . This project builds on our previous work documenting the occurrence of propanotrophic Mycobacterium, R . opacus, R . wratislaviensis MAGs in mixed cultures capable of biodegradation of 1,4-dioxane at both high and low concentrations ( Eshghdoostkhatami et al., 2025 ). The importance of propanotrophs for the biodegradation of environmental contaminants has previously been reported for a range of chemicals. The trend discovered in the current work suggests that bioaugmentation with cultures containing Rhodococcus would be more appropriate at sites dominated by TCE. In contrast, bioaugmentation with cultures containing Mycobacterium would be more appropriate at sites with significant cDCE contamination. At sites with mixed contamination (as is often the case), either would likely be effective, as both Mycobacterium and Rhodococcus enriched cultures degraded both chemicals. The ability to degrade multiple contaminants has also been observed by others. Specifically, ethane-utilizing Mycobacterium spp. strains TA5 and TA27 degraded a range of chlorinated chemicals, including TCE and cDCE ( Hashimoto et al., 2000 ). Similarly, Rhodococcus strains (isolate Rhodococcus Sm-1 and Rhodococcus rhodochrous ATCC 21197) degraded a number of chlorinated chemicals, including TCE and cDCE ( Malachowsky et al., 1994 ). An interesting question concerns why did Rhodococcus and Mycobacterium/Mycolicibacterium dominate in TCE and cDCE degrading cultures, respectively. A speculative hypothesis involves the presence of a catalase gene ( katA ) close to the propane monooxygenase operon in all five Rhodococcus MAGs (this gene was absent in all fifteen Mycobacterium MAGs). Catalases represent oxidative stress-scavenging systems that detoxify reactive oxygen species (such as hydroxyl radicals) in bacteria growing under aerobic conditions ( Alzaidi et al., 2025 ). If TCE biodegradation stimulated hydroxyl radical formation, as has been noted for exposure to antibiotics ( Kohanski et al., 2007 ), Rhodococcus would be better prepared to limit the toxic effects compared to Mycobacterium/Mycolicibacterium . Conversely, Mycobacterium may have dominated cDCE amended propanotrophic cultures due to their impressive diversity, as illustrated by the detection of multiple Mycobacterium MAGs in many samples and multiple trends (eight) for the propane monooxygenase flanking genes. In a methanotrophic mixed culture, cell inactivation from 1,1-DCE transformation was greater than for any of the other chloroethenes tested (tDCE, cDCE, TCE, VC) ( Dolan and McCarty, 1995 ). In studies with the isolate Comamonas testosteroni RF2 (using phenol and sodium lactate as the primary substrates), only a low concentration of 1,1-DCE (0.25 mg/L) was degraded completely ( Zalesak et al., 2017 ). Partial removal occurred at 0.37 mg/L 1,1-DCE and negligible removal was observed at 1.77 mg/L 1,1-DCE ( Zalesak et al., 2017 ). In a butane grown mixed culture, 1,1-DCE transformation caused greater cell inactivation than the transformation of other chlorinated ethenes ( Kim et al., 2000 ). Similarly, microcosm experiments and modeling studies with a butane grown culture (primarily composed of a Rhodococcus sp.) showed high 1,1-DCE transformation product toxicity ( Semprini et al., 2007 ). Further, the authors indicated the loss of performance in the bioaugmentation field test may also have been associated with 1,1-DCE transformation toxicity ( Semprini et al., 2007 ). The cometabolic transformation of a mixture of 1,1,1-TCA and 1, 4-dioxane was also negatively impacted when 1,1-DCE (100   250 μ g/L) was added to laboratory columns filled with beads containing Rhodococcus rhodochrous 21,198 ( Azizian and Semprini, 2022 ). Toxicity from the transformation of 1,1-DCE was also thought to be responsible for a stall in the cometabolic transformation of cDCE, 1, 4-dioxane and 1,1,1-TCA in groundwater microcosm studies ( Rolston et al., 2022 ). 1,1-DCE transformation toxicity was also associated with the limited decline in cDCE, 1,1-DCA and 1,4-dioxane at a field site, undergoing methane and isobutane injections, as well as bioaugmentation (isobutane oxidizer Rhodococcus aetherivorans ENV493 and mixed methanotrophic consortium ENV494M) ( Danko et al., 2023 ). In contrast, Azoarcus sp. DD4 sustained the concurrent co-oxidation of 1, 4-dioxane and 1,1-DCE using propane as the primary substrate ( Deng et al., 2018b ). Further, two psychrophilic consortia degraded 1, 4-dioxane, 1,1-DCE and numerous other chlorinated aliphatic hydrocarbons when amended with propane ( Antunes and Li, 2024 ). The toxicity associated with 1,1-DCE has important implications for the remediation of mixed contaminant sites. Specifically, it may be necessary to have multiple rounds of bioaugmentation during the remediation project to ensure acceptable levels of active propanotrophs. Following the removal of 1,1-DCE, repeated rounds of bioaugmentation may no longer be needed. Also, it may be more effective to bioaugment with different cultures, first with efficient 1,1-DCE degraders, followed by those more capable of degrading the remaining contaminants (e.g. Rhodococcus for TCE). In sum, any bioaugmentation project at a 1,1-DCE contaminated site would benefit from additional information on which propanotrophs are more resistant to the toxic effects of 1,1-DCE. Conclusions In summary, a key finding in the current study is the importance of genera Mycobacterium/Mycolicibacterium and Rhodococcus for cDCE and TCE biodegradation, respectively. Such information could be used to tailor bioaugmentation efforts to individual sites, dependent on the dominant contaminants. Further, the data suggest a more limited number of propanotrophic phylotypes are capable of 1,1-DCE biodegradation. More information is needed on which propanotrophs are more resistant to the toxic effects of 1,1-DCE degradation. The molecular data indicated propane monooxygenases and flanking genes were more consistent between Rhodococcus MAGs compared to Mycobacterium MAGs. The mixed cultures contained both group 5 and putative group 6 propane monooxygenases and, thus, both may be involved in propane and chlorinated ethene degradation. Therefore, qPCR on contaminated groundwater and sediment should target both groups of genes. This work is the first to provide in depth metagenomic information on both gene clusters and flanking genes, contributing to a better understanding of the functional basis for propane oxidation. The mixed cultures developed here have potential for use in bioaugmentation efforts at contaminated sites due to their ability to degrade multiple chlorinated ethenes along with 1,4-dioxane.
