Abstract Abstract Bacterial wilt caused by Ralstonia solanacearum is one of the most severe plant diseases all over the world. Currently, many scientists are using SynCom (synthetic microbial community) to control this disease. However, designing of highly efficient SynCom remains challenging. In this study, we isolated 372 bacteria with different morphologies from the rhizosphere soil of healthy tobacco plants in a diseased field. Based on the antagonistic activity, compatibility, and presence of cheA gene, a marker gene of chemotaxis, we constructed a chemotactic SynCom comprising two Pseudomonas strains and one Bacillus strain. In vitro experiments revealed that vitamin C, propionic acid, and esculetin significantly promoted the growth and antagonistic ability of chemotactic SynCom strains. Next, we optimized the proportion of organic fertilizer to support the growth of the SynCom strains. The application of the bioorganic fertilizer containing chemotactic SynCom can protect diseased tobacco field from R. solanacearum invasion and enhance yields. Finally, rhizosphere bacterial community analysis showed that phyla Bacillota and Chloroflexiota were significantly enriched. This study highlights the potential of chemotactic SynCom for effective biocontrol, offering a new approach for managing bacterial wilt and enhancing crop yields. Graphical Abstract

Introduction Bacterial wilt, caused by the soil-borne pathogen Ralstonia solanacearum, is a major threat to economic crops worldwide, including tomatoes, potatoes and tobacco, leading to serious yield reductions (Hayward 1991 ; Vailleau and Genin 2023 ). In the main tobacco-growing regions in China, yield losses caused by bacterial wilt range from 50 to 60% and almost up to 100% during supreme outbreaks (Jiang et al. 2017 ). Controlling this disease is challenging because of robust survival of R. solanacearum in the soil and its resistance to many chemical and biotic treatments (Álvarez et al. 2010 ; Liu et al. 2024 ). Therefore, there is an urgent need to establish an eco-friendly technical system for reducing soil pathogens and crop protection.
 In recent years, many scientists have explored the use of bacteria from healthy plants as biocontrol agents to suppress plant diseases, including the Pseudomonas and Bacillus genera (Remus-Emsermann et al. 2016 ; Liu et al. 2024 ). The application of SynCom is broad, persistent, and durable in both greenhouse and field systems (Trivedi et al. 2020 ; Liang et al. 2022 ; Pradhan et al. 2022 ). However, the effectiveness of these beneficial microbes in disease suppression is generally limited by various factors, such as rhizosphere microbial interactions, host plant interactions, resource utilization, and competition (Müller et al. 2016 ; Niu et al. 2017 ; Kehe et al. 2021 ). The primary reason is that the rhizosphere is a complex environment comprising of soil properties, host plants, and a diverse array of environmental microorganisms. In the rhizosphere, plants secrete root exudates composed of various substances that form gradient compounds (Badri and Vivanco 2009 ), organic acids, sugars, and vitamins for instance, which can recruit more beneficial microbes (Bais et al. 2006 ; Yang et al. 2023a ). These compounds can be used as prebiotics to further improve SynCom establishment and biocontrol efficiency by selectively stimulating the growth or activity of beneficial microorganisms (Vassileva et al. 2020 ). On the other hand, plant-associated microorganisms utilize chemotaxis to detect and respond to specific chemical signals released by plant roots, allowing them to navigate toward favorable niches in the rhizosphere (Compton et al. 2018 ; Feng et al. 2018 ). Upon reaching the root surface, these microbes adhere to the root epidermis, establish a stable association, and initiate colonization. CheA is a central histidine kinase in the bacterial chemotaxis system, responsible for signal transduction from chemoreceptors to the flagellar motor (Bourret and Stock 2002 ). Its presence typically indicates an intact chemotaxis system, while its absence suggests the loss of such function (Buchan et al. 2010 ; Wuichet and ZhuLin 2010 ). For example, the chemotactic response of Pseudomonas fluorescens to root exudates helps it colonize in the tomato rhizosphere (de Weert et al. 2002 ). Despite facilitating colonization, chemotaxis contributes to biofilm formation, nitrogen fixation, and plant resistance stimulation (Armitage et al. 1988 ; Corral-Lugo et al. 2016 ; Matilla and Krell 2024 ). As a result, more researches have focused on designing a synthetic community with chemotaxis to enhance the recruitment of beneficial bacteria to the rhizosphere (Scharf et al. 2016 ). In this study, we screened and constructed a simplified SynCom based on their antagonistic activity against R. solanacearum and chemotactic capabilities. Furthermore, we evaluated the enhancement of the antibacterial effects of the root-exudated metabolites. A SynCom-based bioorganic fertilizer was developed and evaluated in field trials for its ability to control bacterial wilt and promote tobacco growth. The results demonstrated that this bioorganic fertilizer not only significantly reduced disease incidence but also increased tobacco yield. Additionally, it altered the composition of rhizosphere microbiome and enriched functional pathways related to biosynthesis and metabolism. These findings provide a novel strategy for the biocontrol of bacterial wilt and highlight the potential of chemotaxis-based SynCom engineering for sustainable agriculture. Materials and methods Bacterial growth condition The plant pathogen R. solanacearum (GenBank: KC888020 ), originally isolated from Longgang Town, Kaiyang County, Guiyang City, Guizhou Province (107.0969 E, 26.8819 N), was used as the model pathogen in this study. The pathogen R. solanacearum grew in NB medium (nutrient broth, glucose 10 g/L, beef extract powder 3 g/L, peptone 5 g/L, yeast extract 0.5 g/L, with agar powder 16 g/L for solid medium, adjusted pH 7.0 ~ 7.2). The R. solanacearum cultures were washed twice with sterile 0.9% NaCl, and the OD600 was adjusted to 0.6 (equivalent to approximately 108 colony-forming units per milliliter, CFU/mL) before all experiments. Isolated strains were grown in TSB medium (tryptic soy broth, tryptone 15 g/L, soy peptone 5 g/L and NaCl 5 g/L, and agar powder 16 g/L for solid medium). Extraction of tobacco root exudate The tobacco root exudate extraction method was modified from Liu et al. ( 2015 ). Tobacco cultivar Yunyan87 (Nicotiana tabacum L.) was used as the model plant. Tobacco seeds were soaked in sterilized deionized water three times to prepare for germination. Seedlings were grown using the floating seedling method for 60 days. Healthy tobacco seedlings were selected, and the root-attached substrate was gently rinsed off with sterile ultrapure water, followed by three additional rinses. The seedlings were then fixed in a planting box with sterile sponges, with a light/darkness 12/12 h, and a temperature of 25 °C. During the cultivation phase, Hoagland nutrient solution and micronutrient solution (Maurer et al. 2021 ) were continuously added to the water to support the normal growth of the seedlings. An electromagnetic pump (RESUN, Shenzhen, China) was used to oxygenate the nutrient solution. Ten tobacco plants with same size and well-developed root system were selected, the roots were washed by sterilized water, and were put into a 250-mL sterilized water beaker wrapped in black cloth. The beaker then was placed in a climate chamber (light/darkness: 12 h/12 h, 25 °C) for 72 h. The root exudate solution was filtered through a microporous filter membrane made of organic polyvinylidene fluoride to remove impurities, immediately lyophilized, concentrated, and stored at − 20 °C for metabolites detection. High-throughput isolation of bacteria from rhizosphere soil The high-throughput isolation method of bacteria from soil was modified by Zhang et al. ( 2021 ). For greenhouse experiments, soil was collected from the Fuquan Town, Guizhou Province, China (107.5132°E, 26.7020°N), which has a long history of tobacco cultivation (pH: 4.4, organic matter: 33.51 g/kg, total nitrogen: 1.85 g/kg, total phosphorus: 1.70 g/kg, total potassium: 8.03 g/kg). Tobacco plants were grown in pots under controlled greenhouse conditions (22 °C, > 85% relative humidity, 12 h light/12 h dark photoperiod). Six-week-old healthy tobacco plants were carefully uprooted, and loosely attached soil was gently shaken off. The remaining soil tightly adhering to the roots was collected as rhizosphere soil, and 1 g of rhizosphere soil was suspended in 9 mL of PBS buffer (phosphate-buffered saline). The homogenous solutions were transferred into 50-mL Falcon tubes and added to 25 mL MgCl2 solution for 15 min at room temperature. A 0.5 mL homogenous solution was added into 250 mL of root exudate extracted from the previous method, transferred into a 96-well plate, and incubated at 30 °C and 100 rpm for 48 h. The turbid cultures were stroken onto 50% TSB medium with agar to isolate strains. Each sample solution was repeated for three times. Strains with different colony morphologies were selected for subsequent experiment. Assembly of chemotactic SynCom with antagonism against R. solanacearum To generate a SynCom using isolated strains, we assessed the antagonistic activity of these strains against R. solanacearum using a spraying plate confrontation method, as described by Li et al. ( 2017 ). Sterilized water was used as control. The diameters of inhibition zones were measured. Then, the compatibility between strains was checked using the cross-streak method. In order to determine whether the antagonistic bacteria possess chemotaxis, we used the histidine kinase gene cheA as a molecular marker. According to Buchan’s method (Buchan et al. 2010 ), a pair of primers, P4P5-F (5′-GGIMGIGGIGTIGGIATGGAYGTIGT-3′) and P4P5-R (5′-CCRTCICCIARIATIGTIGCICC-3′), was used for PCR. Based on a heuristic algorithm (Karkaria et al. 2021 ), we designed a SynCom with three strains possessing chemotaxis and antagonism against R. solanacearum. Briefly, this approach involved ranking all candidate strains based on their antagonistic activity against R. solanacearum. Strains with the highest antagonistic potential were prioritized for inclusion in the SynCom. If incompatibility was detected among selected strains, substitutions were made iteratively until a compatible combination was achieved. Identification of targeted strains The 16S rDNA of three strains described above was amplified by colony PCR (polymerase chain reaction) using general bacterial primers for 16S rDNA (forward primer 27F: 5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG −3′, reverse primer 1492R: 5′-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′). PCR products were sent to Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) for Sanger sequencing. The sequences obtained were aligned against the NCBI 16S rRNA database by BLAST. The top-matched reference strains, along with the sequenced strains, were used to construct phylogenetic trees via iTOL ( https://itol.embl.de/ ). Resource utilization patterns of the chemotactic bacterial isolates and R. solanacearum To assess resource utilization of chemotactic bacterial isolates, 60 distinct resources commonly found in tomato root exudates, including amino acids, organic acids, and sugars, were purchased individually and tested (Supplemental Table S1 ). Chemotactic bacterial strains were first grown overnight in TSB medium at 30 °C with shaking at 150 rpm. Cells were collected, washed three times with 0.85% (w/v) NaCl, and resuspended to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.5. For each treatment, 10 μL of the bacterial suspension was added to a 96-well microtiter plate containing 150 μL of 1/10 TSB medium supplemented with 10 mM of a single resource. Plates were incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 170 rpm for 48 h, and bacterial growth was measured using a SpectraMax M5 spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA). For R. solanacearum, it was grown overnight in NB medium at 30 °C with shaking at 150 rpm. Cells were collected, washed three times with 0.85% (w/v) NaCl, and resuspended to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.5. For each treatment, 10 μL of the R. solanacearum suspension was added to a 96-well microtiter plate containing 180 μL of 1/10 NB medium supplemented with 10 mM of a specific resource. Plates were incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 170 rpm for 24 h, and bacterial growth was measured using a SpectraMax M5 spectrophotometer. Effects of prebiotic-treated bacterial fermentation broth on the growth of R. solanacearum Chemotactic bacterial cultures were grown to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.5 and inoculated into TSB medium containing 20 μL of a 1 mM solution of the selected resource in a 96-well microtiter plate. Mock treatment contained sterile water instead of the resource. Plates were incubated at 30 °C with shaking at 170 rpm for 48 h. Following incubation, cultures were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 15 min. Residual bacterial cells were removed by centrifugation followed by filtration through a 0.22-μm membrane, and the supernatant was collected. Subsequently, 2 μL of a R. solanacearum suspension (OD₆₀₀ = 0.5) was added to NB medium supplemented with filter-sterilized supernatants. Cultures were incubated at 30 °C, 170 rpm for 48 h, and growth of R. solanacearum was assessed by measuring OD₆₀₀. Creation of bacterial fertilizer for chemotactic SynCom The bacterial strains were activated in TSB medium with agar at 30 °C for 12 h. We used an inoculation loop, transferred a single colony into 10-mL TSB culture medium, and placed it in a shaker at 30 °C and 170 rpm for 16 h. The fermentation broth was centrifuged at 5000 g for 10 min to collect bacterial cells. We formulated five composite organic fertilizer groups using different mass ratios of four commonly used organic materials in tobacco cultivation, including vermicompost, distiller’s grains, cow dung, and oil cake, named S1 to S5 formulations (Table  1 ). Each strain used in the chemotactic SynCom was inoculated into the composite nutrient at a 5% (v/w) ratio, along with the corresponding prebiotics screened in the previous experiment. The moisture content was adjusted to 40%, and fermentation was carried out at 23 °C. We set up three replicates and prepared a bioorganic fertilizer using a two-stage solid-state fermentation method. During fermentation, the mixture was turned every 12 h, and a moisture content was maintained at 40% to ensure aeration and looseness of the compost. The fermentation period was approximately 7–10 days.

Soil microbial DNA extraction and 16S rRNA genes amplicon sequencing Rhizosphere soil samples were collected from three healthy plants in the field treated with chemotactic SynCom, whereas three samples from a diseased field served as control. Genomic DNA was extracted using the PowerSoil® DNA Isolation Kit (MoBio, Carlsbad, CA, USA), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was quantified using a Qubit 3.0 (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). A two-step dual indexing strategy was used for Illumina MiSeq (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Bacterial community composition was determined by amplification of the V4-V5 region of the 16S rRNA gene with primers 563 F (forward primer: 5′-AYTGGGYDTAAAGVG-3′) and 802R (reverse primer: 5′-TACNVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′). The PCR products were purified using an AxyPrep PCR Clean-up Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA) before performing agarose gel electrophoresis. The concentrations of the purified amplicon products were determined using QuantiFluor-ST (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) and sequenced on an Illumina Hiseq 2500 platform at Shanghai Biozeron Biological Technology (Shanghai, China). Plant treatments in the field We selected a wilt-diseased field in Fuquan town, Guizhou Province as the experimental field. The treatment and control groups were set in the same field plot. A mixture of 100-g bacterial fertilizer and corresponding prebiotics (mass ratio of 99:1) was applied to a hole 15 cm deep and located 10 cm from the base of the tobacco plant. The control treatment followed conventional fertilization practices, including the application of 100 g of cow manure per plant. Disease index was calculated using the formula described by Liu et al. ( 2013 ). Briefly, these plants were categorized into discrete severity levels based on visible symptoms: level 0 (no symptoms), level 1 (mild wilting, < 25% affected), level 2 (moderate wilting, 25–50% affected), level 3 (severe wilting, 51–75% affected), and level 4 (very severe wilting or plant death, > 75% affected). Each level was assigned a corresponding numerical value (0 to 4), and the disease index was calculated using the following formula: D i s e a s e i n d e x = 100 × ∑ i = 0 n ( n i × v i ) N × v max where n i number of plants in severity level, v i disease severity score assigned to level, N total number of surveyed plants, v max the maximum value of the disease severity scale Agronomic traits, including leaf number, height of tobacco plants, stem perimeter, and leaf area index, were recorded before harvest. Quantitative PCR analysis Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using primers for detection. The fliC gene encoding the flagella subunit (forward primer: 5′-GAACGCCAACGGTGCGAACT-3′; reverse primer: 5′-GGCGGCCTTCAGGGAGGTC-3′), was the specific gene for R. solanacearum quantification (Schonfeld et al. 2003 ). PCR was performed using the SYBR Premix Ex Taq™ kit (Takara, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan). The PCR system (20 µL) consisted of 10 µL SYBR Premix Ex Taq, 0.4 µL ROX Reference Dye, 0.4 µL each of primer (5 µmol/L), 2 µL DNA template, and 6.8 µL ddH2O. The PCR conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, for 30 cycles. Data analysis All data analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1. Microbiome community diversity was calculated as ASV (amplicon sequence variant). LorMe package ( https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/LorMe ) was used for microbial analyses. In detail, the alpha diversity was calculated via the “vegan” package (Dixon 2003 ). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was conducted via the package “ape” (Paradis et al. 2004 ) based on the Bray‒Curtis distance matrix. Functional prediction of the microbial communities was performed using Tax4Fun (Aßhauer et al. 2015 ). The ComplexHeatmap package was utilized to depict the heatmap of bacterial resource utilization ability (Gu et al. 2016 ). Results The Chemotactic SynCom composed of Y364, Y832, and Y878 can best inhibit R. solanacearum Bacterial isolates were obtained from healthy tobacco plants growing in a diseased field in Longgang Town, Kaiyang County, Guiyang City, Guizhou Province, China (107.09694° E, 26.88194° N). A total of 372 morphologically distinct bacterial strains were isolated from the rhizosphere soil of these plants. Using co-culture plate assays, 109 strains were identified as having strong and stable antagonistic activity against the pathogen. Among these, 25 strains produced inhibition zones larger than 20 mm (Fig.  1 ). The three most effective strains, Y364, Y362, and Y366, formed inhibition zones of 27.0 mm, 26.1 mm, and 25.6 mm, respectively. Fig. 1 Properties of isolated strains in the rhizosphere soil of healthy plants. The left panel shows the antagonism against R. solanacearum. The right panel shows the available of cheA gene of isolated strains and the compatibility between each strain. Red bold font represents the strains we used to construct SynCom To evaluate the chemotactic potential of the strains, PCR amplification of cheA, a central regulator of bacterial chemotaxis, was performed (Supplemental Fig. S1 ). Results indicated that 13 of the 25 strains harbored a cheA gene, including strain Y364, which exhibited the largest inhibition zone (Fig.  1 ). Next, we assessed the compatibility of these strains by co-culture in growth medium. Most strains were compatible with each other (Fig.  1 ). Based on their strong antagonistic activity and chemotactic capabilities, we selected strains Y364, Y832, and Y878 to construct a chemotactic SynCom targeting R. solanacearum. We identified these three bacterial strains using 16S rDNA sequencing. The phylogenetic tree results showed that Y364, Y832, and Y878 belonged to Pseudomonas nicosulfuronedens, Pseudomonas citronellolis, and Bacillus altitudinis, respectively (Supplemental Fig. S2 ). Vitamin C, propionic acid and esculetin help the SynCom limit growth of R. solanacearum To maximize the effectiveness of the SynCom, we screened the prebiotic compound library (Supplemental Table S1 ) to identify resources that promote the growth of SynCom strains but not that of R. solanacearum. The results showed most of the tested compounds did not promote the growth of R. solanacearum (Supplemental Fig. S3 ). Half of tested compounds significantly promoted the growth of Y364, 19 compounds supported the growth of Y832, and only two compounds enhanced the growth of Y878 (Supplemental Fig. S3 ). To further assess whether these compounds could enhance the antagonistic ability of Y364, Y832, and Y878 against R. solanacearum, we evaluated the inhibitory effects of their fermentation broths supplemented with the respective prebiotics. Compared with the mock treatment, the fermentation broth of Y364 supplemented with exogenous vitamin C showed enhanced inhibition of R. solanacearum. For Y832, four compounds—formic acid, propionic acid, esculetin, and octanoic acid—significantly increased its inhibitory ability. However, for Y878, none of the tested compounds showed a significant difference compared with the mock treatment (Fig.  2 ). Considering the cost of compounds, vitamin C, propionic acid, and esculetin were chosen for subsequent experiments. Asterisks indicate significant differences (p < 0.05) Creation of chemotactic SynCom fertilizer Considering different composite nutrient materials exhibit varying fermentation abilities for the same strain, we formulated five composite organic fertilizer groups using different mass ratios of four commonly used organic materials in tobacco cultivation, including vermicompost, distiller’s grains, cow dung, and oil cake, named S1 to S5 formulations. Considering these three strains were compatible, we tested the impact of each formulation on fermentation ability of each strain. For strain Y364, the highest propagation occurred in the S1 formulation, which yielded the highest fermentation value (Fig.  3 ). There was no significant difference between S1 and S4, but the fermentation value of S1 was significantly higher than those of S2, S3, and S5 by 1.57, 1.29, and 1.51 times, respectively (Fig.  3 ). Similarly, strain Y832 achieved optimal propagation and fermentation abilities with the S1 formulation. There was no significant difference between the S2 and S5 formulations, but both were significantly higher than S3 and S4 formulations. Strain Y878 also showed the best propagation and highest fermentation values under the S1 formulation. Although there was no significant difference between S2, S3, and S4, the S1 formulation was significantly better than S5 (Fig.  3 ). Based on these results, strains Y364, Y832, and Y878 were fermented using the S1 formulation, comprising a substrate mix of vermicompost, wine lees, cow dung, and oil cake in a 1:1:1:1 ratio. The trial was conducted in a field known for severe wilting disease in Fuquan, another town of Guizhou Province. Compared to the control treatment, which is treated with traditional organic fertilization, fields pre-treated with chemotactic SynCom fertilizer exhibited a lower disease index (Fig.  4 A); R. solanacearum in the rhizosphere soil was significantly reduced as well (Fig.  4 B). Notably, the chemotactic SynCom fertilizer nearly doubled crop yield (Fig.  4 C). Tobacco plants treated with chemotactic SynCom fertilizer displayed a significantly increased number of leaves, and a clear increased tendency in leaf area, plant height, and stem perimeter (Supplemental Fig. S 4 ). In conclusion, this chemotactic SynCom fertilizer can reduce bacterial wilt but also promote the growth of tobacco plants. The phyla Bacillota and Chloroflexiota were significantly enriched in the treatment, whereas the phyla Pseudomonadota and Acidobacteriota were significantly reduced after treatment with chemotactic SynCom fertilizer (Fig.  5 C). The data showed 125 genera from phyla Pseudomonadota, Acidobacteriota, and Bacteriodota were enriched in the control, while 76 genera from phyla Bacillota and Chloroflexiota were enriched in the treatment (Fig.  5 C and 5 ). In this study, we used Tax4Fun to predict the functional profiles of microbial communities based on 16S rRNA sequencing data. This approach provides valuable insights into potential functional shifts following SynCom application. The results showed chemotactic SynCom fertilizer enriched most biosynthesis- and metabolism-related pathways, including purine metabolism and pyruvate metabolism (Fig.  5 E), which may enhance the growth and activity of beneficial microbes and strengthen their niche competitiveness. We subsequently examined the correlation between the enriched microbial phyla and the cumulative abundances of functional genes associated with the top 15 KEGG pathways, as predicted by Tax4Fun, in both control and treatment groups. Intriguingly, enriched genes from biosynthesis and metabolism pathways were positively correlated with the enriched phyla Bacillota and Chloroflexiota, whereas it showed a negative correlation with phyla Pseudomonadota and Acidobacteriota (Fig.  5 F), indicating phyla Bacillota and Chloroflexiota were positively correlated with metabolism-related gene pathways, and their synergistic role in enhancing microbial functional potential under SynCom treatment. Discussion In healthy plants, the microbes generally form a multifunction “holobiont” with their host plants to promote plant healthy (VandenkoornHuyse et al. 2015 ). Considering its high efficiency and eco-friendly, the introduction of antagonistic bacteria into diseased soil is a common approach to combat bacterial wilt. These microorganisms directly inhibit pathogens by secreting antibacterial compounds and competing with pathogens for nutrition resources (Gu et al. 2020 ; Rehm et al. 2023 ; Wang et al. 2023 ). Indirectly, they stimulate plant defense, suppress pathogen-associated helpers, and enrich the niches of native beneficial microbes (Finkel et al. 2017 ; Yu et al. 2019 ; Li et al. 2022 ). Colonization of beneficial microbes is required for plant health, growth, and resilience (Babalola 2010 ). Before reaching the root surface, microbes need to navigate toward plant roots by perception of root exudate and targeted movement, a process mediated by chemotaxis (Bais et al. 2006 ). Chemotactic bacterial community not only benefit from their own enhanced motility and root colonized capabilities (Liu et al. 2024 ), but may also contribute to shaping a favorable rhizosphere environment, possibly by secreting signaling molecules or biofilm matrix formulations that facilitate the colonization of other beneficial microbes (Lin et al. 2022 ; Sun et al. 2022 ), collectively enhancing plant resistance to pathogens. In this study, we developed introduced a SynCom design strategy by that intergrates antagonistic strength and chemotactic potential (cheA), a compatibility to ensure functional robustness and ecological balance. This approach goes beyond single-gene selection and aims to capture the complex dynamics required for effective biocontrol. In this study, half of strongly inhibitory strains isolated from healthy plant rhizosphere possessed cheA gene, indicating their ability to sense environmental compounds (Fig.  1 ). Interestingly, another half of isolated strains, which lacked cheA gene, still displayed strong antagonistic activity, suggesting no direct correlation between chemotaxis and antagonism. Strains Y364, Y832, and Y878 used for a SynCom were identified as P. nicosulfuronedens, P. citronellolis, and B. altitudinis, respectively, that exhibited strong antagonism against R. solanacearum, harbored the chemotaxis gene cheA, and showed mutual compatibility (Fig.  1 ). Microbial community with high diversity may result in multiple functioning and stability (Wei et al. 2015 ; Hu et al. 2016 ). Therefore, it would be interesting to test biocontrol efficiency of SynCom with more bacterial species in the future. Currently, Bacillus, Pseudomonas, and other genera are widely used as a biocontrol to protect plant health (Chlebek et al. 2020 ; Yang et al. 2023b ). For instance, P. citronellolis is predominantly isolated from healthy plants and may enhance plant hormone levels to promote resistance (Remus-Emsermann et al. 2016 ), whereas B. altitudinis has been shown to promote plant growth and combat the pathogen V. dahliae (Chlebek et al. 2020 ). Root exudates contain hundreds of different compounds that rhizosphere microbes can utilize to enhance their growth, colonization, and secretion (Hu et al. 2018 ). In this study, we found that half of the tested compounds promoted the growth of Y364, 17 of 60 compounds significantly enhanced the growth of Y832, and only two compounds induced the growth of Y878. However, none of these compounds, affected the growth of R. solanacearum (Supplementary Fig. S3 ). To optimize prebiotic formulations, we also assessed the antagonistic effects of fermentation broths containing various substances on R. solanacearum. Our results showed that the addition of vitamin C, propionic acid, and esculetin enhanced the antagonistic capacity of SynCom fermentation broths against R. solanacearum, indicating that certain prebiotics can improve both growth and bioactivity (Fig.  3 ). This suggests a direct functional role for these additives in promoting disease suppression. In contrast, the subsequent optimization of organic fertilizer substrates was designed as a technical step to maximize biomass yield and fermentation performance of SynCom strains. These formulations were not tested for antagonistic activity in vitro but provide a field-level biocontrol validation (Fig.  4 ). Previous studies have shown that the use of organic materials such as vermicompost, cow manure, and oil cake can significantly improve microbial colonization and persistence in the rhizosphere (Das et al. 2017 ; PanteLides et al. 2023 ; Wu et al. 2023 ). The synergistic effect of these materials not only enhances soil physicochemical properties but also promotes the establishment of functional microbial consortia. Although our findings support the role of chemotactic SynCom in enhancing disease suppression, future studies should include non-chemotactic SynCom as experimental controls. Such a design will further clarify the mechanistic contribution of chemotaxis to SynCom performance and facilitate the rational development of microbial inoculants. To assess its effects on native microbes, we performed 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing of the rhizosphere soil from control and treated groups. After treatment with SynCom fertilizer, the phyla Bacillota and Chloroflexota were significantly enriched, whereas Pseudomonadota and Acidobacteria were notably reduced (Fig.  5 C and 5 ). Tax4Fun functional predictions revealed striking differences between the control and treatment groups in biosynthesis and metabolism pathways, such as purine metabolism and amino acid biosynthesis (Fig.  5 E), indicating that SynCom fertilizer may have activated native microbial metabolism and restricted pathogen niches. Similar researches showed prebiotics can modulate microbial community assembly by enhancing the competitiveness and niche occupancy of beneficial taxa (Song et al. 2021 ; Wen et al. 2023 ). However, it provided only indirect insights into microbial functions and niche shifts. Nonetheless, our findings offer a valuable basis for hypothesis generation, and future studies using direct functional assays will be essential to validate chemotactic strains impacts on microbial communities. Further analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between the enriched genes and the phyla Bacillota and Chloroflexota (Fig.  5 F). The application of chemotactic synthetic bacterial fertilizer enhances the metabolic capacity of the phyla Bacillota and Chloroflexota, thereby improving their ability to utilize soil resources. This advantage enables them to outcompete R. solanacearum for resources, secure a higher ecological niche, and effectively suppress bacterial wilt. In conclusion, we have developed a SynCom fertilizer based on microbial antagonistic activity and chemotaxis. Field experiments demonstrated its biocontrol potential and provided valuable insights into the bacterial community dynamics in rhizosphere soil treated with this chemotactic SynCom fertilizer.
