Efficient synthesis of optically pure D-lactate from mixed sugars by engineered
E. coli consortium.

In this study, we constructed two engineered E. coli strains that utilized
glucose (E. coli GL10) and xylose (E. coli XL12) simultaneously via division of
labor. Both two engineered strains blocked the synthetic pathway of several
byproducts, including succinate, formate, ethanol, and acetate. XL12
demonstrated the alleviation of carbon catabolite repression effect and the
improvement of xylose utilization in mixed sugar. GL10 and XL12 produced
D-lactate from glucose and xylose with an optical purity of 100%, respectively.
Microbial consortium using GL10 and XL12 with the inoculation ratio of 1:1
achieved complete glucose depletion and 53.21% xylose utilization
simultaneously. Notably, when the fermentation was proceeded using mixed sugar
for 36 h, the D-lactate titer of the consortium achieved 3.76 g/L, which is
5.96-fold higher than that of the wild-type (WT) fermentation. The effect of
shaking speed and inoculum ratio on the fermentation performance were
investigated. Specifically, the xylose utilization in the mixed sugars was
improved with an increase of shaking speeds from 50 to 250 rpm, while the yield
of D-lactate did not further increase at 250 rpm. When the inoculation ratio of
GL10 and XL12 was 1:5, the consortium showed better performance in D-lactate
production, which was 6.55-fold higher than that of the WT. The consortium was
also utilized mixed sugars effectively in corn straw hydrolysate and produced
D-lactate of 5.15 g/L, which was 41.87% higher than that of WT. Our study offers
a feasible approach via metabolic engineering and artificial consortium
construction for bio-based D-lactate production.

In this study, we constructed two engineered E. coli strains that utilized glucose (E. coli GL10) and xylose (E. coli XL12) simultaneously via division of labor. Both two engineered strains blocked the synthetic pathway of several byproducts, including succinate, formate, ethanol, and acetate. XL12 demonstrated the alleviation of carbon catabolite repression effect and the improvement of xylose utilization in mixed sugar. GL10 and XL12 produced D-lactate from glucose and xylose with an optical purity of 100%, respectively. Microbial consortium using GL10 and XL12 with the inoculation ratio of 1:1 achieved complete glucose depletion and 53.21% xylose utilization simultaneously. Notably, when the fermentation was proceeded using mixed sugar for 36 h, the D-lactate titer of the consortium achieved 3.76 g/L, which is 5.96-fold higher than that of the wild-type (WT) fermentation. The effect of shaking speed and inoculum ratio on the fermentation performance were investigated. Specifically, the xylose utilization in the mixed sugars was improved with an increase of shaking speeds from 50 to 250 rpm, while the yield of D-lactate did not further increase at 250 rpm. When the inoculation ratio of GL10 and XL12 was 1:5, the consortium showed better performance in D-lactate production, which was 6.55-fold higher than that of the WT. The consortium was also utilized mixed sugars effectively in corn straw hydrolysate and produced D-lactate of 5.15 g/L, which was 41.87% higher than that of WT. Our study offers a feasible approach via metabolic engineering and artificial consortium construction for bio-based D-lactate production. D-lactate is not only widely used as a food additive in fermented foods [ 3 ], but also serves as a key component in the synthesis of stereocomplex polylactic acid (SC-PLA), a copolymer of D-lactate and L-lactate. SC-PLA exhibits comparable physicochemical properties, mechanical performance, and thermal stability to petroleum-based plastics, highlighting its considerable potential for application in food packaging materials. However, its practical applications are severely limited by thermal instability above 175 °C [ 4 ]. Owing to its higher heat deflection temperature, D-lactate elevates the melting point of poly-D-lactate (PDLA) by 40°C-50 °C, thereby broadening its utility in food packaging applications [ [5] , [6] , [7] ]. Moreover, PLA-derived products can replace petroleum-based polymers such as polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), and polystyrene (PS) in biocompatibility-sensitive applications, including fiber manufacturing, nonwoven, and films [ 8 ]. Given that the physicochemical properties and biodegradation kinetics of PLA are intrinsically governed by its stereochemical configuration, the production of D-lactate with high optical purity has emerged as a critical technological barrier for industrial advancement in the bioplastic sector. Lactate is synthesized primarily through two approaches, chemical catalysis and microbial biosynthesis. Chemical synthesis of lactate relies on petroleum-derived precursors. Conventional processes involve sequential catalytic oxidation of ethylene to acetaldehyde, then to acetonitrile, followed by hydrolysis to yield racemic lactate (a mixture of L- and D-lactate) [ 9 , 10 ]. This necessitates chiral separation to obtain enantiomerically pure D-lactate, rendering such methods unsuitable for large-scale production. In contrast, microbial biosynthesis offers a sustainable, scalable platform. Among reported microbial chassis, Lactobacillus gasseri (i-ldhL1) produce D-lactate with a high conversion rate when glucose is used as the sole carbon source [ 11 ]. Heterofermentative lactic acid bacteria readily use xylose as a carbon source, however, their phosphoketolase pathway results in concomitant production of lactic acid and acetic acid [ 12 ]. Recombinant Corynebacterium glutamicum can yield up to high titer of D-lactate (the highest level reached 195 g/L), but suffers from byproducts formation and stringent nutritional demands [ 13 ]. Thus, a microbial chassis that can produce high optically pure D-lactate with a minimum formation of byproducts is needed. Escherichia coli excels with rapid growth, minimal nutrients, and superior genetic tractability [ 14 ]. E. coli harbors the ldhA gene, which endows it with inherent advantages for producing D-lactate of high optical purity. Therefore, the thorough exploitation of the potential in different carbon sources utilization by E. coli facilitate the production of high optical purity D-lactate. Lignocellulosic sugars derived from non-food biomass offer advantages in terms of abundance, sustainability, and cost-effectiveness, making them attractive feedstocks for bioplastic manufacturing [ 15 ]. The utilization of non-food lignocellulosic biomass as a core substrate involves pretreatment and hydrolysis to convert cellulose and hemicellulose into ferment sugars (e.g., glucose and xylose), thereby advancing economic viability and reducing fossil fuel dependency [ 16 ]. Glucose and xylose can both be used as the sole carbon source for E. coli, and a high yield (more than 95%) and optical purity (more than 99%) of D-lactate are achieved, as observed in engineered E. coli JU15 and LAC [ [17] , [18] , [19] ]. Nevertheless, the compositional complexity of lignocellulosic hydrolysates generally leads to insufficient utilization of mixed sugars by engineered E. coli. The strain AV03 (ΔpoxB, ΔackA-pta, ΔmgsA) derived from E. coli JU15 further improves the simultaneous consumption of mixed sugars and weaken the acetic acid production in simulated hydrolysate [ 20 ]. However, a small amount of L-lactic acid was detected in the fermentation of 40 g/L xylose by AV03, which still limited the preparation of lactate with high optical purity. Carbon catabolite repression (CCR) complicates the design and control of fermentation process using lignocellulosic biomass hydrolysate, resulting in reduced productivity and underutilized pentose sugars [ 21 ]. To address these limitations, synthetic biology approaches have proposed modular co-culture strategies that employ engineered microbial populations with defined metabolic interactions to enhance mixed-sugar utilization [ 22 , 23 ]. By designing functionally complementary consortia, metabolic division of labor can be optimized to improve D-lactate production [ 24 ]. Shifting from engineering a single “universal” strain to a co-culture system comprising glucose- and xylose-specific strains represents a fundamental paradigm shift. The core theoretical advantage lies in the physical segregation of metabolic tasks, transforming inherent incompatibilities within a single strain into coordinated division of labor and collaboration at the community level, thereby achieving system-level performance beyond that of conventional approaches [ 25 ]. Eiteman [ 26 ] constructed two substrate-selective E. coli strains, ALS1073 (ΔpflBΔptsGΔmanZΔglk), unable to utilize glucose, and ALS1074 (ΔpflBΔxylA), unable to utilize xylose, which co-produced D-lactate along with byproducts (succinate, acetate) under mixed glucose and xylose fermentation. The presence of byproducts compromises the purity of the D-lactate product. This study aimed to alleviate the CCR effect in engineered E. coli during mixed sugars fermentation and enhance D-lactate production. Two division-of-labor engineered E. coli strains, XL12 and GL10, were constructed and characterized in terms of sugar utilization and D-lactate synthesis capabilities. A co-culture strategy was implemented to improve metabolic orthogonality toward specific carbon sources within mixed sugars. The effects of shaking speed and inoculum ratio on D-lactate production from mixed sugar by the microbial consortium were investigated. The performance of the consortium in corn straw hydrolysate fermentation to produce D-lactate was explored. 2 Materials and methods 2.1 Strain construction E coli DH5α was used as a host for plasmid construction. E coli BL21 (DE3) was used as a parental strain for generating knockout mutants. Genome editing was performed using the CRISPR/Cas9 system [ 27 ]. The detailed procedures of genome editing are provided in Section S1 of the Supplementary Materials. To redirect carbon flux, the ptsI gene (disabling the phosphotransferase system for glucose uptake) and glk gene (eliminating glucose phosphorylation) were deleted. Subsequent knockout of pps, poxB, and pflB in the base strain yielded strain XL5 (ΔglkΔptsIΔppsΔpoxBΔpflB). Further knockout of pta, frdABC, adhE, dld, and mgsA in XL5 generated strain XL12 (ΔglkΔptsIΔppsΔpoxBΔpflBΔptaΔfrdABCΔadhEΔdldΔmgsA) ( Fig. 1 A). In parallel, a glucose-specialized strain, designated GL10, was constructed by deleting pps, poxB, pflB, pta, frdABC, adhE, dld, and mgsA genes yielded a glucose-specialized strain designated as GL10 ( Fig. 1 B). Gene expression levels comparisons between XL12 and WT were performed using Student's t-test. p < 0.01 marked with “∗∗” was considered very statistically significant. 3 Results and discussion 3.1 Strain construction Strain XL12 (ΔglkΔptsIΔppsΔpoxBΔpflBΔptaΔfrdABCΔadhEΔdldΔmgsA) and strain GL10 (ΔppsΔpoxBΔpflBΔptaΔfrdABCΔadhEΔdldΔmgsA) were successfully constructed via targeted gene knockout ( Fig. 1 ). Colony PCR and agarose gel electrophoresis confirmed the genotype of each mutant ( Fig. S1 ). The targeted genes were successfully knockout and confirmed by qRT-PCR. The genes pgi, ppc and ackA in strain XL12 was down-regulated by 70.95%, 37.52% and 59.75% respectively compared to the wild-type (WT) ( Fig. S2 ). Specifically, the significant down-regulated in pgi expression implied that the knockout of glk limited the flux of the Embden-Meyerhof-Parnas (EMP) pathway in XL12. The down-regulation of ppc expression suggested that pps gene knockout reduced the metabolic flux of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP) into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. The intermediate PEP might be directed to the pyruvate synthesis pathway, thus facilitating the synthesis of the target product D-lactate. The decrease in ackA expression might result in the inhibitory synthesis of the by-product acetate. 3.2 Fermentation performance of xylose-utilizing strains 3.2.1 D-lactate production by E. coli XL5 in single carbon source medium Prior to XL12 construction, XL5 was constructed as an intermediate step to investigate whether the deletion of glk, ptsI, pps, poxB, and pflB could disrupt glucose metabolism and boost carbon flux to D-lactate. Strain XL5 (ΔglkΔptsIΔppsΔpoxBΔpflB) was successfully constructed via CRISPR/Cas9 ( Fig. S3 ). Growth and sugar consumption kinetics of XL5 under different carbon sources were analyzed. With glucose as the sole carbon source, XL5 exhibited a significantly delayed exponential growth phase, reaching an OD600 of 1.46 at 36 h–24 h later than WT. WT consumed most glucose within 12 h and leaving 0.67 g/L residual glucose. XL5 initiated glucose utilization after 24 h and depleted it by 36 h. With xylose as the sole carbon source, XL5 reached a maximum biomass 0.46-fold higher than in glucose-grown cultures. Both WT and XL5 fully consumed 5 g/L xylose within 36 h and 48 h, respectively ( Fig. 2 A). Deletion of ptsI and glk in XL5 disrupted the phosphotransferase system (PTS), impairing glucose utilization. Residual glucose consumption indicated the involvement of alternative transporters, such as galactose—H+ symport protein GalP and methylgalactoside transport system Mgl, which are inherently less efficient than PTS, causing slower glucose assimilation and growth delay [ 31 ]. When glucose as the sole carbon source, WT produced 0.09 g/L D-lactate after 36 h. XL5 produced 2.66 g/L of D-lactate, corresponding to a 53.2% conversion yield and a productivity of 0.07 g/L/h, which was 28.56-fold higher than that of the WT. When xylose was used as the sole carbon source, D-lactate was not detected in the WT culture, while XL5 produced 0.21 g/L D-lactate, achieving a conversion rate of 4.2% ( Fig. 2 B). Zhou et al. demonstrated that in glucose-grown E. coli, deletions of pps and poxB had little effect on D-lactate production, whereas deletion of pflB improved yield by 6% [ 32 ]. The low D-lactate yield from xylose suggests that knocking out pps, poxB, and pflB is insufficient to fully redirect metabolic flux. 3.2.2 D-lactate production by E. coli XL12 in single carbon source medium To further enhance D-lactate production, XL12 was constructed via metabolic flux redirection. When glucose was used as the sole carbon source, XL12 demonstrated improved growth compared to the WT. XL12 reached an OD600 of 2.89 at 36 h. WT depleted 5 g/L of glucose within 12 h, whereas XL12 began utilizing glucose at 12 h and completed consumption by 36 h. When xylose was used as the sole carbon source, XL12 exhibited slower growth compared to WT. WT consumed 5 g/L of xylose within 36 h, while XL12 initiated xylose utilization at 12 h ( Fig. 3 A). The earlier utilization of xylose by XL12 (12 h earlier than XL5) may be attributed to the deletion of the mgsA. Methylglyoxal is synthesized with the enzymatic activity of methylglyoxal synthase coding by mgsA gene. The accumulation of methylglyoxal may damage the cell metabolism and may have caused continuously high stress levels, thereby limiting product synthesis [ 33 ]. Moreover, deletion of mgsA improves sugar co-metabolism in E. coli [ 34 ]. Therefore, establishing a mgsA deficient strain may redirect the carbon metabolic flux from the methylglyoxal bypass to the glycolysis and pentose phosphate pathways (PPP), thereby enhancing the xylose conversion efficiency. These results demonstrates that the knockout of the glk and ptsI genes successfully reprogrammed XL12 into a xylose-preferring D-lactate producer. Meanwhile, deletion of competing pathway genes reduced byproducts such as succinate, formate, acetate, and ethanol ( Fig. S4 and S5 ), enhancing carbon flux from xylose to D-lactate. 3.2.3 D-lactate production by E. coli XL12 in mixed sugar medium In mixed sugar medium, XL12 exhibited better growth than WT after 24 h. As expected, WT depleted glucose rapidly but could not utilize xylose in mixed sugar medium. On the contrary, XL12 co-utilized glucose and xylose simultaneously in the mixed sugar medium. Although XL12 consumed glucose slowly in comparison with WT, XL12 utilized 56.57% of xylose at 72 h ( Fig. 4 A). More importantly, the optical purity of D-lactate reached 100% ( Table S4 ). Previous studies on E. coli fermentation suggest that optimal C/N ratios typically enhance transcriptional mRNA levels and enzymatic activity, thereby boosting product synthesis [ 35 ]. Nitrogen limitation can impede further growth of strain [ 36 ]. This implies that C/N ratio in the mixed-sugar medium may influence both sugar utilization efficiency and strain growth. Furthermore, the knockout of the dld gene in strain XL12 blocked the reverse reaction of D-lactate, preventing lactate from serving as a carbon source to enter the TCA cycle via pyruvate, thereby potentially further enhancing the utilization of carbon sources in the medium [ 37 ]. When glucose was used as the sole carbon source, GL10 exhibited growth kinetics similar to WT. Both WT and GL10 fully consumed 5 g/L glucose within 12 h, indicating that genetic modifications did no impair glucose metabolism. When xylose was used as the sole carbon source, growth and xylose consumption rates of GL10 and WT were nearly identical ( Fig. 5 A). Knockout of frdABC, pflB, adhE, poxB and pta effectively blocked the accumulation of succinate, formate, ethanol, and acetate in single-carbon fermentation ( Fig. S7 and Fig. S8 ). 3.3.2 D-lactate production by E. coli GL10 in mixed sugar medium In mixed sugar medium, GL10 exhibited improved growth over WT, with consistently higher OD600 values throughout fermentation. Both WT and GL10 fully consumed 5 g/L glucose within 12 h. Whereas WT and GL10 showed negligible xylose utilization ( Fig. 6 A). After 36 h, GL10 produced 3.45 g/L D-lactate in mixed sugars, representing an 8.32-fold increase over WT, with a conversion rate of 34.5% and productivity of 0.10 g/L/h ( Fig. 6 B). As expected, the optical purity of D-lactate generated from GL10 also reached 100% ( Table S4 ). No detectable succinate, formate, acetate, or ethanol was observed ( Fig. S9 ). By comparison with the single glucose system, the D-lactate conversion rate of GL10 decreased from 63% to 34.5% in mixed-sugars. This reduction is likely attributed to slower growth and the inability to utilize xylose after glucose exhaustion. Lactate anabolism was suppressed due to growth limitation and low xylose uptake [ 42 ]. These findings suggest that the presence of xylose may negatively influence the capacity of GL10 to produce D-lactate in mixed-sugar systems. To address this limitation, a co-culture system with metabolically specialized strains (e.g., GL10 and XL12) was developed to improve D-lactate yield in mixed sugar fermentation. Fig. 6 3.4 Mixed sugar co-fermentation Based on the performance of single-carbon-source fermentation, XL12 and GL10 exhibited clear metabolic division of labor. However, mixed sugar fermentation using either strain alone remained limited by substrate interaction. To further improve D-lactate production, a bacterial consortium fermentation strategy was adopted. 3.4.1 D-lactate production by the consortium in mixed sugars A dual-strain consortium is considered an effective approach for co-utilizing glucose and xylose [ 43 ]. Under identical inoculum densities, the consortium (GL10 and XL12) exhibited superior growth to WT in mixed sugar medium ( Fig. S10A ). Both the consortium and WT fully consumed glucose within 12 h ( Fig. S10B ), but the consortium consumed more xylose (1.22 g/L) than WT (0.45 g/L) by 36 h ( Fig. S10C ). Dien et al. demonstrated that the deletion of the ptsG gene within the PTS enhanced xylose consumption in the presence of glucose, though 40% of xylose remained unused after glucose depletion [ 44 ]. Our results confirm that the consortium maintains glucose utilization while enhancing xylose utilization efficiency. It indicates that despite knockout of pps, poxB, pflB, pta, frdABC, adhE, dld, and mgsA, key EMP pathway enzymes such as hexokinase and phosphofructokinase remain functional, allowing glucose breakdown to pyruvate to proceed. Pyruvate can be channeled to D-lactate through the remaining pathways (such as lactate dehydrogenase or entry into the TCA cycle) without the disrupting metabolic flux. In mixed sugar fermentation, the consortium produced 3.76 g/L of D-lactate, achieving a 37.6% conversion yield and a productivity of 0.10 g/L/h–a 5.96-fold increase over WT. Eiteman et al. previously confirmed that co-culture systems enable lactate production from mixed sugars [ 26 ]. E. coli can adapt to the presence of mixed sugars through a variety of sugar transporter proteins and complex gene expression regulatory mechanisms. However, when a single strain is used to synthesize D-lactate from mixed sugars, the metabolic repression induced by CCR lead to low substrate conversion efficiency. The construction of a functionally complementary E. coli co-culture system may improve the utilization efficiency of mixed sugars through metabolic division of labor and synergistic effects between strains, thereby facilitating D-lactate biosynthesis. 3.4.2 Impact of shaking speed on the consortium performance in mixed sugar fermentation Effects of shaking speed (50 rpm and 250 rpm) on D-lactate production was investigated. At 50 rpm, the consortium exhibited consistently lower OD600 values and reduced sugar consumption (both glucose and xylose) relative to WT. WT showed 4.22 g/L glucose and negligible xylose consumption at 36 h. The consortium utilized 1.26 g/L glucose and 0.27 g/L xylose at the same time ( Fig. S11A ). In contrast, the consortium exhibited better growth and improved sugar utilization with the shaking speed at 250 rpm. All glucose was consumed by both WT and the consortium at 12 h. When the fermentation proceeded for 72 h, the utilization of xylose by the consortium was 4.29 times higher than that of WT ( Fig. S11B ). The fermentation performance on 50 rpm showed that WT produced 2.99 g/L D-lactate with a conversion rate of 29.9% and a productivity of 0.08 g/L/h when the fermentation proceeded for 36 h. The consortium produced 1.04 g/L D-lactate with a yield of 0.10 g/g and a conversion rate of 10.4% at the same time ( Fig. 7 ). The production of D-lactate decreased by 65.22% compared to WT and by 72.94% relative to its own performance at 150 rpm. The fermentation performance on 250 rpm showed that higher shaking speed improved biomass and sugar consumption but not D-lactate titer. The consortium produced 3.22 g/L D-lactate with a conversion yield of 32.2% and a productivity of 0.09 g/L/h when the fermentation proceeded for 36 h ( Fig. 7 ). At the same shaking speed, D-lactate was not detected in WT. The better growth of the consortium (OD600 = 2.44) and the maximum xylose utilization (36.2%) were observed at 250 rpm. Nevertheless, maximum D-lactate production (3.5 g/L) occurred at 150 rpm. While lower DO enhances lactate dehydrogenase activity and lactate production [ 45 ]. DO is a crucial factor that affect microbial growth, product accumulation, and lactate production [ 46 ]. Compared with the culture in 250 rpm, the consortium resulted in reduced sugar consumption and higher D-lactate production. This may be attributed to the fact that oxygen acts as an electron acceptor for oxidative phosphorylation [ 47 ]. Relatively low DO may reflect a relatively low level of oxidative phosphorylation, which may benefit D-lactate production. On the contrary, when the fermentation proceeded at 250 rpm, sufficient DO may lead to more sugar consumption, an increased oxidative phosphorylation, and ATP accumulation, thereby diverting carbon flux away from D-lactate synthesis. Therefore, DO levels significantly regulate the metabolic resources allocation between E. coli growth and D-lactate synthesis. 3.4.3 Impact of inoculation ratio on the consortium performance in mixed sugar fermentation To improve xylose utilization in the mixed sugar fermentation, the inoculum ratio of GL10 to XL12 was adjusted. As a result, at an inoculation ratio of 1:5, the consortium consumed 92.93% of glucose and 4.05% of xylose by 36 h. At an inoculation ratio of 1:15, the consortium consumed 85.29% of glucose and 19.37% of xylose. With the increase of XL12 inoculum size, the consortium showed better growth than WT ( Fig. 8 A and B). The titer of D-lactate reached 4.53 g/L with a conversion rate of 45.3%, and a production rate of 0.13 g/L/h at an inoculation ratio of 1:5, which was 6.55-fold higher than those of WT. At an inoculation ratio of 1:15, the titer of D-lactate reached 4.38 g/L with a conversion rate of 43.8% and a production rate of 0.12 g/L/h, which did not show significant difference over the culture with an inoculation ratio of 1:5 ( Fig. 8 C). ABC transporter complex xylFGH is responsible for the transmembrane transport of xylose in E. coli. One molecule of ATP per sugar is needed by the ABC transporter [ 48 , 49 ]. In the XL12, deletion of the ptsI gene impairs the function of the PTS system, thereby indirectly reducing ATP production efficiency by inhibiting the EMP. To resist the survival pressure of low pH, the cells need to allocate additional energy for the expression and regulation of acid tolerance functional factors [ 50 ], which further exacerbates ATP consumption. Meanwhile, the medium pH was about 4.5-5.0 when the fermentation finished, implying that the acidification of the medium may inhibit the capacity of E. coli in xylose utilization. Environmental stress not only inhibits E. coli growth but also reprograms intracellular metabolic fluxes [ 51 ], potentially explaining the weakened xylose utilization in mixed sugars. Some strategies are commonly used to improve the resistance of E. coli to acidic condition during fermentation, including adaptive laboratory evolution (ALE), heterologous expression of anti-acid components, and neutralizing agent supplementation [ 22 , 50 , 52 , 53 ]. It should be noted that the period of ALE is comparative longer. Heterologous expression of anti-acid components may bring metabolic burden to microbial cells. The addition of neutralizing agents may increase osmotic stress and complicates the downstream product recovery process. Therefore, integrating genetic modifications with multi-faceted acid resistance strategies may further improve the resistance of E. coli to acidic pH and strengthen D-lactate production. 3.5 D-lactate production by the bacterial consortium in corn straw hydrolysate Due to the phenolic compounds have strong toxicity toward E. coli cells, the fermentation was performed using detoxified corn straw hydrolysate. As a result, the utilization efficiency of glucose and xylose by the consortium was similar to that of WT. When fermentation proceeded to 72 h, the residual glucose and xylose in the consortium were 0.50 ± 0.09 g/L and 3.23 ± 0.15 g/L, respectively. Notably, the D-lactate yield of the bacterial consortium was significantly higher than that of WT. At 36 h of fermentation, the D-lactate titer reached 5.15 ± 0.22 g/L, which was 41.87% higher than that of WT ( Fig. 9 ). The yield of D-lactate in corn straw hydrolysate was higher than those of the simulated hydrolysate systems described above. Corn straw hydrolysate may contain other carbohydrate components such as arabinose, which can be utilized by E. coli to enter the PPP for energy metabolism [ 54 , 55 ]. This may be one of the important contributing factors to the improved D-lactate yield in the consortium. After 12 h of fermentation, the mixed bacterial consortium basically ceased or only utilized a negligible amount of carbohydrate substrates. This phenomenon is likely closely associated with the acidification of the fermentation medium caused by the efficient synthesis of D-lactate, as evidenced by the measured medium pH of 4.5 at the end of cultivation. This result further confirms that acid toxicity exerts a significant inhibitory effect on the metabolic activity of E. coli. Fig. 9 4 Conclusions In this study, we employed CRISPR/Cas9 to alleviate CCR effect, reprogram metabolic fluxes, enhance mixed sugars utilization, and improve D-lactate production. Based on the characterization of two engineered carbon metabolic division of labor strains, a bacterial consortium was established and was capable of utilizing glucose and xylose simultaneously. Notably, the bacterial consortium achieved 0.376 g/g sugar of D-lactate with 100% optical purity, which is 5.96-fold higher than that of WT fermentation. The dissolved oxygen level may critically influence sugar utilization and D-lactate production by engineered E. coli. An increase of the proportion of XL12 in the bacterial consortium reduced the sugar consumption but benefited D-lactate accumulation. The bacterial consortium is capable of utilizing mixed sugars in corn stalk hydrolysate and effectively synthesize D-lactate. Overall, these results provide conceptual frameworks and experimental evidence for sustainable D-lactate biorefinery from mixed sugars.
