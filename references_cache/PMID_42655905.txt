Title: Innovatively Unlocking Anammox Driven by Nitrite Accumulation in a nrfA‐Deficient Shewanella oneidensis MR‐1 Consortium
Journal: Water Environment Research
Publication date: 2026 Aug 26

===== OPEN-ACCESS FULL TEXT (PMC13519132 selected support sections) =====
ABSTRACT With the advancement of industrialization and urbanization, nitrogen pollution has become increasingly severe, and accelerating the nitrogen cycle is of great significance for nitrogen removal in wastewater treatment. To enhance the nitrogen (N) removal efficiency, we constructed a novel coupled system integrating anammox with Shewanella oneidensis MR‐1. We specifically focused on the functional differences between its wild‐type and a nrfA ‐deficient mutant (Δ nrfA ). We systematically evaluated the N removal performance, microbial community structure, and nitrogen/carbon metabolic functional genes under different inoculation ratios. Although the wild‐type strain could supply nitrite (NO 2 − ‐N) for anammox via dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium (DNRA), its excessive inoculation triggered substrate competition with denitrification, weakening the anammox dominance. Conversely, the mutant strain can cause the accumulation of NO 2 − ‐N by blocking the DNRA pathway, thereby forming a stable synergistic interaction with anammox bacteria. The coupled system achieved a maximum total N removal efficiency of 97% with the fastest reaction kinetics at an optimal anammox‐to‐mutant volume ratio of 5:1. Metagenomic analysis corroborated these findings, revealing significant enrichment of key anammox functional genes ( hzs and hdh ) under this optimized condition. In summary, strategically coupling anammox with an electron‐transfer‐capable, DNRA‐deficient mutant ensures a sustained NO 2 − ‐N supply. This genetic manipulation strategy offers a novel, efficient, and stable paradigm for advancing anaerobic nitrogen removal in nitrate‐laden wastewater treatment. Keywords: anammox, DNRA, metagenomics, mutant, nitrogen removal
Summary A novel coupled system integrating anammox with Shewanella oneidensis MR‐1 was constructed. This study investigates the differences in nitrogen removal performances between the wild‑type and the nrfA ‑deficient mutant ( ΔnrfA ) of S. oneidensis MR‑1 when coupled with anammox. This study regulates electron flow through genetic engineering strategies by coupling anammox with electron‑transfer‑capable ΔnrfA S. oneidensis MR‑1. The highest total N removal rate (97%) and the fastest reaction kinetics were achieved at anammox sludge to ΔnrfA mutant volume ratio of 5:1.
This study proves that regulating electron flow via genetic engineering strategies by coupling anammox with electron‐transfer‐capable ΔnrfA Shewanella oneidensis MR‐1 represents a novel approach for developing efficient and stable anaerobic N removal processes.

1. Introduction
1. Introduction With the development of industry, the discharge of nitrogen‐rich wastewater into natural environments has disrupted the aquatic N balance, causing severe damage to the aquatic ecological environment. Conventional biological N removal processes, especially the nitrification–denitrification pathway, include two phases: (1) Ammonium (NH 4 + ‐N) is oxidized to nitrate (NO 3 − ‐N) under aerobic conditions, and (2) NO 3 − ‐N is reduced to nitrogen gas (N 2 ) under anaerobic conditions, and the organic carbons serve as electron donors (Yang et al. 2020 ). However, this process requires substantial supplementation of oxygen and organic carbons, resulting in high operational costs and the emissions of greenhouse gas N 2 O (Yang et al. 2020 ). In contrast, the anaerobic ammonium oxidation (anammox) process, characterized by its cost‐effectiveness and environmental friendliness—including low oxygen demand, minimal sludge production, reduced greenhouse gas emissions, and no need for external carbon sources—has been widely applied in advanced wastewater treatment (Kartal et al. 2013 ; Zhou et al. 2023 ). Broda ( 1977 ) predicted from evolutionary and thermodynamic perspectives the existence of unknown bacteria capable of oxidizing NH 4 + ‐N to N 2 . Mulder et al. ( 1995 ) experimentally confirmed the occurrence of the anammox process; Strous et al. ( 1998 ) further proposed the stoichiometric ratio of this reaction. 1 NH 4 + + 1.32 NO 2 − + 0.066 HCO 3 − + 0.13 H + → 1.02 N 2 + 0.26 NO 3 − + 0.066 CH 2 O 0.5 N 0.15 + 2.03 H 2 O In the metabolic pathway of anammox, NO 2 − ‐N is initially reduced to NO by the enzyme nitrite reductase (NIR) (Reaction a) (Madigan et al. 2022 ). Subsequently, under the catalysis of hydrazine synthase, the generated NO reacts with NH 4 + ‐N to form hydrazine (N 2 H 4 ) (Reaction b) (Madigan et al. 2022 ). The N 2 H 4 is then oxidized to N 2 by hydrazine dehydrogenase (HDH), during which electrons are released (Reaction c) (Madigan et al. 2022 ). These electrons are subsequently channeled through the electron transport chain to energetically drive both Reactions a and b (Madigan et al. 2022 ). To date, anammox bacteria (AnAOB) encompass seven genera with 25 validly published species, predominantly including: Candidatus Brocadia , Candidatus Kuenenia , Candidatus Scalindua , Candidatus Anammoxoglobus , Candidatus Jettenia , Candidatus Anammoximicrobium , Candidatus Brasilis (Khramenkov et al. 2013 ; Viancelli et al. 2011 ). The deficiency of NO 2 − ‐N as an electron acceptor in wastewater often fails to maintain the optimal NH 4 + /NO 2 − ratio (1:1.32) in anammox reactors, resulting in suboptimal N removal efficiency (< 89%) and limiting the application of anammox processes (Eng Nkonogumo et al. 2024 ; Xue et al. 2023 ). To enhance the efficiency of anammox system, several integrated processes combining anammox with complementary N removal pathways have been developed (Yang et al. 2020 ). For instance, partial nitritation‐anammox (PN/A) and partial denitrification‐anammox (PD/A) processes offer significant energy savings, yet struggle with long‐term operational instability and the overreduction of NO 2 − ‐N to N 2 (Cao et al. 2017 ; Du et al. 2019 ). Similarly, sulfur‐based denitrification with anammox (SDA) and denitrifying anaerobic methane oxidation (DAMO) coupled with anammox expand the applicability of the process but are hindered by the production of toxic sulfide byproducts and slow microbial growth rates, respectively (Kleerebezem and Mendez 2002 ; Deng et al. 2022 ; Zhou et al. 2023 ). Maintaining a stable and efficient NO 2 − ‐N supply remains a primary challenge. To address these substrate‐supply bottlenecks, leveraging specific Dissimilatory Nitrate Reduction to Ammonium (DNRA) bacteria offers a promising alternative. Hao ( 2022 ) observed the coupling of the dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium (DNRA) and anammox during the enrichment of DNRA bacteria in a sequencing batch reactor (SBR). In this system, the interactions among DNRA microorganisms, AnAOB, and heterotrophic denitrifying bacteria significantly influence N removal efficiency. Notably, an excessively high DNRA rate may lead to elevated NH 4 + ‐N levels in the effluent. Currently, various anammox‐based coupled processes still face challenges in practical applications, leading to limited N removal efficiency. In this context, Shewanella , a DNRA bacterium, is a facultative anaerobe widely distributed in marine and freshwater environments, and it can utilize a variety of electron acceptors under anoxic conditions, enabling its survival in diverse habitats (Hau 2007 ). S. oneidensis MR‐1, a versatile facultative anaerobe, emerges as an ideal model organism for metabolic engineering. Crucially, although its genome contains the nrfA gene driving DNRA, it inherently lacks the nirK or nirS genes required for respiratory denitrification (Cruz‐García et al. 2007 ). This unique genetic background ensures that its nitrate respiration does not lead to gaseous nitrogen loss. During nitrate respiration by wild‐type S. oneidensis MR‐1, nitrate is rapidly converted to nitrite, exhibiting a transient nitrite accumulation before further reduction to ammonium (Cruz‐García et al. 2007 ). Although this natural time lag provides a temporary NO 2 − ‐N supply, it is insufficient to sustain robust anammox activity. To transform this transient accumulation into a steady, reliable supply, we innovatively propose a targeted genetic intervention. By precisely knocking out the nrfA gene, the ΔnrfA mutant fundamentally severs the DNRA pathway at the nitrite reduction step. We hypothesize that this genetic blockade will enforce robust and stable NO 2 − ‐N accumulation, perfectly tailored to feed the anammox process without competing for substrate. Currently, the survival characteristics, nitrogen removal efficiency, and synergistic interactions of S. oneidensis MR‐1 within complex wastewater microbiomes remain poorly understood. To establish a stable and efficient anammox‐coupled N removal system, this study coupled anammox with S. oneidensis MR‐1. By analyzing various parameters including NH 4 + ‐N, NO 2 − ‐N, NO 3 − ‐N, COD, TN, metagenomic data, and three‐dimensional excitation‐emission matrix (3D‐EEM) spectra, we evaluated the differences in N removal performance and underlying metabolic mechanisms between the two systems. This genetic manipulation strategy aims to provide new insights and theoretical support for the development of stable, highly efficient anammox‐based processes for nitrate‐laden wastewater treatment.

2.1. Reactor's Setup and Operation
2.1. Reactor's Setup and Operation An up‐flow anaerobic sludge blanket (UASB) reactor was fed with synthetic wastewater. The reactor had an effective working volume of 3.3 L. The reaction temperature was maintained at 30°C ± 1°C using a circulating water bath in the outer wall. The reactor was operated continuously with a hydraulic retention time (HRT) of 12 h. The synthetic wastewater (the influent) was composed of NH 4 Cl (0.306 g/L) and NaNO 2 (0.520 g/L), establishing an NH 4 + ‐N to NO 2 − ‐N molar ratio of 1:1.32. The wastewater also contained KH 2 PO 4 (0.027 g/L), MgSO 4 •7H 2 O (0.533 g/L), CaCl 2 (0.136 g/L), NaHCO 3 (0.50 g/L) (Zhou and Yao 2005 ), and 1.25 mL/L of trace element Solutions I and II (Table S1 ) (Xu 2023 ). The influent pH was adjusted to 7.4 ± 0.2 using 1‐M HCl. Dissolved oxygen was removed from the influent and the reactor by purging with N 2 gas (99.999%). The reactor was enclosed in light‐impermeable black cloth to eliminate phototrophic activity. Seed anammox sludge (500 mL) was collected from the anaerobic zone of a full‐scale A 2 /O municipal wastewater treatment company (Lianyungang, China).

2.2. Bacterial Strains and Culture Conditions
2.2. Bacterial Strains and Culture Conditions S. oneidensis MR‐1 was usually cultured in Luria‐Bertani broth or plates. Both S. oneidensis MR‐1 and Δ nrfA S. oneidensis MR‐1 were provided by Professor Qiu, Institute of Hydrobiology, Chinese Academy of Sciences.

2.3. Cultivation of S. oneidensis MR‐1 and ΔnrfA S. oneidensis MR‐1 (a Mutant Strain Lacking nrfA Gene)
2.3. Cultivation of S. oneidensis MR‐1 and ΔnrfA S. oneidensis MR‐1 (a Mutant Strain Lacking nrfA Gene) S. oneidensis MR‐1 cultures were mixed with an equal volume of sterile 40% (v/v) glycerol stock to a final glycerol concentration of 20% (v/v) and preserved at −80°C. All Shewanella cultures were grown at 30°C. S. oneidensis MR‐1 was inoculated onto a LB plate with a sterile loop under aseptic conditions. A single colony from a 16‐ to 48‐h LB plate was inoculated in 10‐mL sterile LB broth. The broth culture was incubated with shaking at 120 rpm overnight (about 24 h) until reaching a turbid, cloudy state. This culture was then subcultured (1:100) into fresh 10‐mL LB broth and incubated overnight. For large‐scale preparation, it was subcultured (1:100) into fresh 100‐mL LB broth and incubated 24 h. This culture was finally subcultured (1:100) into fresh into 300‐mL LB broth for subsequent use. Δ nrfA S. oneidensis MR‐1 cultures were mixed with an equal volume of sterile 40% (v/v) glycerol stock to a final glycerol concentration of 20% (v/v) and preserved at −80°C (Figure S4 ). Δ nrfA S. oneidensis MR‐1 was inoculated onto a LB plate with a sterile loop under aseptic conditions. A single colony from a 16‐ to 48‐h LB plate was inoculated in 5‐mL sterile LB broth. The broth culture was incubated at 30°C with shaking at 200 rpm overnight (about 24 h) until reaching a turbid, cloudy state. The culture was finally subcultured (1:100) into fresh into 300‐mL LB broth for subsequent use.

2.5. Coupling Assays
2.5. Coupling Assays The mixed medium was composed of NH 4 Cl (0.306 g/L) and NaNO 3 (0.6419 g/L). The mixed medium also contained CaCl 2 (0.136 g/L), NaCl (0.46 g/L), NaHCO 3 (0.50 g/L), K 2 HPO 4 (0.225 g/L), KH 2 PO 4 (0.252 g/L), MgSO 4 •7H 2 O (0.65 g/L) (Zhou and Yao 2005 ), 25‐mM sodium lactate (electron donor), 1.25 mL/L of trace element Solutions I and II (Table S1 ) (Xu 2023 ), and 10 mL/L of Wolfe's trace element solution (Table S2 ) (Han 2018 ). The mixed medium pH was adjusted to 7.4 ± 0.2 using 1‐M HCl and 1‐M NaOH. The mixed medium, anammox sludge (UASB), and microbial culture (OD 600 ≈0.6) were separately dispensed into 500 mL reagent bottles. N 2 gas (99.999%) was purged for 10 min to remove dissolved oxygen from the reagent bottles. Samples were collected every 8 h, totaling 9–11 sampling events. The collected water samples were filtered through 0.45‐μm microporous membrane filters before analysis, with measured parameters including NH 4 + ‐N, NO 2 − ‐N, NO 3 − ‐N, COD, and TN. 2.8. Metagenomic Sequencing
2.8. Metagenomic Sequencing Approximately 30 mL of the sludge–water mixture from the final time point was stored at −80°C for 3 days, followed by freeze‐drying for 48 h using a freeze dryer (CTFD‐10S, Creatrust). The samples were submitted to Beijing Scale Biotechnology Co. Ltd. for sequencing. The sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus sequencing system. Genomic DNA (1 μg) was extracted from the sample and was randomly fragmented into segments of approximately 350 bp using a Covaris ultrasonic disruptor to construct the library. The entire library preparation was completed through a series of steps including end repair, addition of A‐tails, ligation of sequencing adapters, purification, and PCR amplification. After the library passes the quality check, different libraries were pooled according to the requirements for effective concentration and target on‐machine data volume and then subjected to PE150 sequencing. Raw data obtained from the Illumina sequencing platform were preprocessed using fastp to obtain clean data for subsequent analysis. The clean data were assembled and analyzed using MEGAHIT software, followed by breaking the assembled scaffolds at the N‐junction to obtain scaftigs without N (Qin et al. 2010 ; Li et al. 2015 ). ORF prediction was performed on the scaftigs (≥ 500 bp) of each sample using MetaGeneMark, and the predicted results with a length of less than 100 nt were filtered out (Karlsson et al. 2012 ; Mende et al. 2012 ; Li et al. 2014 ; Oh et al. 2014 ; Qin et al. 2014 , Qin et al. 2010 ; Zhu et al. 2010 ; Nielsen et al. 2014 ; Zeller et al. 2014 ; Sunagawa et al. 2015 ). For the ORF prediction results, CD‐HIT software was used to eliminate redundancy and obtain a nonredundant initial gene catalogue (the nucleic acid sequences encoded by successive nonredundant genes are called genes) (Li and Godzik 2006 ; Fu et al. 2012 ; Zeller et al. 2014 ). Bowtie2 was employed to align the clean data of each sample to the initial gene catalogue, and the number of reads of each gene on each sample was calculated. Genes with mapped reads ≤ 2 in each sample were filtered out to obtain the final gene catalogue (unigenes) for subsequent analyses (Zeller et al. 2014 ). Based on the number of reads aligned and gene length, the abundance information of each gene in each sample was calculated (Zeller et al. 2014 ). Using the abundance information of each gene in the gene catalogue across all samples, basic information statistics, core‐pan gene analysis, correlation analysis between samples, and Venn diagram analysis of gene number were performed. Unigenes were aligned against Micro_NR using DIAMOND software (Buchfink et al. 2015 ). Because each sequence may have multiple alignment results, the LCA (Lowest Common Ancestor) algorithm was adopted to determine the species annotation information of the sequence (Huson et al. 2011 ).

3.2.2. Enhanced Performance and Optimization With the ΔnrfA Mutant
3.2.2. Enhanced Performance and Optimization With the ΔnrfA Mutant The falling trend of NH 4 + ‐N concentration across all groups was observed (Figure 2a ), especially ΔCK2 and ΔB1 groups with 100% NH 4 + ‐N removal efficiency (Figure 2b ). However, ΔB1 exhibited significantly faster removal kinetics, reaching 97% removal efficiency within 16.5 h, compared to only 75.8% for ΔCK2 at the same time point. As the proportion of S. oneidensis MR‐1 increased, a progressive accumulation of NO 2 − ‐N was found (Figure 2e ), peaking at 52.4 mg/L. This indicated that NO 3 − ‐N was primarily reduced to NO 2 − ‐N rather than being fully denitrified. Concurrently, the gradual increase in final NH 4 + ‐N concentrations suggested a decline in the anammox contribution, with the generated NO 2 − ‐N being partially diverted to denitrification instead of anammox. 3.3. Evolution of the Microbial Community Structure
3.3. Evolution of the Microbial Community Structure The addition of anammox sludge resulted in the emergence of Planctomycetota , which accounted for 15%~21% of the community and represented the primary taxonomic group of anammox bacteria (Figure 3 ). Concurrently, Chloroflexota appeared in association with Planctomycetota , comprising 10%~13% of the microbial community. In anammox reactors, Chloroflexota bacteria coexisted with anammox bacteria and degraded cellular compounds derived from anammox bacterial metabolites and necromass (Kindaichi et al. 2012 ). Most denitrifying bacteria belonged to Pseudomonadota (from which Pseudomonadota and Campylobacterota have been separated), Bacillota (formerly Firmicutes), and Bacteroidota (Miao et al. 2015 ; Oren and Garrity 2021 ). Pseudomonadota play critical roles in N and phosphorus removal, as well as in organic compound degradation (Hao 2022 ). Bacillota primarily consist of the classes Clostridia and Bacilli , both of which are common taxonomic groups of DNRA bacteria (Hao 2022 ). The strain ΔnrfA S. oneidensis MR‐1 added to the ΔB groups is a DNRA bacterium belonging to Pseudomonadota . In the ΔCK1 group, Pseudomonadota accounted for up to 70% of the microbial community, with the primary function for denitrification based on N metabolism gene analysis. In ΔCK2, the introduction of anammox sludge led to Planctomycetota occupying a substantial ecological niche, accompanied by a decrease in Pseudomonadota and an increase in Bacteroidota . Following the addition of ΔnrfA S. oneidensis MR‐1 in the ΔB groups, the abundance of Pseudomonadota decreased significantly compared to the ΔCK groups, whereas the proportion of Planctomycetota increased markedly. 3.4. Mechanisms of Coupled Carbon and Nitrogen Metabolism
3.4. Mechanisms of Coupled Carbon and Nitrogen Metabolism As shown in Figure 6 , the dominant functional genes were NO 2 − ‐N reductase genes ( nirB and nirD ) and the periplasmic NO 2 − ‐N reductase gene ( nrfA ) in the DNRA pathway, which were NADH‐dependent (Zhang and Yu 2020 ), along with elevated abundances of the nitric oxide reductase gene ( norB ) and nitrous oxide reductase gene ( nosZ ) in the denitrification pathway as well as the hydrazine synthase gene ( hzs ) in the anammox pathway. With the exception of ΔCK1, the membrane‐bound NO 3 − ‐N reductase genes ( narG and narH ) showed the highest relative abundances among all N metabolism genes in all groups, especially the ΔB groups. In contrast, ΔCK1 displayed the highest relative abundances of norB , norC , and nosZ among all groups, whereas the hzs and hydrazine dehydrogenase ( hdh ) genes were nearly undetectable. These findings may imply that denitrification could be the dominant N transformation pathway in ΔCK1 during the later operational phase. The relative abundances of hzs and hdh genes in ΔCK2 were higher than those in ΔCK1, which can presumably be attributed to the introduction of anammox sludge. Furthermore, the ΔB groups showed even greater abundances of hzs and hdh compared to ΔCK2, potentially indicating that the addition of ΔnrfA S. oneidensis MR‐1 may further enhance anammox activity. The sole existing nirB and nirD genes and lower nrfA and nrfH genes abundance were observed in ΔCK1, compared to the other groups. The ΔB groups also had slightly higher abundances of nirB and nirD than ΔCK2. Notably, the ammonia removal efficiency tended to decrease with the increased inoculation ratio of ΔnrfA S. oneidensis MR‐1, as an excessively high proportion of this strain may alter the microbial community structure, occupy the ecological niche of anammox bacteria, and potentially weaken their ammonia removal capacity. Among the top 10 most abundant metabolic pathways, the TCA cycle, the reductive TCA cycle, and NADH‐related reactions appear to be dominant (Figure 7 ). Meanwhile, the abundance of key genes indicates that genes associated with the TCA cycle and the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) are likely significantly more abundant than those involved in glycolysis. The ΔCK2 group displays the highest abundance of the TCA cycle and reductive TCA cycle pathways among all groups, followed by the ΔB4 group. Correspondingly, as shown in Figure 8 , the key TCA cycle genes ACO (encoding aconitase) and IDH1 (encoding isocitrate dehydrogenase) also tend to show higher abundance in these groups. In contrast, the ΔCK1 group exhibits not only lower abundance of the TCA cycle and reductive TCA cycle pathways but also lower ACO and IDH1 gene abundance relative to other groups, which may suggest inefficient TCA cycle operation in this group. Furthermore, with the exception of the ΔCK1 group, all other treatments maintain high abundance of NADH‐related pathways and pathways associated with secondary carbon fixation. Metagenomic profiling of nitrogen and central carbon metabolism may partially reveal the underlying mechanisms driving the superior performance of the ΔnrfA mutant coupled system. In the nitrogen metabolic network, membrane‐bound nitrate reductase genes ( narG , narH ) exhibited the highest relative abundances across all systems, particularly in the ΔB groups. Notably, although the control group (ΔCK1) was dominated by complete denitrification genes ( norB , norC , nosZ ), the addition of the ΔnrfA mutant (ΔB groups) appears to significantly enrich key anammox functional genes, such as hydrazine synthase ( hzs ) and hydrazine dehydrogenase ( hdh ). Crucially, the ΔnrfA mutant may effectively block the terminal reduction of nitrite to ammonium via the Nap/Nrf pathway. This targeted genetic blockade could facilitate the accumulation of NO 2 − ‐N, potentially diverting the nitrogen flux towards the enriched anammox bacteria. To sustain these robust nitrogen conversion pathways, the microbial community may adopt divergent central carbon metabolic strategies to allocate electron equivalents. The synergistic systems (ΔCK2 and ΔB4) seem to fundamentally rely on a highly efficient tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), rather than canonical glycolysis, to drive energy metabolism. The added lactate acts simultaneously as an organic carbon source and a critical electron donor. Intracellularly, lactate oxidation fuels the TCA cycle (evidenced by higher abundances of ACO and IDH1 genes), generating abundant NADH. This NADH is then shuttled through the electron transport chain‐comprising NADH dehydrogenase, the quinone pool, the bc 1 complex, and cytochrome c‐to the respective denitrifying reductases (Jiang et al. 2020 ). In stark contrast, the ΔCK1 group exhibited a weakened TCA flux and upregulated glycolytic genes (e.g., GAPDH), indicating a metabolic strategy that is presumably less efficient and may struggle to support multipathway conversions. The compatibility between the high‐efficiency TCA strategy in the ΔB groups and the strategic genetic absence of nrfA may ensure that electrons are precisely funneled towards NO 2 − ‐N generation rather than NH 4 + ‐N production, thereby potentially establishing an optimal metabolic environment for anammox.

4. Conclusion
4. Conclusion This study investigates a novel N removal system by coupling anammox bacteria with Shewanella oneidensis MR‐1, focusing on both the wild‐type strain and a mutant lacking the nrfA gene ( ΔnrfA ). The research aimed to enhance N removal efficiency, particularly from wastewater containing NO 3 − ‐N. The addition of S. oneidensis MR‐1 generally promoted DNRA, supplying NO 2 − ‐N for anammox. However, excessive amounts led to competition, shifting the dominant microbial process towards DNRA and denitrification, thereby reducing the anammox contribution. In contrast, the system with the ΔnrfA mutant (unable to perform DNRA) showed superior performance. Specifically, the ΔB1 group, with a 5:1 ratio of anammox sludge to ΔnrfA S. oneidensis MR‐1, achieved the highest total N removal efficiency (97%) with rapid kinetics. Metagenomic analysis revealed that this configuration enriched anammox bacteria (Candidatus Brocadia ) and enhanced the abundance of key anammox genes ( hzs and hdh ), whereas the mutant strain can cause the accumulation of NO 2 − ‐N by blocking the DNRA pathway. The study concludes that strategically coupling anammox with electron‐transfer‐capable bacteria like the ΔnrfA mutant, which directs electron flow towards NO 2 − ‐N production for anammox rather than NH 4 + ‐N generation, is a promising strategy for developing efficient and stable anaerobic N removal processes.

5. Perspective
5. Perspective This study develops a novel pathway for nitrite accumulation in anammox processes and establishes a coupled system. Relevant process parameters and underlying mechanisms will be further optimized and supplemented in future research. Future long‐term continuous reactor trials are required to evaluate the stable performance of the coupled system under practical running conditions. Subsequent tests should quantify gaseous nitrogen metabolites alongside liquid nitrogen pollutants to fully partition the contributions of anammox, denitrification, and DNRA. Time‐series metagenomic sampling should be implemented to track dynamic shifts in microbial communities and functional genes over the whole reaction cycle. Moreover, multiomics detection including RT‐qPCR and metatranscriptomics, as well as key enzyme activity assays, will be adopted to validate gene expression and catalytic functions, providing direct molecular evidence to support the speculated regulatory mechanisms of the ΔnrfA mutant strain. Finally, future research could carry out isotope labeling tests to quantitatively differentiate the contribution of anammox, denitrification, and DNRA, which will provide more persuasive quantitative evidence for relevant mechanisms.
