BACKGROUND: Lignocellulosic biomass represents an ideal feedstock for 
biochemical production as a sustainable alternative to fossil resources. 
However, high concentrations of inhibitors inevitably generated during 
pretreatment-particularly phenolic compounds-severely impede microbial growth 
and metabolism, representing a major bottleneck in biorefining. In contrast to 
expensive physicochemical detoxification or single-strain strategies burdened by 
heavy metabolic loads, microbial co-culture systems employing division of labor 
offer a highly efficient and cost-effective novel pathway.
RESULTS: This study developed a novel dual Bacillus coagulans co-culture system 
consisting of a phenolic acid decarboxylase (PAD)-overexpressing engineered 
strain, DSM1-25280, and a high-yield L-lactic acid (LA) producer, CC17B-1, 
capable of directly utilizing highly toxic lignocellulosic hydrolysates for 
high-titer LA production. Notably, overexpression of PAD in the engineered 
strain DSM1-25280 not only enhanced tolerance to phenolic acids but also 
significantly accelerated vanillin degradation. By establishing a sequential 
inoculation strategy and optimizing both inoculation ratios and intervals, the 
dual-strain system achieved a LA titer of 124.72 g/L from undetoxified corncob 
hydrolysates. Multidimensional association analysis suggested a strongly 
supported model of niche succession process within the system, transitioning 
from biodetoxification-mediated commensalism to competitive exclusion. 
Furthermore, we constructed an enhanced three-strain co-culture system by 
incorporating a sugar-metabolism-deficient engineered Pseudomonas putida KT2440 
ZL as a heterologous aromatic scavenger. This system further elevated the LA 
titer to 146.00 g/L with a yield of 98.9%, ranking among the highest levels 
reported to date for LA production from undetoxified hydrolysates.
CONCLUSIONS: The constructed microbial co-culture system significantly enhances 
LA fermentation performance using undetoxified lignocellulosic hydrolysates, 
bypassing the need for physicochemical detoxification. Demonstrating exceptional 
modularity and robustness, this sequential co-culture strategy effectively 
overcomes the toxicity bottlenecks inherent in traditional bioconversion 
processes. Ultimately, this work not only validates the superiority of division 
of labor in valorizing complex feedstocks but also provides a novel approach for 
the efficient biomanufacturing of high-value chemicals from low-grade biomass.

Background Lignocellulosic biomass represents an ideal feedstock for biochemical production as a sustainable alternative to fossil resources. However, high concentrations of inhibitors inevitably generated during pretreatment—particularly phenolic compounds—severely impede microbial growth and metabolism, representing a major bottleneck in biorefining. In contrast to expensive physicochemical detoxification or single-strain strategies burdened by heavy metabolic loads, microbial co-culture systems employing division of labor offer a highly efficient and cost-effective novel pathway. Results This study developed a novel dual Bacillus coagulans co-culture system consisting of a phenolic acid decarboxylase (PAD)-overexpressing engineered strain, DSM1-25280, and a high-yield L-lactic acid (LA) producer, CC17B-1, capable of directly utilizing highly toxic lignocellulosic hydrolysates for high-titer LA production. Notably, overexpression of PAD in the engineered strain DSM1-25280 not only enhanced tolerance to phenolic acids but also significantly accelerated vanillin degradation. By establishing a sequential inoculation strategy and optimizing both inoculation ratios and intervals, the dual-strain system achieved a LA titer of 124.72 g/L from undetoxified corncob hydrolysates. Multidimensional association analysis suggested a strongly supported model of niche succession process within the system, transitioning from biodetoxification-mediated commensalism to competitive exclusion. Furthermore, we constructed an enhanced three-strain co-culture system by incorporating a sugar-metabolism-deficient engineered Pseudomonas putida KT2440 ZL as a heterologous aromatic scavenger. This system further elevated the LA titer to 146.00 g/L with a yield of 98.9%, ranking among the highest levels reported to date for LA production from undetoxified hydrolysates. Conclusions The constructed microbial co-culture system significantly enhances LA fermentation performance using undetoxified lignocellulosic hydrolysates, bypassing the need for physicochemical detoxification. Ultimately, this work not only validates the superiority of division of labor in valorizing complex feedstocks but also provides a novel approach for the efficient biomanufacturing of high-value chemicals from low-grade biomass. In particular, global market demand for LA is surging, driven by its critical role as the precursor for synthesizing the biodegradable plastic polylactic acid (PLA) [ 1 ]. Currently, microbial fermentation dominates industrial LA production, accounting for approximately 90% of the global output [ 2 ]. However, high feedstock costs and competition with food crops remain the core challenges facing this process. Consequently, utilizing abundant and renewable lignocellulosic biomass as an alternative to traditional food-based feedstocks has emerged as an inevitable trend [ 3 ]. Despite this promising prospect, the inherent recalcitrance of lignocellulose necessitates harsh pretreatment to release fermentable sugars, a process that inevitably generates various toxic inhibitors, including weak acids, furan derivatives (e.g., furfural and 5-hydroxymethylfurfural [HMF]), and more cytotoxic phenolic compounds [ 4 ]. These diverse and complex compounds exert potent inhibitory effects on microorganisms. Phenolic compounds, in particular, impose severe toxicity by adsorbing onto and disrupting the phospholipid bilayer of cell membranes, leading to a loss of cellular integrity, which severely inhibits microbial growth and acid-producing capacity, thereby escalating fermentation costs [ 5 ]. For instance, studies have demonstrated that concentrations of p-coumaric acid and ferulic acid as low as 1 g/L can significantly inhibit microbial growth [ 6 ]. Consequently, the efficient removal of these inhibitors, without sacrificing fermentable sugars, represents a critical bottleneck to achieving efficient LA biorefining from lignocellulosic feedstocks. To mitigate the detrimental effects of inhibitors, various detoxification strategies have been developed to convert these substances into inactive compounds or reduce their concentrations. However, traditional physicochemical methods—such as sulfite addition, alkali neutralization, activated carbon treatment, and lignin blockers—while effective, are often accompanied by drawbacks such as high costs, lack of specificity, the introduction of new stress-inducing compounds, and substantial loss of fermentable sugars [ 7 , 8 ]. In contrast, biological detoxification provides a milder, highly specific, and environmentally friendly alternative by utilizing microorganisms to tolerate or degrade inhibitors in situ [ 9 ]. However, natural microbial strains often struggle to simultaneously achieve high stress tolerance and high productivity [ 10 ]. In recent years, researchers have devoted efforts to metabolically engineering single chassis cells capable of utilizing lignocellulosic biomass for chemical production while tolerating inhibitors [ 11 ]. For instance, Bohyun et al. enhanced the tolerance of Saccharomyces cerevisiae by knocking out the PHO13 and ALD6 genes, achieving an LA yield of 27.7 g/L in synthetic lignocellulosic hydrolysate, representing a 1.3-fold increase over the parental strain [ 12 ]. Similarly, Qiu et al. overexpressed a short-chain dehydrogenase in Pediococcus acidilactici to accelerate vanillin degradation, resulting in a 13.9% increase in LA yield [ 13 ]. However, integrating multiple functionalities—such as broad-spectrum detoxification, high yield, and maintenance of cell growth—into a single strain often imposes a severe metabolic burden on host cells [ 14 ]. This burden forces microorganisms to compromise the allocation of limited cellular resources, resulting in a metabolic trade-off between growth robustness and production efficiency [ 15 ]. When the host reaches the threshold of severe metabolic inhibition, even minor perturbations in growth or stress can trigger a systemic metabolic collapse and a drastic reduction in yield [ 16 ]. Consequently, microbial co-culture strategies provide a viable pathway to overcome this metabolic bottleneck. Artificial microbial consortia alleviate the metabolic burden on individual strains by distributing complex metabolic tasks among distinct strains through a division of labor [ 17 ]. Consequently, constructing microbial consortia capable of simultaneous detoxification and production holds immense promise for lignocellulosic biorefining. For example, Zhu et al. developed a co-culture system comprising xylose-utilizing and inhibitor-tolerant S. cerevisiae strains, which produced 58.8 g/L of ethanol from undetoxified, pretreated corn stover, representing increases of 21.2% and 41.0% compared to the respective monocultures [ 18 ]. Similarly, Zou et al. constructed a co-culture of the engineered Pseudomonas putida KT2440 responsible for detoxification and the lactate producer Bacillus coagulans NL01, capable of producing 35.8 g/L of LA directly from undetoxified corn stover hydrolysate [ 19 ]. These studies further substantiate the potential of microbial co-culture strategies for processing complex lignocellulosic feedstocks. However, despite these encouraging advances, simultaneously achieving biological detoxification and high-efficiency production within a single system remains a formidable challenge. Current research is primarily limited to the conversion of low-toxicity hydrolysates and has yet to overcome the bottlenecks in the utilization of feedstocks characterized by high sugar concentrations and elevated inhibitor contents [ 20 ]. Furthermore, current artificial consortia predominantly focus on "cross-kingdom" combinations (e.g., fungi-bacteria). Due to significant physiological differences between species—such as mismatches in pH, temperature, and oxygen requirements—complex process controls, including membrane-aerated reactors [ 21 ], 3D printing [ 22 ], and hydrogel entrapment [ 23 ], are often required to create artificial niches that maintain coexistence. This inevitably increases the complexity and cost of industrial applications. Meanwhile, understanding the underlying mechanisms within co-culture systems is crucial for optimizing culture conditions, enhancing target product yields, and designing stable systems; however, research on these mechanisms remains relatively limited [ 24 ]. To address these challenges, this study developed a novel dual B. coagulans co-culture system designed to achieve efficient LA conversion from lignocellulosic hydrolysates containing high concentrations of inhibitors. The system comprises the engineered strain B. coagulans DSM1-25280, which overexpresses phenolic acid decarboxylase (PAD), and the high-LA-yielding strain B. coagulans CC17B-1 [ 25 ]. As conspecific strains, they share identical fermentation conditions and possess superior compatibility, thus eliminating the need for phased medium treatment. Simultaneously, the inhibitor degradation profile and the mechanisms underlying strain interactions during this process were elucidated. To further enhance system robustness in highly toxic environments, the engineered P. putida KT2440 ZL [ 19 ]—characterized by broad-spectrum aromatic degradation capabilities and deficient sugar metabolism—was introduced to construct a fortified three-strain co-culture system. This study presents a novel approach for the efficient utilization of high-toxicity biomass feedstocks. Materials and methods Materials and reagents Undetoxified dilute acid corncob hydrolysate concentrate and corn steep liquor powder (CSLP) were kindly provided by a biomass factory in Shanghai, China. Yeast extract (YE) was purchased from Oxoid (Hampshire, UK), and chloramphenicol was obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. All other analytical-grade chemicals used in this study were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, USA). Construction of the B. coagulans DSM1 overexpression system To develop an efficient expression system suitable for B. coagulans DSM1, three promoters were evaluated: P43 (from Bacillus subtilis), PldhL (from Lactococcus lactis), and PlutP (from B. coagulans). Green fluorescent protein (GFP) was employed as the reporter to assess promoter strength. The three promoters were individually ligated with the gfp gene into the pNw33n vector using the Hind III/Bgl II and Bgl II/EcoR I restriction sites, respectively, to construct the recombinant plasmids pNw33n-P43-gfp, pNw33n-PldhL-gfp, and pNw33n-PlutP-gfp (Additional file 1 : Table S1). The constructed plasmids were electroporated into competent B. coagulans DSM1 cells. After 24 to 48 h of incubation, the plates were placed in a dark box to observe colony fluorescence intensity under 488 nm excitation light once single colonies had developed. Plasmid and strain constructions Genomic DNA of B. coagulans CC17A [ 26 ] was extracted using a bacterial genomic DNA extraction kit (Takara, Japan). Using this genome as the template, the target gene BCOA_RS25280 was amplified by PCR with corresponding primers (Additional file 1 : Table S2). After gel purification and recovery, the target gene fragment was ligated into the pNw33n-P43 vector to construct the recombinant plasmid pNw33n-P43-RS25280. Subsequently, the recombinant plasmid was electroporated into B. coagulans DSM1 cells. The cells were then plated on BC medium supplemented with 7 μg/mL chloramphenicol and incubated at 45 °C for 24–36 h. Single colonies were picked and verified by PCR, and the resulting engineered strain was designated B. coagulans DSM1-25280. Strains and media The strains used in this study, including B. coagulans CC17A [ 26 ], B. coagulans DSM1, B. coagulans CC17B-1 [ 25 ], and P. putida KT2440 ZL [ 19 ], were all preserved at -80 °C in our laboratory. For solid medium, 16 g/L agar was added before sterilization, and 1 g/L MgCl2 was added after sterilization. B. coagulans seed medium (g/L): glucose 20, YE 1, CSLP 2.5, NH4Cl 1, MgSO4 0.2, pH 7.2. For solid medium, 16 g/L agar was added before sterilization. When culturing DSM1-25280, 15 μg/mL chloramphenicol was added prior to use. P. putida seed medium (LB) (g/L): tryptone 10, YE 5, NaCl 10. For solid medium, 16 g/L agar was added before sterilization. Hydrolysate fermentation medium (g/L): YE 7.3, CSLP 18.25, NH4Cl 7.3, MgSO4 0.2, KH2PO4 0.22, 100% (v/v) corncob hydrolysate, pH 7.2. Evaluation of stress tolerance of recombinant B. coagulans The recombinant strain B. coagulans DSM1-25280 and the empty vector control strain DSM1-pNw33n-P43 were activated in seed medium at 50 °C and 150 rpm for 12 h. Subsequently, the strains were inoculated with a 20% (v/v) inoculum into seed medium supplemented individually with 50 mg/L of nine inhibitors (4-hydroxybenzoic acid, syringic acid, vanillic acid, 4-hydroxybenzaldehyde, vanillin, p-coumaric acid, syringaldehyde, ferulic acid, and caffeic acid). The cultures were incubated at 50 °C and 150 rpm, and samples were collected periodically for HPLC analysis. LA fermentation experiments For the monoculture fermentation of B. coagulans DSM1-25280 (strain A) and B. coagulans CC17B-1 (strain B), the strains were activated in seed medium at 50 °C and 150 rpm for 12 h. Cells were collected by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 3 min and washed twice with 0.85% (w/v) NaCl solution. The strains were inoculated at 4 g/L (wet weight) into 150 mL shake flasks containing 50 mL of hydrolysate fermentation medium, supplemented with 0.5 g CaCO3/g sugar to regulate the pH during fermentation. Fermentation was conducted under anaerobic conditions at 50 °C and 150 rpm. For the co-culture fermentation of strain A and strain B, ten schemes were designed based on different inoculation sequences, ratios, and timings (Table  1 ). All other experimental conditions were the same as in monoculture fermentation.

Microbial population dynamics analysis in co-culture system Viable cell counts (colony-forming units, CFU) at various fermentation time points in the optimal co-culture scheme were determined using a plate counting method based on differential antibiotic resistance. Samples were collected periodically and serially diluted with 0.85% (w/v) NaCl solution to appropriate concentrations. A 200 μL aliquot of each dilution was spread onto two types of solid media: (1) antibiotic-free seed medium plates to enumerate the total number of strain A and strain B; and (2) seed medium plates supplemented with 15 μg/mL chloramphenicol to enumerate only the resistant strain A. Plates were incubated at 50 °C for 24 h. The population of strain B was calculated based on the difference between the two counts. Simultaneously, a negative control was established by plating activated strain B onto plates containing 15 μg/mL chloramphenicol to confirm its inability to grow. Analytical methods and calculations Fermentation samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter prior to analysis using an Agilent 1260 HPLC system. The concentrations of glucose, xylose, furfuraldehydes, acetic acid, LA, and various phenolic inhibitors were determined as previously reported [ 25 ]. Total phenolic content was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method [ 27 ]. Sugar utilization rate (%) was defined as the ratio of the consumed total sugar concentration to the initial total sugar concentration. LA yield (%) was defined as the ratio of the produced LA concentration to the initial total sugar concentration. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Results and discussion Construction and inhibitor tolerance analysis of engineered B. coagulans DSM1—25,280 Promoter screening and establishment of an efficient overexpression system B. coagulans, as an acid-tolerant and thermophilic Gram-positive industrial strain, has garnered significant attention for its ability to produce optically pure L-LA at high temperatures [ 28 ]. Although this strain shows some potential for tolerance to lignocellulosic inhibitors, its inherent stress tolerance often falls short of meeting the requirements of industrial fermentation in high-concentration complex hydrolysate environments [ 29 ]. Furthermore, compared to well-established model organisms such as Escherichia coli [ 30 ], S. cerevisiae [ 31 ], and P. putida [ 32 ], genetic manipulation tools for B. coagulans remain relatively limited. To construct an engineered B. coagulans strain with efficient detoxification capabilities, establishing a robust gene expression system is a critical prerequisite. In this study, three constitutive promoters were screened: P43 (from B. subtilis), PldhL (from L. lactis), and PlutP (from B. coagulans DSM1). Recombinant plasmids expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) were constructed and electroporated into DSM1 to qualitatively evaluate the transcriptional activity of each promoter. As shown in Fig.  1 A, the recombinant strain driven by the P43 promoter (DSM1-pNw33n-P43-gfp) exhibited the strongest fluorescence intensity. This result indicates that the P43 promoter derived from B. subtilis possesses optimal transcriptional activity in B. coagulans; therefore, it was selected for the efficient expression of key detoxification enzyme genes in subsequent experiments. C Inhibitor degradation substrate spectrum of the recombinant strain B. coagulans DSM1-25280 Reinforcement of specific detoxification functions by overexpression of PAD Previous transcriptomic analysis revealed that the gene BCOA_RS25280, encoding a putative PAD, was significantly upregulated by 13.82-fold at the transcriptional level under hydrolysate stress, suggesting a critical role in the stress tolerance process [ 26 ]. To verify its function and enhance stress tolerance in the strain, the BCOA_RS25280 gene was introduced into B. coagulans DSM1 to construct the recombinant engineered strain B. coagulans DSM1-pNw33n-P43-RS25280, hereafter referred to as DSM1-25280. To evaluate the stress tolerance potential of the recombinant strain, nine typical inhibitors commonly found in lignocellulosic hydrolysates were selected. The degradation performance of the starting strain (harboring the empty vector) and the recombinant strain was compared in media containing individual inhibitors, each at an initial concentration of 50 mg/L. As shown in Fig.  1 B, although the starting strain DSM1 exhibited some conversion ability toward certain aldehyde inhibitors, its degradation efficiency for highly toxic phenolic acids—particularly ferulic acid—was extremely low. This limited metabolic capability is a major factor restricting its survival in complex hydrolysate environments. In contrast, the PAD-overexpressing strain DSM1-25280 demonstrated significantly enhanced degradation efficacy against key phenolic acid inhibitors, successfully overcoming the metabolic limitations of the starting strain (Fig.  1 C). Specifically, for ferulic acid, which DSM1 struggles to degrade, the conversion rate exceeded 85.7% within 24 h, leaving only 9.76 mg/L remaining. Simultaneously, the concentrations of p-coumaric acid and caffeic acid decreased to 4.65 mg/L and 6.01 mg/L, respectively. Notably, although PAD primarily targets phenolic acid substrates, the recombinant strain also exhibited a significantly faster degradation rate of vanillin compared to the starting strain, achieving complete degradation within 12 h. Instead of a direct enzymatic reduction, this accelerated vanillin degradation is likely an indirect pleiotropic benefit of PAD overexpression. The precise physiological mechanism underlying this accelerated degradation remains to be further elucidated [ 33 ]. In summary, by overexpressing PAD, the engineered strain DSM1-25280 not only overcame the rate-limiting step of phenolic acid metabolism but also reinforced its basal tolerance and conversion efficiency for aldehyde inhibitors through potential mechanisms, laying a foundation for its core detoxification role in the subsequent co-culture system. Construction of a dual B. coagulans co-culture system for LA fermentation of undetoxified corncob hydrolysate To realize the economic feasibility of lignocellulosic biorefineries, this study aimed to overcome the challenge of producing high-concentration L-LA using highly toxic lignocellulosic hydrolysates. As shown in Fig.  2 B, the undetoxified corncob hydrolysate concentrate obtained from the factory has a complex composition. It contains not only high concentrations of mixed sugars (glucose 125.48 g/L, xylose 22.15 g/L) but also significant amounts of highly toxic inhibitors, including acetic acid (5.18 g/L), furan derivatives (HMF 1.04 g/L, furfural 2.04 g/L), and up to 4.14 g/L of total phenols. Such high-sugar and high-toxicity characteristics pose a severe challenge to microbial fermentation, rendering it extremely difficult for a single strain to simultaneously balance high stress tolerance and high productivity. As shown in Fig.  2 A, although the previously constructed engineered strain B. coagulans DSM1-25280 (strain A) reinforced its detoxification capabilities, it possessed distinct metabolic defects: it was unable to utilize xylose and showed extremely low LA production efficiency, yielding only 13.20 g/L LA from 40 g/L pure glucose after 48 h of fermentation (data not shown). Meanwhile, B. coagulans CC17B-1 [ 25 ] (strain B), obtained from previous studies, possesses excellent broad-spectrum sugar utilization capabilities and acid production performance, along with a certain degree of tolerance and degradation ability toward inhibitors. Based on this, a novel co-culture system based on "intraspecific functional complementarity" was designed (Fig.  2 C), aiming to achieve efficient coupling of biological detoxification and LA fermentation through the division of labor between strains, thereby overcoming the bottlenecks associated with utilizing highly toxic hydrolysates. F Degradation of phenolic inhibitors by strain B (monoculture S2) within 72 h Decisive effect of inoculation sequence on fermentation performance To compare the performance of monoculture and various co-culture modes in undetoxified hydrolysates and to establish a feasible inoculation strategy, five experimental schemes were designed (S1–S5, Table  1 ). As shown in Fig.  2 D, LA production was extremely low (< 10 g/L) when strain B was cultured alone (S2) or inoculated as the pioneer strain (S5). However, strain B, like strain A, actually exhibited a certain degree of inhibitor degradation capability in the hydrolysates (Fig. 2 E and F). This phenomenon of "degradation without acid production" reveals the predicament faced by strain B. The high concentrations of inhibitors in the undetoxified hydrolysates forced the cells to divert all metabolic energy toward survival maintenance and damage repair rather than product synthesis. This excessive metabolic burden subjected the cells to severe metabolic inhibition, ultimately exceeding their physiological tolerance limit and resulting in irreversible cellular damage. Similarly, in S5, strain B had already lost viability before the addition of strain A; since the subsequently added strain A possessed weak acid production capabilities, the overall fermentation failed. Notably, the simultaneous inoculation strategy (S3) similarly accumulated only 6.09 g/L of LA. Only the sequential strategy (S4), involving the pre-inoculation of strain A, successfully initiated fermentation, with LA production surging to 81.75 g/L. This result confirms that environmental modification by strain A is a prerequisite for the survival and acid production of strain B. With simultaneous inoculation, strain A failed to reduce environmental toxicity to a safe threshold within a very short period to protect strain B, resulting in the loss of strain B viability at the early stage of fermentation. In contrast, in S4, strain A acted as a pioneer, effectively lowering environmental toxicity and paving the way for the colonization and metabolism of the subsequent primary producer, strain B. Such temporal cooperation based on metabolic division of labor is well reflected in both natural and artificial microbial communities; for instance, white-rot fungi often first degrade lignin to create favorable conditions for cellulose-degrading microorganisms [ 34 ]. Similarly, Schlembach et al. employed sequential inoculation by first utilizing Trichoderma reesei to hydrolyze substrates, followed by inoculation with Ustilago maydis to convert the released sugars into itaconic acid [ 35 ]. Furthermore, the metabolic compatibility of the intraspecific strains and consistent growth conditions in this study further enhanced system stability [ 36 ]. Optimization of inoculation ratio and timing for the co-culture system To balance biological detoxification efficiency with LA production performance and maximize substrate utilization, this study further systematically optimized inoculation ratios (S4, S6, and S7) and timing (S8–S10). First, the effect of different inoculation ratios on fermentation was investigated at a fixed inoculation interval of 24 h. When the inoculation ratios of strain A to strain B were 1:3, 2:2, and 3:1, the final LA concentrations were 105.11, 81.75, and 76.23 g/L, respectively (Fig. 3 A, B, and C). The results indicated that as the initial inoculation ratio of the primary producer, strain B, increased, both LA production and sugar utilization significantly improved. Since strain A consumed negligible substrate within 24 h of inoculation, this result was therefore not attributed to substrate competition between strains A and B. Instead, an excessively high proportion of strain A occupied the limited inoculation niche, resulting in insufficient presence of the high-yielding primary producer in the system to perform fermentation tasks. On the other hand, although strain A reduced environmental toxicity, residual inhibitors still posed a challenge to strain B. Microorganisms often exhibit positive density dependence, known as the Allee effect, when facing adverse environmental stress [ 37 ]. A higher inoculation density conferred stronger population stress tolerance to strain B. High-density cell populations can rapidly overcome residual inhibitory pressure and initiate metabolism by secreting more extracellular protective factors and quorum sensing signal molecules, or by diluting the amount of toxin entering each cell [ 38 ]. As demonstrated by Fiegna et al., the Allee effect exists in five different bacteria under acid stress, where high-density inoculation protects the bacterial population from stress [ 39 ]. Therefore, adopting a "low-A, high-B" strategy (1:3) ensured sufficient detoxification capacity (provided by strain A) while maximizing the system's population stress tolerance and acid production potential through high-density inoculation of strain B. Induced niche succession potentially driven by metabolic trade-offs: from commensalism to competitive exclusion Microbial interactions determine the structural stability and metabolic functions of artificial bacterial consortia, serving as a prerequisite for achieving efficient conversion in complex biological processes [ 36 ]. Comprehensively elucidating microbial interaction mechanisms within co-culture systems is crucial for the rational design and precise regulation of artificial microbial communities. Population dynamics serve as the most intuitive key representation reflecting community interactions [ 40 ]. Therefore, this study employed a differential plating method based on antibiotic resistance to track the population dynamic trajectories of two B. coagulans strains during the S9 fermentation process (Fig.  4 ) and proposed potential interaction models in conjunction with metabolite analysis. Fig. 4 Population dynamics of the dual B. coagulans co-culture system under the optimal sequential strategy. A Visualization of differential plating based on chloramphenicol resistance (Antibiotic-containing plates: strain A; Antibiotic-free plates: total bacteria). B Time–course dynamics of viable cell counts (CFU/mL) for strain A and strain B during fermentation During the initial stage of fermentation (0–12 h), the system was completely dominated by strain A, whose viable cell count rapidly increased to 1.75 × 104 CFU/mL. During this period, strain A played the role of a niche constructor [ 41 ], efficiently degrading highly toxic phenolic compounds in the hydrolysate. Total phenols decreased to 3.61 g/L (Additional file 1 : Fig. S1), completing the critical environmental modification. With the inoculation of strain B at 12 h, the system entered a transient phase of commensalism mediated by biological detoxification. During this stage (12–24 h), strain A continued to modify the environment, enabling strain B to colonize and slowly proliferate within the ameliorated niche. However, the metabolic activity of strain B was primarily directed toward adapting to residual inhibitors; consequently, the LA concentration was only 13.47 g/L at this point (Fig.  3 E). Notably, although strain B was a beneficiary, it also assisted strain A in co-detoxification, creating a favorable environment for its own subsequent fermentation. The synergistic effect of the two strains accelerated the degradation of inhibitors, with p-coumaric acid dropping sharply to 5.99 mg/L during this stage (Table  2 ). However, this coexistence did not last long. After 24 h, as key inhibitors in the environment were further degraded, the interaction between the strains shifted rapidly. This stage featured a significant population succession: strain A plummeted from 2.00 × 104 CFU/mL to 5.00 × 101 CFU/mL at 48 h and vanished completely after 72 h. Meanwhile, strain B exhibited exponential explosive growth, reaching 1.40 × 109 CFU/mL, rapidly replacing strain A to become the absolute dominant population and dominating the fermentation until the end. This was synchronously accompanied by a surge in LA production from 13.47 g/L to 86.91 g/L, with a production rate of 1.82 g/L/h. This phenomenon indicates suggests that once the basis for coexistence provided by environmental modification (toxicity reduction) disappeared, the relationship between strains rapidly shifted to intense exploitative competition for shared limiting resources [ 42 ]. We hypothesize that this succession phenomenon can be interpreted through the lens of trade-off between self-preservation and nutritional competence (SPANC trade-off) in bacteria [ 43 ]. As an engineered strain, strain A was forced to bear the high metabolic cost of synthesizing exogenous detoxification enzymes and maintaining stress resistance homeostasis, which biased its resource allocation toward self-preservation rather than nutritional competence [ 44 ]. Therefore, in a non-toxic, superior environment, the growth rate and substrate affinity of strain A were likely significantly lower than those of strain B. Conversely, as a high-yield LA producer, strain B had no extra metabolic burden and concentrated metabolic resources on growth rather than defense. Thus, in the early stage with high inhibitor concentrations, strain A survived by virtue of its stress resistance advantage, forming a transient commensal relationship with strain B. However, once the stress was relieved, strain B, relying on its more efficient sugar transport and metabolic capabilities, rapidly gained the upper hand in competition and eliminated strain A. Nevertheless, it is important to acknowledge that CFU-based tracking inherently reflects cellular culturability rather than absolute real-time metabolic viability. While the SPANC trade-off provides a compelling ecological framework for this population shift, alternative or concurrent physiological mechanisms cannot be ruled out. For instance, the rapid decline of strain A might also be exacerbated by severe product inhibition. As strain B accumulated high titers of LA, the intrinsic acid sensitivity of strain A might have been inferior to that of the primary producer, thereby accelerating its elimination from the system. In summary, this study presents a strongly supported model of induced niche succession that transitions from biological detoxification-mediated commensalism to competitive exclusion. This unique engineered succession mechanism not only theoretically demonstrates the feasibility of dynamically regulating community structure via environmental factors but also holds significant potential for future biorefinery applications. The timely elimination of strain A implies that the fermentation system can automatically remove the detoxification module that is no longer needed. This ensures that during the main acid production phase, valuable carbon sources and limiting nutrients are not diverted by the auxiliary strain but are exclusively utilized by the primary producer, strain B. This mechanism enhanced the overall operational efficiency and product conversion rate of the system to the greatest extent, achieving the maximization of the system’s atom economy, thus suggesting the effectiveness of this sequential strategy in resolving the conflict between cooperation and competition within artificial microbial consortia. Reinforcement of the three-strain co-culture system: introduction of P. putida as a heterologous aromatic scavenger Although the constructed dual-strain co-culture system demonstrated strong potential for treating undetoxified hydrolysates, it was restricted by the inherent substrate spectrum of B. coagulans, which hindered the complete degradation of complex lignin derivatives, resulting in the final LA yield and sugar utilization rate of the system remaining below 90%. To further enhance system robustness and break the bottleneck of acid production, an engineered P. putida KT2440 ZL (strain C) [ 19 ], featuring broad-spectrum aromatic degradation pathways and blocked sugar metabolism, was introduced. This resulted in the construction of a functionally highly complementary modular three-strain co-culture system (Fig.  5 A). Fig. 5 Construction and fermentation performance of the artificial three-strain co-culture system reinforced by P. putida. A Schematic diagram of the modular three-strain co-culture system construction. B Fermentation profile of the three-strain co-culture system in undetoxified hydrolysates Initially, strain C was inoculated for 12 h of aerobic cultivation. During this period, strain C acted as a heterologous aromatic "scavenger" for the system, aiming to leverage its powerful aromatic catabolic capability [ 45 ] to eliminate recalcitrant aromatic compounds that were difficult for strain A to fully convert, without consuming fermentation substrates. Subsequently, the system was switched to anaerobic conditions to initiate the established A-B relay program. As shown in Fig.  5 B, the introduction of strain C further shortened the fermentation lag phase, indicating more thorough elimination of environmental toxicity. Ultimately, the three-strain co-culture system accumulated 146.00 g/L of LA within 108 h, representing a 17.1% increase compared to the dual-strain system. Meanwhile, the LA yield and sugar utilization rate reached as high as 98.9% and 98.4%, respectively, with an LA production rate of 1.35 g/L/h. These results confirm that by introducing a functionally complementary "scavenger" module, the sequential co-culture strategy exhibits exceptional modularity and robustness, providing a highly promising biotechnological approach for efficient biomanufacturing using highly toxic lignocellulosic feedstocks. To evaluate the bioprocessing potential of this study, the results were briefly compared with representative recent studies on LA production from lignocellulosic biomass (Table  3 ). It should be explicitly noted that direct cross-study comparisons are inherently limited due to immense variations in feedstock types, pretreatment methods, sugar concentrations, and, most importantly, the specific inhibitor profiles of the hydrolysates. Most existing strategies typically yield LA concentrations below 80 g/L when treating undetoxified hydrolysate. In contrast, the co-culture system constructed in this study achieved significantly higher LA concentrations (124.72–146.00 g/L) without any physicochemical detoxification. To the best of our knowledge, this represents one of the highest reported LA production levels from undetoxified hydrolysates to date.
